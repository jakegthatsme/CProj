# II.07 — The Political-Economic-Disturbance Diagnostic: Currency, Supply Chain, Debt, Labor Displacement, Capital Concentration

## Part II — Disturbance-Regime Diagnostics and the Implementation Gap

*Fifth-pass designation: V. This document is the seventh document of Part II, specifying the political-economic-disturbance regime as one of the five regimes the constitutional architecture must absorb.*

The political-economic-disturbance regime engaged in this document is the regime acting on the political-economic substrate of the biocultural polity — the currency and monetary system, the supply chains through which the material economy is conducted, the debt architecture, the labor market, and the distribution of capital. The document specifies the regime through five analytical dimensions and the cross-regime couplings through which it acts, coordinating with Document II.03 on the contemporary American capture architecture at the cultural-political-economic intersection and with Document II.08 on the broader cross-regime interactions.

The sixth-pass project rests on the load-bearing triad articulated in Document III.01 and not re-argued here. The document assumes the constitutional ontology of Document III.01, the systems-science substrate of Document V.01, the biological-evolutionary critique of Document II.01, the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01, and the foundational-normative inheritance of Document I.01. It does not duplicate the capture architecture of Document II.03 or the cross-regime interactions of Document II.08, and it does not specify decision rights, which are Part XII's terrain. The analytical work proceeds in seven movements: currency-and-monetary shocks; supply-chain collapse; debt-crisis cascades; technological labor displacement; capital concentration and contemporary monopoly; cross-regime interactions; and closing synthesis.

# Part One — Currency-and-Monetary Shocks

The currency-and-monetary dimension is engaged through the post-2008 crisis literature, the heterodox-economics canon, and the comparative-monetary-history record.

Adam Tooze's *Crashed: How a Decade of Financial Crises Changed the World* (2018) supplies the authoritative documentary account of the 2008 financial crisis and its decade-long aftermath, registering the crisis not as a discrete American event but as a planetary financial-system disturbance whose institutional response — the Federal Reserve's swap-line provision to foreign central banks, the European austerity regime, the unprecedented monetary intervention — established the architecture under which the subsequent decade was governed. The heterodox-economics canon supplies the analytical apparatus the orthodox monetary economics did not. Hyman Minsky's *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986) specified, in the financial-instability hypothesis it is named for, that financial systems are endogenously unstable — that periods of stability generate the leverage and risk-taking that produce instability — and the post-2008 record has been read as the Minsky moment the orthodox apparatus had been incapable of anticipating. L. Randall Wray's *Modern Money Theory: A Primer on Macroeconomics for Sovereign Monetary Systems* (2012) and Stephanie Kelton's *The Deficit Myth: Modern Monetary Theory and the Birth of the People's Economy* (2020) articulate the modern-monetary-theory position, specifying that a sovereign issuer of fiat currency operates under different fiscal constraints than the household-budget analogy of orthodox public finance assumes, and that the operative constraint is inflation and real-resource availability rather than the financing of expenditure. Costas Lapavitsas's *Profiting Without Producing: How Finance Exploits Us All* (2013) supplies the financialization analysis: that the contemporary economy has been reorganized so that financial activity increasingly extracts profit through monetary and quasi-monetary operations rather than through the financing of productive enterprise. Mark Blyth's *Austerity: The History of a Dangerous Idea* (2013) supplies the political-economic critique of the austerity response, documenting how the post-2008 austerity regime extended and deepened the crisis it claimed to address.

The comparative-monetary-history literature carries the analysis at deeper temporal scale. Barry Eichengreen's *Globalizing Capital: A History of the International Monetary System* (1996) traced the long history of international monetary arrangements, and his *Exorbitant Privilege: The Rise and Fall of the Dollar and the Future of the International Monetary System* (2011) traced the architecture of dollar primacy. Moritz Schularick's work on financial cycles, extending the Minsky framework into systematic empirical analysis, established the contemporary statistical record of financial crisis dynamics across the long historical record. Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff's *This Time Is Different: Eight Centuries of Financial Folly* (2009) supplied an influential synthesis of the long history of sovereign, banking, and currency crises — but the document must engage this work alongside the Pollin-and-Ash critique that materially revised its central claims. The Herndon-Ash-Pollin paper of 2013 documented that the empirical results in Reinhart and Rogoff's subsequent work linking high public debt to lower economic growth — results that had been deployed in the post-2008 austerity debate as quantitative justification for fiscal contraction — contained spreadsheet errors and contested coding choices, and that the corrected results did not support the strong threshold claim the original analysis had carried. The document carries Reinhart-Rogoff's historical synthesis as a genuine contribution while registering the contestability of the policy claims drawn from it: a constitutional architecture must be built on evidence robust to its own critical scrutiny.

The substantive constitutional treatment of currency architecture is in Document XII.02; this thread specifies the diagnostic foundation.

# Part Two — Supply-Chain Collapse

The supply-chain dimension is engaged through the COVID-19 archive, the political economy of supply-chain consolidation, the resilient-supply-chain literature, and the contemporary policy response.

The COVID-19 pandemic supplied the most comprehensive contemporary demonstration of supply-chain fragility, registered across the McKinsey, World Bank, and academic supply-chain literatures: the simultaneous disruption of production, transport, and distribution across multiple sectors; the cascading shortages of medical equipment, semiconductors, foodstuffs, and the inputs to nearly every industry; and the recognition that the highly optimized just-in-time global supply chain produced over the preceding decades had been optimized for efficiency under stable conditions rather than for resilience under disturbance. Peter S. Goodman's *Davos Man: How the Billionaires Devoured the World* (2022), engaged in Document II.04 for the biological-political-economic coupling, is engaged here for its broader documentation of how the supply-chain crisis was navigated by the contemporary class of global capital — concentrating wealth and protecting certain supply chains, particularly the financial and pharmaceutical, while leaving others, particularly those serving working populations, to the disruption.

The resilient-supply-chain literature, anchored in Yossi Sheffi's *The Power of Resilience: How the Best Companies Manage the Unexpected* (2015), specified the engineering and management principles through which supply chains can be designed for resilience under shock — redundancy, modularity, geographic diversification, the visibility of upstream dependencies — and the comparative work of Willy C. Shih and others has documented how the consolidation of global supply chains has reduced these resilience properties in pursuit of margin. The contemporary policy response — the United States CHIPS and Science Act of 2022, the supply-chain provisions of the Inflation Reduction Act, the European Union's strategic-autonomy framework, and the broader trajectory of near-shoring, friend-shoring, and re-shoring — registers a recognition at the policy level that the supply-chain architecture inherited from the preceding decades is incompatible with the disturbance landscape the polity faces. The diagnostic specifies that supply-chain disturbance is a standing feature of the political-economic regime, not a discrete crisis to be navigated and forgotten, and the constitutional architecture must absorb it through the cultivation conditions Parts VI and XII operationalize.

# Part Three — Debt-Crisis Cascades

The debt-crisis dimension is engaged through the long-historical archive, the sovereign-debt literature with its contestation, the household-debt literature, and the climate-and-ecological-debt literature.

David Graeber's *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) supplies the long-historical anchor: Graeber's anthropological-historical analysis specified that debt is not a recent or natural institution but a particular and ancient social technology whose form has varied substantially across civilizations, and that the contemporary form — debt as universal, individuated, and morally weighted — is a specific historical construction rather than the timeless arrangement the contemporary common sense takes it to be. Graeber's analysis specifies the long pattern of debt-jubilee and debt-cancellation across the ancient world and the consequences, in repeated historical episodes, when accumulated debt was not periodically cleared.

The contemporary sovereign-debt-crisis literature carries the analysis into the present. Reinhart and Rogoff's *This Time Is Different* (2009), engaged with the Pollin-and-Ash critique in Part One, supplied the foundational catalog of sovereign-debt crises across the long historical record. Jerome Roos's *Why Not Default? The Political Economy of Sovereign Debt* (2019) supplied the substantive recent analysis of why sovereign defaults — long predicted by economic theory to be common under conditions of unsustainable debt — have become relatively rare in the contemporary period, locating the explanation in the institutional architecture through which creditors enforce repayment against sovereign debtors. Atif Mian and Amir Sufi's *House of Debt: How They (and You) Caused the Great Recession, and How We Can Prevent It from Happening Again* (2014) supplied the household-debt analysis, specifying that the 2008 financial crisis was driven in significant part by the prior accumulation of household debt rather than only by financial-sector dynamics, and that the policy response — which protected financial institutions while leaving household debt intact — substantially deepened the recession that followed. Mark Blyth's *Austerity*, engaged in Part One, supplied the political-economic critique of the austerity response that the debt-crisis framing was used to authorize.

The climate-and-ecological-debt literature carries the analysis into the register of intergenerational and inter-national obligation. Joan Martínez-Alier's *The Environmentalism of the Poor: A Study of Ecological Conflicts and Valuation* (2002) supplied the foundational articulation of ecological debt — the recognition that the global North has, across centuries of industrial development, drawn down ecological resources and dispersed ecological harms whose costs have been borne disproportionately by the global South, and that an honest account of the debt between regions would have to include this ecological accumulation alongside the financial. Walden Bello's *Deglobalization: Ideas for a New World Economy* (2002) extended the analysis through the global-South debt-and-structural-adjustment literature, specifying how the debt architecture of the post-colonial international system has functioned as an instrument of structural adjustment, opening southern economies to the terms set by northern creditors and constraining their developmental autonomy. The debt-crisis dimension is, in sum, both a contemporary political-economic disturbance and the contemporary form of a centuries-long architecture whose disturbance is structurally embedded.

# Part Four — Technological Labor Displacement

The technological-labor-displacement dimension is engaged through the automation literature, with the central contested empirical claim engaged on its strongest terms.

Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael A. Osborne's "The Future of Employment: How Susceptible Are Jobs to Computerisation?" (2013) supplied the influential estimate that approximately 47 percent of United States jobs were at high risk of automation over the following one to two decades, and the paper became the most-cited reference in subsequent policy discussion of automation and labor. The document engages the Frey-Osborne estimate with the contestability the subsequent literature has established. The methodology — extrapolating from expert assessments of which occupations could in principle be automated by extant or near-term technologies — has been challenged by Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo's "Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor" (2019) and the broader Acemoglu-Restrepo automation-and-tasks corpus, which specifies that the relevant analytical unit is the task rather than the occupation, that occupations are typically bundles of tasks of which only some are automatable, and that historical automation has reliably produced both displacement of automatable tasks and creation of new tasks complementary to the new technology, so that the net employment effect depends on the balance of these dynamics rather than only on the displacement side. David Autor's "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation" (2015) supplied the canonical statement of the polarization-of-labor-markets analysis: that automation has tended to hollow out middle-skill occupations while increasing demand at the high-skill and low-skill poles, producing the labor-market polarization the contemporary economy registers. The Brookings, OECD, and ILO future-of-work corpus has carried the institutional-policy literature, generally arriving at more moderate displacement estimates than the original Frey-Osborne figure while affirming substantial structural change.

Aaron Benanav's *Automation and the Future of Work* (2020) supplies the demand-side critique that the document must hold alongside the technological-displacement analyses: Benanav specifies that the contemporary labor-market dysfunction — stagnant wages, precarious employment, underutilization of labor — is driven less by an unprecedented wave of automation than by a long-running secular slowdown in aggregate demand for labor, traceable to structural features of the global political economy that the automation framing has tended to obscure. Wolfgang Streeck's scholarship on the political economy of labor-market transformation supplies the comparative analysis of how labor-market institutions across the advanced economies have been reshaped under the pressures of financialization, globalization, and political reorganization. The diagnostic carries the dispute substantively rather than flattening it: technological displacement is real, the magnitude of the displacement is contested at the specifications the Frey-Osborne figure popularized, and the constitutional architecture must be built to absorb both the technological displacement that is occurring and the deeper structural labor-market reorganization that has been underway across decades.

# Part Five — Capital Concentration and Contemporary Monopoly

The capital-concentration dimension is engaged through the long-historical-inequality literature, the contemporary wealth-concentration scholarship, and the monopoly-and-antitrust canon.

Thomas Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2014) supplied the long-historical synthesis: the empirical demonstration, drawing on tax records and other archival sources across two centuries, that capital concentration is the long-run tendency of capitalist economies under conditions in which the rate of return to capital exceeds the rate of economic growth, and that the mid-twentieth-century period of declining inequality across the advanced economies was a historical anomaly produced by the destruction of capital in two world wars and the redistributive politics of the post-war settlement rather than the steady state of the system. Piketty's *Capital and Ideology* (2019) extended the analysis into the political and ideological history of inequality regimes across the longer historical record. Gabriel Zucman's *The Hidden Wealth of Nations: The Scourge of Tax Havens* (2015) supplied the analysis of offshore wealth, documenting the magnitude of capital held in tax-haven jurisdictions and the consequences for national-tax-base measurement and for the assessment of inequality. Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman's *The Triumph of Injustice: How the Rich Dodge Taxes and How to Make Them Pay* (2019) supplied the United States–focused analysis of how the contemporary American tax system has been reshaped to favor capital over labor and the wealthy over the rest. Branko Milanovic's *Global Inequality: A New Approach for the Age of Globalization* (2016) supplied the global-scale analysis, registering the divergent trajectories of within-country and between-country inequality across the era of globalization.

The contemporary monopoly-and-antitrust literature has emerged as the substantive response to the recognition that capital concentration is, in the contemporary economy, registered most directly in market concentration and in the dominance of a small set of platform monopolies. Lina Khan's "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" (Yale Law Journal, 2017) supplied the foundational legal analysis specifying that the contemporary antitrust framework — built on the consumer-welfare standard developed in the 1970s and 1980s — was structurally incapable of recognizing the kind of market dominance Amazon and the parallel platform corporations had built, because the dominance does not register as consumer-price harm but in the platform's capacity to organize, surveil, and extract from the economic activity conducted on it. Khan's analysis has shaped the subsequent contemporary antitrust scholarship and her subsequent tenure as chair of the United States Federal Trade Commission. Tim Wu's *The Curse of Bigness: Antitrust in the New Gilded Age* (2018) supplied the historical context and the case for the recovery of the original antitrust tradition that the consumer-welfare standard had displaced. Matt Stoller's *Goliath: The 100-Year War Between Monopoly Power and Democracy* (2019) supplied the political-historical narrative of how the American antitrust tradition was built, was dismantled across the second half of the twentieth century, and is now being reconstituted. The Tax Justice Network and the scholarship of Nicholas Shaxson supply the contemporary documentation of the tax-haven and platform-monopoly architecture through which the capital concentration is conducted and protected.

The diagnostic specifies that capital concentration is the central long-run political-economic disturbance of the contemporary period — the disturbance through which the political and economic power required for the constitutional cultivation Part VI specifies has been progressively concentrated in a small subset of actors whose interests are systematically misaligned with the cultivation framing.

# Part Six — Cross-Regime Interactions

The political-economic-disturbance regime is coupled to the other four regimes, and this section specifies the political-economic-regime side of each coupling. Document II.03 carries the cultural-political-economic capture architecture in its contemporary American form; Document II.08 carries the integrating cross-regime architecture; this section specifies the political-economic-regime side at the diagnostic depth those documents then integrate.

The political-economic-cultural coupling registers the capital-concentration reproduction of caste and oligarchy across generations, and is carried in its concrete contemporary American form by Document II.03's capture architecture; the technological labor displacement of Part Four reproduces racial and embodied inequality through the differential exposure of populations to displacement and the differential access to the new tasks that automation creates. The political-economic-cognitive coupling is the coupling Document II.06 specified the cognitive-regime side of: surveillance capitalism as concentrated political-economic-and-cognitive disturbance; algorithmic labor-market discrimination; AI-driven capital concentration. The political-economic-biological coupling is the coupling Document II.04 specified the biological-regime side of: the pandemic-and-supply-chain coupling demonstrated by COVID-19; the political economy of pharmaceutical development that determines access to medicines and vaccines; the food-system-and-pandemic coupling through which industrial agriculture generates pandemic pathogens. The political-economic-physical-environmental coupling is the coupling Document II.05 specified the physical-environmental-regime side of: the climate-and-financialization literature through Adrienne Buller, in which the climate disturbance is being routed through financial instruments that principally serve to extend the asset base of existing financial actors; the long fossil-fuel-political-economy archive through which climate policy has been shaped, delayed, and absorbed across half a century.

# Part Seven — Closing Synthesis

The five dimensions of the political-economic-disturbance regime specified in this document — currency-and-monetary shocks, supply-chain collapse, debt-crisis cascades, technological labor displacement, capital concentration — together with the cross-regime couplings of Part Six, specify the political-economic landscape the constitutional architecture must absorb.

This document is the Part II political-economic-regime diagnostic against which Document XII.02 on currency architecture, Document X.01 on the political economy of transformative legislation, and the structural-commitments documents of Part XII operate. Document II.03 carries the contemporary American capture architecture at the cultural-political-economic intersection that this diagnostic's findings about capital concentration most directly intersect with. Document II.08 integrates the cross-regime interactions architecture this document's Part Six specified the political-economic-regime side of. The cultivation-terrain documents of Part VI — particularly Document VI.01 on land, Document VI.04 on circular material flows, and Document VI.07 on care and social reproduction — specify the cultivation conditions through which the political-economic regime's harm is mediated. Documents III.01, V.01, and VII.01 supply the ontology, the systems-science substrate, and the methodological discipline under which the document has been conducted.

The political-economic substrate of the biocultural polity is being disturbed at sustained intensity across each dimension named above, and the disturbance is structurally bound to the architecture of the contemporary political economy rather than being a sequence of contingent crises within an otherwise stable system. A constitutional architecture adequate to its biocultural ontology must absorb the regime not as a sequence of episodic shocks to be managed but as the standing condition of the political-economic substrate through which the polity's material life is conducted. What this document diagnoses, the currency architecture of Document XII.02, the political-economy-of-transformative-legislation analysis of Document X.01, the structural-commitments architecture of Part XII, and the cultivation-terrain documents of Part VI are written to operationalize.
