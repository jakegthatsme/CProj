## Thread D — The Renovation-versus-Decolonial Question Engaged as Analytical Move

The renovation-versus-decolonial question runs through the project from the fifth-pass framing forward. The fifth-pass project understood its work as renovation: the captured eighteenth-century constitutional architecture is to be replaced by a new architecture synthesized from the multi-traditional canon at primary-articulator standing. The sixth-pass commitment moves beyond the design-renovation framing under three pressures the decolonial canon names. This thread engages the question at the level of the arc as a whole and gives the project's settled analytical position.

The decolonial critique, in its strongest articulation, holds that any single-document constitutional synthesis drawing on multiple traditions performs an act of appropriation by virtue of the synthesis-into-architecture, regardless of how carefully the source-traditions are preserved in the synthesis. Walter D. Mignolo articulates the critique through the coloniality framework in *The Darker Side of Western Modernity: Global Futures, Decolonial Options* (Duke, 2011) and elaborates it with Catherine Walsh in *On Decoloniality: Concepts, Analytics, Praxis* (Duke, 2018); Aníbal Quijano in "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America" (*Nepantla* 1, no. 3, 2000) articulates the coloniality-of-power matrix that structures the categories under which knowledge is produced and integrated; María Lugones articulates the coloniality of gender in "Heterosexualism and the Colonial/Modern Gender System" (*Hypatia* 22, no. 1, 2007: 186–209) and "Toward a Decolonial Feminism" (*Hypatia* 25, no. 4, 2010: 742–759); Boaventura de Sousa Santos articulates the cognitive injustice of epistemicide in *Epistemologies of the South: Justice against Epistemicide* (Paradigm, 2014); Eve Tuck (Unangax̂) and K. Wayne Yang articulate decolonization-not-as-metaphor in "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" (*Decolonization: Indigeneity, Education and Society* 1, no. 1, 2012: 1–40); Linda Martín Alcoff articulates the analytical politics of whiteness in *The Future of Whiteness* (Polity, 2015) and elaborates the methodological consequences for cross-traditional knowledge work. The critique has additional articulators across the decolonial canon — Linda Tuhiwai Smith on decolonizing methodologies, Sylvia Wynter on the overrepresentation of "Man," Cedric Robinson on the Black radical tradition, the broader Modernity/Coloniality and decolonial-thought corpus — all of whom the project engages directly in the upstream documents `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6`, `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6`, and `XIII.01_Decision_Rights_Synthesis_v6`.

The cultivation framing's response to the critique, developed across the project's arc, holds that cultivation sits differently in relation to the decolonial critique than design-renovation does. The sixth-pass analytical move — articulated at the level of methodology in `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6`, at the level of constitutional ontology in `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6`'s Thread M, at the level of method in `XIII.01_Decision_Rights_Synthesis_v6`'s Thread B, and at the level of constitutional proposal in `XIII.01_Decision_Rights_Synthesis_v6`'s Thread Q — is that cultivation is not synthesis-into-architecture but the maintenance of conditions under which traditions continue to grow alongside one another in their distinctive registers. The move has three substantive forms.

First, cultivation specifies a discipline, not a fixed architecture.
