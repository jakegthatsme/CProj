## Part One — The Founding-Era Anti-Corruption Tradition Reconstructed and Re-Framed Under Cultivation

The founding-era anti-corruption tradition is engaged through Zephyr Teachout's *Corruption in America: From Benjamin Franklin's Snuff Box to Citizens United* (2014), which reconstructs the anti-corruption constitutional commitment the founders held. Teachout argues that the founders understood corruption as a broad constitutional category, and that the contemporary judicial narrowing of that category — engaged in Part Three — has sharply reduced the constitutional vocabulary available to address concentrated-wealth capture of political institutions. The founders engaged anti-corruption commitments at several registers: through *Federalist 10* on concentrated factional interests and the conditions under which republican government might be sustained against them; through *Federalist 51* on separation of powers as anti-concentration architecture; through *Federalist 57* on the conditions under which representatives remain accountable to their constituents; and through *Federalist 76* on appointments and the exercise of appointment power without corruption. The Anti-Federalist anti-concentration tradition — engaged in Document I.03 through the Brutus, Federal Farmer, and Centinel essays — articulated parallel concerns about concentrated power and the conditions under which republican government might be sustained.

The Emoluments Clauses of the Constitution — Article I, Section 9, Clause 8, prohibiting foreign emoluments, and Article II, Section 1, Clause 7, prohibiting domestic emoluments to the President — constitute the founding-era constitutional architecture of anti-corruption operationalized through specific provisions. The litigation surrounding the application of the Emoluments Clauses during the Trump administration of 2017 to 2021 provides the contemporary engagement with that architecture's operation. The longer anti-corruption tradition extends through the Progressive Era anti-trust corpus — Louis Brandeis's *Other People's Money and How the Bankers Use It* (1914) on concentrated financial power, Theodore Roosevelt's New Nationalism address of 1910 on concentrated wealth as a constitutional problem, and the wider Progressive Era anti-trust legislation and adjudication — and through the New Deal and Great Society anti-concentration architecture. The campaign-finance regulatory architecture of 1971 through 1974 — the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, the 1974 amendments following Watergate, and the establishment of the Federal Election Commission — constitutes the most recent federal anti-corruption architecture before the post-1976 judicial dismantling Part Three engages.

### Cultivation-Condition Re-Frame

Under the cultivation framing, the founding-era anti-corruption tradition is re-read as a cultivation-condition specification: anti-corruption commitments are not engineered safeguards specifiable in advance of cultivation but cultivation conditions for the operation of republican decision rights. Those conditions are sustained or eroded across generations through political and institutional work; they are not properties of designed architecture alone but of the ongoing political-institutional-cultural cultivation through which the architecture operates. The Anti-Federalist scale-and-locality tradition engaged in Document I.03 specifies the cultivation-condition substrate: feedback conditions, the scale-matching of decision rights to the substrate they operate over, the cultivation of civic capacity, and the conditions of consent. The normative commitments survive the cultivation re-frame — anti-corruption, anti-concentration, fiduciary public-trust government, and feedback-and-accountability architecture are foundational cultivation conditions — while what does not survive is the design-imposition framing under which the founders sometimes operated in specifying the institutional architecture meant to secure those commitments.

## Part Two — Eastern Political-Economic Traditions on Concentrated Power as Primary Articulators

The Eastern political-economic primary articulators on concentrated power stand at primary-articulator constitutional standing in the multi-traditional canonical inheritance. They are engaged here as primary articulators of anti-concentration constitutional commitments the contemporary Western anti-oligarchy literature partially recovers — not as comparative supplement to a primarily Anglo-American anti-corruption argument. The multi-traditional canonical inheritance is the ground against which the contemporary American capture architecture is read.

### The Confucian and Mencian Tradition

The Confucian and Mencian tradition on concentrated power is engaged through the *Mencius* on *renzheng* — humane rule — and on the legitimacy of overthrow when the polity's conditions of life are abandoned. The engagement runs through Bryan W. Van Norden's translation of *Mengzi: With Selections from Traditional Commentaries* (2008); through Wm. Theodore de Bary's *The Trouble with Confucianism* (1991) and *Asian Values and Human Rights: A Confucian Communitarian Perspective* (1998); and through Tu Weiming's *Confucian Thought: Selfhood as Creative Transformation* (1985) and *Centrality and Commonality* (1989). The Mencian doctrine articulated at Mengzi 1A.7, 1B.8, and 4A.9 establishes that the ruler's legitimacy depends on the cultivation of virtue and on the maintenance of the conditions under which the people flourish; when the people are left without their constant means of livelihood, *zhi chan* — when concentrated wealth at the top denies the people their material foundation — legitimacy is forfeit, and overthrow becomes legitimate. The engagement at Mengzi 1B.8 is foundational: asked whether the regicide of King Zhou by King Wu was legitimate, Mencius answered that what was killed was not a king but "a mere fellow," a ruler who had so thoroughly abandoned the cultivation of virtue that he had ceased to be a ruler in any real sense.

The Confucian remonstrance tradition, *jian* — institutionalized through the Imperial Censorate systems of the Han, Tang, Song, Ming, and Qing dynasties as a constitutional architecture of public check against sovereign authority — articulates anti-concentration commitments at the level of institutional architecture. The contemporary relevance is engaged through the wider Confucian-democracy scholarship: de Bary, Tu Weiming, Joseph Chan's *Confucian Perfectionism* (2014), and Daniel Bell's *The China Model* (2015), the last engaged with critical caveats.

### The Daoist Tradition

The Daoist tradition on concentrated power is engaged through the *Daodejing*'s treatment of the violence of hoarded plenty and the wisdom of economic moderation. Chapters 53, 75, and 77 — engaged through Philip J. Ivanhoe's *The Daodejing of Laozi* (2002) and Brook Ziporyn's *Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings* (2009) — articulate a critique of concentrated wealth as violence against the polity's conditions. The Daoist commitment is that concentrated wealth produces social and political harm not as a collateral effect but as a constitutive feature; the cultivation of the polity under Daoist commitments requires a structural commitment against accumulation. Chapter 77's image of the Way as a bow that reduces what is excessive and adds to what is deficient specifies redistribution as the natural ordering the polity should track.

### The Classical Indian Arthashastra Tradition

Kautilya's *Arthashastra* — engaged through Patrick Olivelle's *King, Governance, and Law in Ancient India: Kauṭilya's Arthaśāstra* (2013) — is a foundational political-economic-constitutional text comparable in scope and analytical depth to the Aristotelian *Politics* but with a longer continuous tradition of engagement. The *Arthashastra*'s treatment of the corruption of revenue administration — its detailed account of the conditions under which revenue officials defraud the sovereign and the public, the institutional architecture necessary to constrain such corruption, and the accountability mechanisms it specifies — articulates a foundational engagement with anti-corruption commitments at constitutional standing. The dharmashastra tradition's constraints on unrestricted accumulation and the Asokan dharma-edicts on right rule, engaged through Romila Thapar's *Aśoka and the Decline of the Mauryas* (1961, revised through 2012), extend the engagement.

The dalit-Ambedkarite re-reading of the classical Indian tradition is a foundational counter-tradition at primary-articulator standing. B. R. Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* (1936) and the wider Ambedkarite corpus articulate a critique of concentrated caste-and-capital power as a constitutive architecture of structural oppression the classical Indian tradition historically maintained. Both the classical Indian and the dalit-Ambedkarite traditions are preserved here at primary-articulator standing in their contention with each other.

### The Islamic Anti-Riba and Zakat-and-Waqf Architecture

The Islamic political-economic tradition on concentrated power is engaged through the Qur'anic prohibition of *riba* — usury, construed broadly to include exploitative interest and rentier extraction; through the obligatory *zakat* architecture, the obligation of wealth redistribution to the poor specified at Qur'an 9:60 and across the Qur'anic and Sunnah corpus; and through the *waqf* tradition as an anti-concentration cultivation-condition specification — the perpetual charitable trust under which property is endowed for charitable or public purposes in perpetuity with the principal remaining inviolate. The wider Islamic political-philosophical tradition through Al-Farabi, Al-Mawardi, and Ibn Khaldun extends the engagement. The scholarship runs through Murat Çizakça's *A History of Philanthropic Foundations: The Islamic World from the Seventh Century to the Present* (2000), through Timur Kuran's *The Long Divergence: How Islamic Law Held Back the Middle East* (2011), engaged with critical-historical-economic caveats, and through Wael B. Hallaq's *The Impossible State* (2013) and *Sharīʿa: Theory, Practice, Transformations* (2009).

### The Andean and African Anti-Accumulation Traditions

The Andean *ayni* reciprocity tradition — engaged through the PRATEC corpus, through Josef Estermann's *Filosofía Andina* (1998), and through Eduardo Gudynas on *Buen Vivir* — articulates anti-accumulation cultivation-condition specifications at primary-articulator standing. The African Ubuntu economic critique of accumulation — engaged through Mogobe Ramose's *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (1999) and the wider African moral-economy literature — articulates relational commitments that concentrated accumulation violates. The Caribbean tradition through C. L. R. James, through Eric Williams's *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944), and through the contemporary engagement extends the analysis into the political economy of slavery and its afterlife.

## Part Three — The Post-1976 Doctrinal Sequence as Constitutional Architecture of Capture

The post-1976 doctrinal sequence — *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976), *Federal Election Commission v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life* (1986), *Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce* (1990), *McConnell v. Federal Election Commission* (2003), *Federal Election Commission v. Wisconsin Right to Life* (2007), *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission* (2010), *SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission* (2010, D.C. Circuit), *McCutcheon v. Federal Election Commission* (2014), *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta* (2021), and *Janus v. American Federation of State, County, and Municipal Employees* (2018) — constitutes the constitutional architecture under which concentrated-wealth political participation has been progressively expanded and the post-Watergate campaign-finance regulatory architecture progressively dismantled.

### Buckley and the Money-as-Speech Equivalence

*Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976), established the money-as-speech equivalence the subsequent sequence elaborated. The per curiam opinion upheld the Federal Election Campaign Act's contribution limits as constitutional under the interest in preventing corruption and the appearance of corruption, while striking down independent-expenditure limits as an impermissible burden on political speech. The analytical move — that the expenditure of money in political communication is expressive speech protected by the First Amendment, with regulation of that expenditure subject to the most demanding scrutiny — established the doctrinal foundation for the subsequent expansion of concentrated-wealth political participation. The critique of *Buckley*'s move runs through Lawrence Lessig's *Republic, Lost: How Money Corrupts Congress — and a Plan to Stop It* (2011), through Teachout's *Corruption in America* (2014), and through Richard L. Hasen's *Plutocrats United: Campaign Money, the Supreme Court, and the Distortion of American Elections* (2016) and *Cheap Speech: How Disinformation Poisons Our Politics* (2022).

### The Austin–McConnell–Citizens United Sequence

*Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce*, 494 U.S. 652 (1990), upheld Michigan's prohibition on corporate independent expenditures in candidate elections, articulating the anti-distortion rationale: the concentration of corporate wealth creates a distortion in political processes the state has an interest in preventing. *McConnell v. FEC*, 540 U.S. 93 (2003), upheld the principal provisions of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, including the restrictions on soft-money contributions to political parties and the electioneering-communications restrictions on corporate and union expenditures within specified windows before federal elections. *Federal Election Commission v. Wisconsin Right to Life*, 551 U.S. 449 (2007), began the narrowing of *McConnell* through its engagement with as-applied challenges to the electioneering-communications provisions.

*Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), overruled *Austin* and the relevant portions of *McConnell*, holding that the First Amendment prohibits restrictions on independent corporate political expenditures in candidate elections. Justice Kennedy's majority opinion held that corporations and unions hold First Amendment expression rights coextensive with those of individuals in the political-speech context, and that *Austin*'s anti-distortion rationale did not survive First Amendment analysis. Justice Stevens's dissent, joined by Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor, argued that corporations are legal-person entities created under specific legal frameworks for specific purposes rather than natural persons holding inherent First Amendment rights; that the anti-distortion rationale and the interest in preventing corruption and its appearance survive constitutional analysis; and that the consequences of the majority's holding for democratic governance are severe. The scholarly engagement runs through Robert C. Post's *Citizens Divided: Campaign Finance Reform and the Constitution* (2014), Adam Winkler's *We the Corporations: How American Businesses Won Their Civil Rights* (2018), and Lessig, Hasen, and Teachout.

### SpeechNow, McCutcheon, AFP v. Bonta, and Janus

*SpeechNow.org v. FEC*, 599 F.3d 686 (D.C. Cir. 2010), held that independent-expenditure-only committees — the Super-PAC architecture — cannot be subject to contribution limits, effectively eliminating the contribution-limit architecture for political action committees that operate independently of candidate campaigns. *McCutcheon v. FEC*, 572 U.S. 185 (2014), struck down aggregate-contribution limits on individual donors to federal candidates, committees, and parties, expanding the volume of contributions any single donor can make in an election cycle. *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta*, 594 U.S. ___ (2021), held that California's requirement of donor disclosure for tax-exempt charitable organizations operating in the state violated the First Amendment's associational-rights protection, restricting the disclosure architecture for charitable organizations engaged in political advocacy.

*Janus v. American Federation of State, County, and Municipal Employees, Council 31*, 585 U.S. ___ (2018), is engaged here as part of the broader money-in-politics asymmetry. The decision held that public-sector unions cannot collect agency fees from non-member employees, reducing the financial resources of public-sector unions and altering the political-economy balance between concentrated-wealth political-advocacy entities and labor unions. *Janus* is not itself a campaign-finance decision, but the money-in-politics asymmetry the post-1976 sequence has produced is reinforced by the constraints *Janus* places on labor-union political resources.

### The Doctrinal Sequence as Capture Architecture

The post-1976 sequence is read not as a series of separate decisions but as the constitutional architecture under which concentrated-wealth capture of the political-economic conditions of cultural formation has been licensed and expanded. The cumulative architectural effect — the expansion of corporate political-speech rights, the elimination of independent-expenditure limits, the elimination of aggregate-contribution limits, the reduction of donor-disclosure architecture, and the constraints on union political resources — constitutes a constitutional architecture of capture operating through formally First Amendment-protected channels. The engagement with the dissents — Stevens in *Citizens United*, Breyer in *McCutcheon* and *Bonta*, and the Brennan-Marshall-White tradition on the speech-and-money distinction — articulates the constitutional alternative the majority sequence has displaced. The engagement does not collapse into polemic; the doctrinal-historical analysis attends to the constitutional arguments on each side of the majority-dissent disagreement.
