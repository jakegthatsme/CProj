## PART ONE — WHY REPRESENTATIVE DEMOCRACY ALONE IS INSUFFICIENT AS FEEDBACK CONDITION

### 1.1 The argument articulated

The argument that representative democracy alone is insufficient as feedback condition for the contemporary disturbance landscape operates at four levels. The deliberative-democracy tradition has articulated the structural case that the electoral apparatus, by itself, does not produce the deliberation the political-philosophical case for democratic legitimacy requires. The cognitive-aware critique that `II.06_Cognitive_Informational_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v6` and `VIII.01_Minds_Markets_Sacred_v6` carry has articulated the case that the contemporary cognitive-and-informational disturbance landscape produces feedback failure between empirical conditions and political response that no quantity of electoral mobilization alone can close. The oligarchic-capture critique that `II.03_Oligarchy_and_Dark_Money_v6`'s diagnostic engages has articulated the case that the contemporary American electoral apparatus has been progressively captured by concentrated political-economic interests in ways that the representative apparatus alone cannot reverse. The Indigenous-democratic critique has articulated the case that the inherited Western representative apparatus operates within a narrow band of the deliberative-political possibilities the Indigenous governance traditions have cultivated across centuries and that the Indigenous traditions supply institutional vocabulary the representative apparatus does not adequately engage.

The four critiques converge on the same diagnostic: representative democracy operates as one institutional layer of a polity's feedback architecture, and the contemporary disturbance landscape requires additional layers — deliberative, digital, structural-public-check, Eastern-deliberative, Indigenous-democratic — that operate jointly with representative democracy rather than as substitutes for it. The architectural move the present document develops is the specification of those additional layers as feedback architecture for the executive and legislative decisions the representative apparatus produces.

### 1.2 The deliberative-democracy tradition's analytical case

Jürgen Habermas's *Between Facts and Norms: Contributions to a Discourse Theory of Law and Democracy* (1992; English translation 1996) articulates the foundational political-philosophical case for the insufficiency of the electoral apparatus as feedback condition. Habermas's analytical case is that democratic legitimacy depends on the communicative conditions under which laws are produced — the conditions under which affected parties can participate in deliberation oriented to consensus through the force of the better argument, free from coercion and from systematic distortion of communication — and that the electoral apparatus operating alone does not produce these conditions. The implication is that the structural-architectural reconstruction of democratic legitimacy requires the institutional supplementation of the electoral apparatus with deliberative architectures whose communicative content engages the conditions Habermas's analytical apparatus specifies.

James Fishkin's deliberative-polling apparatus articulated across his career and in *Democracy When the People Are Thinking: Revitalizing Our Politics through Public Deliberation* (Oxford University Press, 2018) supplies the empirical methodology for measuring the gap between contemporary public opinion and the opinion that would emerge from structured deliberation among representative samples of the public. The Deliberative Polling methodology — recruitment of a representative sample, structured deliberation with balanced briefing materials, small-group discussion with trained moderators, plenary sessions with experts, surveys before and after deliberation — has been deployed in approximately one hundred Deliberative Polls across approximately thirty countries, with documented effects including attitude change toward better-informed positions, increases in policy knowledge, and the production of policy preferences that more accurately reflect what citizens would think under structured deliberation than do uninformed-public surveys.

Jane Mansbridge's *Beyond Adversary Democracy* (1980) and her subsequent work on representation, deliberation, and unitary versus adversary democracy supply the analytical apparatus on the distinction between the adversary-democratic register (in which conflicting interests are resolved through institutional mechanisms that aggregate preferences) and the unitary-democratic register (in which shared interests are cultivated through deliberation oriented to common ground). Mansbridge's contribution is the empirical demonstration that adversary-democratic and unitary-democratic registers operate on different scales and require different institutional architectures. Bernard Manin's *The Principles of Representative Government* (1997) supplies the analytical-historical apparatus on the structural difference between representative government as the contemporary American electoral apparatus operates and democratic government as the Athenian and other classical democratic traditions operated through sortition, citizen assemblies, and direct deliberation.

Cristina Lafont's *Democracy without Shortcuts: A Participatory Conception of Deliberative Democracy* (Oxford University Press, 2020) articulates the analytical concerns about lottocratic legitimacy that the sortition tradition raises. Lafont's case is that sortition-based citizens'-assembly outputs, by themselves, do not carry the political legitimacy electoral outputs carry, and that the institutional design of deliberative architectures must address the legitimacy question through the integration of deliberative outputs with the broader political-deliberative process. The critique is constructive: it specifies design questions that deliberative architectures must address rather than rejecting deliberative architectures.

Hélène Landemore's *Open Democracy: Reinventing Popular Rule for the Twenty-First Century* (Princeton University Press, 2020) articulates the architecturally ambitious institutional theory in which deliberative methodologies are integrated into a comprehensive institutional architecture combining lottocratic representation, enhanced accessibility, transparency, and inclusion. Landemore's empirical work draws on the Iceland constitutional process of 2010-2013, the Irish Citizens' Assemblies, the French Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat, and other deliberative experiments. David Van Reybrouck's *Against Elections: The Case for Democracy* (2016) articulates the historical and theoretical case for sortition as alternative to electoral democracy, tracing sortition from Athenian democracy through medieval Italian city-states through the contemporary deliberative-democracy revival.

### 1.3 The cognitive-aware critique

`II.06_Cognitive_Informational_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v6` and `VIII.01_Minds_Markets_Sacred_v6` carry the cognitive-and-informational-disturbance diagnostic at length; the present section engages the contributions for the feedback-condition argument. Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), Yaacov Trope and Nira Liberman's construal-level theory ("Construal-Level Theory of Psychological Distance," *Psychological Review*, 2010), Dan Kahan and the Cultural Cognition Project on identity-protective cognition, Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber's *The Enigma of Reason* (2017) on the argumentative theory of reason, and the broader cognitive-science apparatus converge on the diagnostic that human cognition systematically distorts the processing of political and policy information under conditions of cultural-cognitive polarization, psychological distance, and the contemporary informational landscape. The implication is that the electoral apparatus operating alone — premised on individual reasoners independently weighing evidence — systematically underperforms the diagnostic the cognitive-science apparatus articulates. The architectural response is the cultivation of deliberative-institutional architectures within which the cognitive features can be addressed through structured deliberation, perspective-taking, narrative exchange, and the integration of empirical evidence with cultural-identity processing.

### 1.4 The oligarchic-capture critique

`II.03_Oligarchy_and_Dark_Money_v6`'s diagnostic carries the oligarchic-capture analysis at length. The contributions for the feedback-condition argument include the empirical record of contemporary American campaign-finance architecture post-*Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), the empirical record of the dark-money infrastructure that has organized contemporary American political-economic capture, and the broader empirical record of the structural-political concentration that has progressively constrained the electoral apparatus's feedback capacity. ### 1.5 The Indigenous-democratic critique

The Indigenous-democratic critique operates from outside the Western representative-democratic apparatus and supplies cultivation-condition vocabulary the apparatus does not generate from within itself. The Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace (Kaianere'kó:wa), the Cherokee constitutional traditions, the Pueblo confederated-governance traditions, the Coast Salish traditional governance, and the broader Indigenous governance traditions across the Americas articulate deliberative architectures that operate through consensus-with-clan-deliberation, the seven-generation decision rule, structural-political-architectural commitments to nested deliberation across scales, and the broader institutional vocabulary `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6` and `IV.01_Deep_Substrate_v6` carry. ## PART TWO — THE DELIBERATIVE-DEMOCRACY TRADITION FOREGROUNDED

### 2.1 Habermas and the theory of communicative action

Jürgen Habermas's *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981; English translation 1984/1987) and *Between Facts and Norms* (1992; English translation 1996) articulate the political-philosophical apparatus on the communicative conditions of democratic legitimacy. The communicative-action apparatus specifies four validity claims raised in deliberation — truth, normative rightness, sincerity, comprehensibility — and the structural conditions under which deliberation can engage the claims through the force of the better argument rather than through coercion or systematic distortion. The implication for the e-democracy architecture is direct: the deliberative-institutional architecture must support communicative conditions under which the four validity claims can operate, with the institutional design addressing the distortion-of-communication conditions that contemporary informational and political-economic configurations produce.

The Habermasian apparatus has been critically engaged by multiple subsequent analytical traditions. Nancy Fraser's "Rethinking the Public Sphere: A Contribution to the Critique of Actually Existing Democracy" (*Social Text*, 1990) articulates the counterpublic critique: the Habermasian public sphere has historically operated through structural exclusions (gender, race, class) that the analytical apparatus did not adequately address, and the institutional architecture of deliberative democracy must engage the counterpublic configurations through which excluded groups have constructed their own deliberative apparatuses outside the dominant public sphere. The integration with the e-democracy architecture operates through the structural commitment that deliberative architectures must engage the counterpublic configurations rather than absorbing them into a single deliberative register.

### 2.2 Fishkin and the deliberative-polling apparatus

The Deliberative Polling methodology engaged at Section 1.2 supplies the operational record of structured deliberation across approximately a hundred deployments. The methodology's significance for the e-democracy architecture operates through the institutional model the methodology supplies: structured deliberation among representative samples of the public, with balanced briefing materials, trained moderation, expert engagement, and surveys before and after deliberation. The methodology demonstrates that structured deliberation can produce attitude change toward better-informed positions across contested policy questions, and that the institutional infrastructure required for the methodology is operationally feasible at scales of municipal, state, and national policy deliberation.

### 2.3 Mansbridge, Manin, Lafont, Landemore, Van Reybrouck

The deliberative-democracy and sortition scholarship engaged at Section 1.2 supplies the broader analytical apparatus on the institutional design of deliberative architectures. Mansbridge's apparatus on adversary-versus-unitary democracy specifies the institutional-design distinction; Manin's apparatus on representative-versus-democratic government specifies the historical-comparative distinction; Lafont's apparatus on lottocratic legitimacy specifies the design concerns the institutional architecture must address; Landemore's *Open Democracy* specifies the architecturally ambitious institutional theory; Van Reybrouck's *Against Elections* specifies the historical and theoretical case for sortition. The integration with the e-democracy architecture operates through the institutional vocabulary the deliberative-democracy and sortition scholarship supplies for the design of deliberative architectures at multiple scales of decision-making.

### 2.4 The citizens' assembly tradition's operational record

The Citizens' Assembly tradition has produced an operational record substantial enough to assess. The Irish Citizens' Assemblies on the Eighth Amendment (abortion), conducted 2016-2017 and producing recommendations the subsequent referendum substantially adopted, represent one of the most consequential deployments of sortition-based deliberation in contemporary politics. David Farrell's *A Conservative Revolution? Electoral Change in Twenty-First Century Ireland* (Oxford University Press, 2013) and his broader work on Irish deliberative-democracy practice supplies the institutional analysis. The Irish Citizens' Assembly on Climate (2022) produced substantive recommendations that have continued to shape Irish climate policy. The French Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat (2019-2020) convened 150 randomly selected citizens to produce 149 recommendations on climate policy. The British Columbia Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform (2004), the Ontario Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform (2006-2007), the UK Climate Assembly, the Brussels and German citizens' assemblies, and the approximately six hundred deliberative processes catalogued in the OECD's *Innovative Citizen Participation and New Democratic Institutions: Catching the Deliberative Wave* (2020) constitute the contemporary record.

Yves Sintomer's *The Government of Chance: Sortition and Democracy from Athens to the Present* (Cambridge University Press, 2023) supplies the contemporary historical-analytical apparatus on the sortition tradition. Gianpaolo Baiocchi's *Militants and Citizens: The Politics of Participatory Democracy in Porto Alegre* (Stanford University Press, 2005) supplies the institutional analysis of the participatory-budgeting tradition that has propagated across multiple Brazilian municipalities and subsequently across multiple international jurisdictions.

### 3.2 Beth Simone Noveck and the wiki-government apparatus

### 3.3 Audrey Tang and the Taiwanese civic-tech apparatus

Audrey Tang served as Taiwan's Digital Minister from 2016 through 2024 and previously as a leading figure in the g0v civic-tech movement that emerged from the 2014 Sunflower Movement. The vTaiwan platform operates through a structured process: open online consultation in which citizens submit statements about a policy issue; Pol.is statistical analysis that identifies points of agreement and disagreement across statements; structured deliberation oriented to consensus-points the Pol.is analysis surfaces; integration of the consensus-points into the formal policy-making process. The platform has been deployed on policy issues including the regulation of Uber and other gig-economy platforms, the legalization of cannabis for medical purposes, the regulation of online alcohol sales, and various other questions on which the conventional political process produced gridlock. Audrey Tang and E. Glen Weyl's *Plurality: The Future of Collaborative Technology and Democracy* (2024) articulates the broader theoretical framework within which the Taiwanese institutional experiments operate. The Plurality framework specifies that digital-deliberation infrastructure can address the cultural-cognition polarization the cognitive-aware critique engages by surfacing the consensus-points across cultural-political identifications and constructing the institutional architecture within which cross-identity deliberation can produce substantive policy outcomes.

### 3.4 Pol.is, Decidim, and the contemporary digital-deliberation platforms

Pol.is operates as open-source software developed by Colin Megill and the Pol.is team and deployed in vTaiwan and in approximately a hundred subsequent jurisdictions. The methodology operates through statistical analysis of submitted statements and the surfacing of points of consensus across cultural-political identifications, with the institutional architecture supporting the deliberative process at scales beyond what conventional in-person deliberation can achieve.

Decidim, developed by Barcelona-en-Comú during Ada Colau's mayoral administration (2015-2023), operates as open-source digital infrastructure for participatory democracy. The platform has been adopted by municipalities across Spain, France, Belgium, Italy, Mexico, and elsewhere, and supports participatory budgeting, citizen-initiated legislation, citizen consultation, and the broader content of digital participatory democracy. The Barcelona-en-Comú archive documents the integration of the Decidim platform with the broader political-organizing infrastructure that produced and sustained the Colau administration.

The CONSUL Democracy platform, developed by the Decide Madrid initiative during Manuela Carmena's mayoral administration (2015-2019), operates as the parallel Spanish-municipal digital-deliberation platform. Better Reykjavik, developed by the Citizens Foundation in Iceland and operationalized since 2010, operates as the long-running Icelandic digital-deliberation platform. The Estonian e-participation architecture, operating since the early 2000s within the broader Estonian e-government infrastructure, supplies the operational record on digital-deliberation infrastructure at national scale within the broader e-government configuration.

### 3.5 The Helsinki and Catalan participatory-budgeting documentation

The Helsinki participatory-budgeting program (OmaStadi), operating since 2018, constitutes the contemporary Nordic participatory-budgeting record. The program allocates approximately 8.8 million euros annually through participatory-budgeting deliberation operating through both digital and in-person infrastructure. ## PART FIVE — EASTERN DELIBERATIVE TRADITIONS AT PRIMARY-ARTICULATOR STANDING

### 5.1 The Zen-Buddhist *sangha* consensus tradition

The Zen-Buddhist *sangha* deliberation tradition operates as one of the longest continuously-practiced consensus-deliberation institutions in the human historical record. The *sangha* — the monastic community — operates under the Vinaya, the body of monastic discipline articulated through approximately twenty-five hundred years of textual and practical tradition, which specifies the institutional architecture of collective deliberation. The Vinaya specifies the conditions under which the *sangha* may make collective decisions — the *kammavācā* procedures — including the conditions for quorum, the structural-procedural commitments to hearing all parties, the structural-procedural commitments to the seeking of consensus rather than the operation of majority decision, and the broader institutional architecture of monastic deliberation.

Thich Nhat Hanh's *The Heart of the Buddha's Teaching: Transforming Suffering into Peace, Joy, and Liberation* (Broadway Books, 1998) and his broader corpus on engaged-Buddhist deliberative practice supplies the contemporary articulation of the *sangha* deliberation tradition. Thich Nhat Hanh's analytical contribution, drawing on his decades of monastic practice and his broader engagement with contemporary social-political conditions, is the demonstration that the *sangha* deliberation tradition operates through specific cultivation-condition practices — deep listening, loving speech, mindful presence, structural-procedural commitments to consensus — whose institutional content can be engaged as cultivation-condition vocabulary for contemporary deliberative architectures.

Sulak Sivaraksa and the International Network of Engaged Buddhists (INEB) supply the contemporary articulation of the engaged-Buddhist deliberative practice that operates across multiple Asian contexts and integrates the *sangha* deliberation tradition with contemporary social-political concerns. The integration with the e-democracy architecture is direct: the *sangha* deliberation tradition supplies cultivation-condition vocabulary the contemporary deliberative architectures can engage at primary-articulator standing, with the recognition that the contemporary digital-deliberation infrastructure is recent and partial recovery of insights the *sangha* tradition has carried at greater temporal depth.

### 5.2 The Confucian remonstrance tradition (*jian*)

The Confucian remonstrance tradition (*jian*) operates as the institutional architecture of structured criticism of authority under the Confucian moral-political tradition. The tradition's textual foundation includes the *Analects* of Confucius (particularly the passages on the duty of remonstrance — *Analects* 4.18, 11.24, 14.22, and others), the *Mencius* on the conditions under which authority loses its legitimacy through failure to engage remonstrance, and the broader Confucian textual tradition on the institutional architecture of remonstrance. The institutional record includes the historical-imperial remonstrance institutions across multiple Chinese dynasties — the Censorate (*yushitai*), the Hanlin Academy's remonstrance functions, the various dynastic remonstrance bureaucracies — and the broader institutional record of structured criticism within Confucian political institutions.

Wm. Theodore de Bary's *The Trouble with Confucianism* (Harvard University Press, 1991) and *Asian Values and Human Rights: A Confucian Communitarian Perspective* (Harvard University Press, 1998) supply the contemporary scholarly articulation of the Confucian remonstrance tradition's significance for contemporary deliberative-political analysis. De Bary's analytical contribution is the demonstration that the Confucian tradition includes substantial institutional and intellectual resources for the cultivation of structured criticism of authority that the contemporary literature on Confucianism-and-democracy has often underestimated. Tu Weiming's body of work on Neo-Confucian remonstrance and on the contemporary engagement of Confucian political-philosophical tradition with contemporary deliberative-political concerns extends the analytical apparatus.

The integration with the e-democracy architecture operates at primary-articulator standing. The Confucian remonstrance tradition supplies cultivation-condition vocabulary on the institutional architecture of structured criticism — the procedural commitments, the moral-philosophical foundation, the relationship between criticism and the broader moral-political tradition — that the contemporary e-democracy architecture can engage as articulation of the structural-public-check commitments rather than as comparative supplement to a Western-deliberative apparatus.

The historical-institutional record includes the early Islamic *shura* councils — the consultative council convened by the early caliphs, the broader institutional architecture of consultation within the early Islamic polity — and the subsequent juridical and political-philosophical tradition on *shura* across multiple Islamic political traditions.

Kamali's analytical contribution is the demonstration that the Islamic tradition includes substantial institutional and juridical resources for the cultivation of consultative deliberation, with the *shura* tradition's institutional architecture operating across multiple Islamic juridical schools and across multiple historical Islamic polities.

Abdullahi Ahmed An-Naʿim's *Islam and the Secular State: Negotiating the Future of Shari'a* (Harvard University Press, 2008) articulates the contemporary engagement of the Islamic moral-political tradition with the contemporary state-and-deliberative-democratic configuration. An-Naʿim's analytical case is that the Islamic moral-political tradition operates within the deliberative-political configuration through specific institutional architectures the *shura* tradition articulates, with the contemporary integration requiring the careful institutional-design work that the contemporary Islamic political-philosophical literature engages. ### 6.2 The Habermasian public-sphere apparatus and Fraser's counterpublic critique

Jürgen Habermas's *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere: An Inquiry into a Category of Bourgeois Society* (1962; English translation 1989) articulates the foundational analytical-historical apparatus on the bourgeois public sphere — the institutional configuration of coffeehouses, salons, journals, and the broader communicative infrastructure within which the eighteenth-century European public sphere operated as the site of rational-critical discourse on matters of public concern. Habermas's analytical contribution is the demonstration that the public sphere is not a pre-political given but a specific historical-institutional configuration whose content has been constructed through specific institutional and political-economic developments, and that the contemporary structural transformation of the public sphere through commercial mass media, advertising, and the broader contemporary media configuration has produced the systematic erosion of the communicative conditions the public sphere requires for the deliberative function democratic legitimacy demands.

The Habermasian apparatus has been critically engaged by multiple subsequent traditions. Nancy Fraser's "Rethinking the Public Sphere: A Contribution to the Critique of Actually Existing Democracy" (*Social Text*, 1990) articulates the counterpublic critique. Fraser's analytical contribution is the demonstration that the Habermasian public sphere has historically operated through structural exclusions — gender, race, class — that the analytical apparatus did not adequately address, and that the institutional architecture of deliberative democracy must engage the counterpublic configurations through which excluded groups have constructed their own deliberative apparatuses outside the dominant public sphere. The counterpublic configurations operate as parallel deliberative infrastructures that supply both the internal-cultivation work of subordinated groups and the external-deliberative engagement with the broader political-deliberative configuration. The integration with the e-democracy architecture operates through the structural commitment that the architecture must engage the counterpublic configurations rather than absorbing them into a single deliberative register.

### 6.3 Benkler on networked communicative production

Yochai Benkler's *The Wealth of Networks: How Social Production Transforms Markets and Freedom* (Yale University Press, 2006) articulates the analytical apparatus on the structural transformation of communicative production under the contemporary networked digital configuration. Benkler's analytical contribution is the demonstration that the contemporary networked digital configuration enables non-market, peer-production communicative architectures (Wikipedia, open-source software, networked journalism, the broader content of peer-production) that operate as alternatives to the commercial-mass-media configuration the contemporary information environment has been dominated by. The institutional implication for the e-democracy architecture is direct: the cultivation of networked-communicative-production infrastructure operates as one cultivation-condition layer of the communicative substrate the e-democracy architecture depends on, with the integration of the peer-production infrastructure with the broader deliberative-institutional apparatus the architectural-design question the cultivation work engages.

### 6.4 Curran, McChesney, Pickard on the structural conditions of democratic media

James Curran's *Media and Democracy* (Routledge, 2011) supplies the contemporary comparative-analytical apparatus on the structural conditions of democratic media systems. Curran's analytical contribution is the demonstration that the contemporary American media configuration — substantially commercial, substantially advertising-funded, substantially concentrated — has produced specific structural-political-economic consequences for the democratic-deliberative function the media should serve, and that the comparative record across multiple democratic societies articulates institutional alternatives the American configuration has not engaged.

Robert W. McChesney's *Rich Media, Poor Democracy: Communication Politics in Dubious Times* (University of Illinois Press, 1999) and his broader corpus on the political economy of communication supply the foundational political-economic apparatus on the structural conditions of contemporary American media. McChesney's analytical contribution is the demonstration that the contemporary American media configuration has been substantially constructed through specific policy choices — the Telecommunications Act of 1996, the broader regulatory-architectural decisions the FCC has made across decades, the policy choices regarding public-service media funding, the broader policy-architectural decisions on media ownership and concentration — and that the structural-political-economic case for the architectural reconstruction of the American media system operates through the engagement with the political-economic configuration McChesney's apparatus articulates.

Victor Pickard's *Democracy Without Journalism? Confronting the Misinformation Society* (Oxford University Press, 2019) supplies the contemporary articulation of the collapse of the commercial journalism model and the case for public alternatives. Pickard's analytical contribution is the demonstration that the contemporary American commercial-journalism configuration has been substantially undermined by the contemporary digital-platform configuration's capture of advertising revenue, that the contemporary collapse of local journalism has produced systematic information-environment failures that the contemporary policy literature has documented, and that the structural-political-economic case for substantial public investment in journalism through public-service media, community-media support, and broader public-investment architectures operates through the engagement with the empirical record of commercial-journalism collapse. The integration with the e-democracy architecture is direct: the e-democracy architecture's communicative substrate requires the cultivation of journalism-as-cultivation-work that operates outside the commercial-journalism configuration, with public-investment architectures supporting the cultivation.

### 6.5 Public-service media

The public-service-media tradition operates across multiple democratic societies as the institutional architecture of media that operates outside the commercial-market configuration. The British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC), established by Royal Charter in 1922 and operating under the contemporary BBC Charter (renewed 2017, effective 2017-2027), constitutes the foundational instance of public-service media. The BBC's institutional architecture includes funding through the license fee (the per-household charge that supports the BBC's operations outside the commercial-advertising configuration), the structural-political-economic commitments to editorial independence under the Charter's terms, and the broader institutional commitments to the public-service media mission articulated through the Reithian principles ("inform, educate, entertain") and the contemporary public-service media literature.

The comparative public-service media corpus across multiple democratic societies — the Public Broadcasting Service (PBS) and National Public Radio (NPR) in the United States, ARD and ZDF in Germany, France Télévisions and Radio France in France, NHK in Japan, ABC and SBS in Australia, CBC and Radio-Canada in Canada, RAI in Italy, RTVE in Spain, and the broader international public-service-media configuration — supplies the institutional record across decades of operational experience. The contemporary comparative public-service-media scholarship including Karol Jakubowicz's *Public Service Broadcasting in Comparative Perspective* (multiple contributions), the broader European Broadcasting Union research, and the international comparative analysis supplies the analytical apparatus on what features of public-service-media institutional architecture have produced durable democratic-deliberative function and what features have produced operational difficulties.

The integration with the e-democracy architecture operates through the cultivation of public-service-media institutional architecture as one of the communicative-cultivation layers the e-democracy architecture depends on. The structural-political-economic commitments to public-investment funding, editorial independence, and the broader public-service mission operate as cultivation conditions for the journalism-as-cultivation-work the e-democracy architecture requires.

### 6.6 Community media

The institutional architecture of community media — the community-radio configurations across multiple Latin American and African contexts, the community-television configurations across multiple urban contexts in multiple jurisdictions, the contemporary community-media digital configurations — operates as cultivation infrastructure for the local-community deliberative practice the broader e-democracy architecture depends on.

The integration with the e-democracy architecture operates through the cultivation of community-media institutional architecture as one of the communicative-cultivation layers the architecture depends on, distinct from the public-service-media and commercial-media configurations.

The alternative-media tradition operates as the institutional architecture of media that operates outside both the commercial-media and the public-service-media configurations, with substantive editorial commitments to perspectives that the dominant media configurations have systematically underrepresented. *Democracy Now!*, established by Amy Goodman in 1996 and operating across more than fourteen hundred public-radio and public-television stations in the contemporary United States and internationally, constitutes the foundational instance of contemporary American alternative-media operation. *The Real News Network*, established by Paul Jay and operating from 2007 forward, supplies the parallel video-and-digital alternative-media operation. *The Intercept*, established by Glenn Greenwald, Laura Poitras, and Jeremy Scahill in 2014 in the wake of the Edward Snowden disclosures, supplies the contemporary investigative-alternative-media operation. *Jacobin* magazine, founded by Bhaskar Sunkara in 2010, supplies the contemporary American socialist-political alternative-media operation; *Current Affairs*, founded by Nathan J. ### 6.9 Indigenous-media tradition

The Indigenous-media tradition operates as the institutional architecture of media produced by and for Indigenous communities, with the broader content of structural-political-cultural commitments to Indigenous sovereignty, Indigenous-language preservation, and the broader cultivation of Indigenous communicative-cultural practice.
