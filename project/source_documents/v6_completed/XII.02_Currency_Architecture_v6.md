## PART ONE — THE CRITIQUE OF UNIVERSAL FUNGIBILITY

### 1.1 The universal-fungibility regime

The contemporary American currency architecture operates as a universal-fungibility regime: a single monetary medium — the dollar — treated as the universal medium of exchange across all domains of social and economic life. Under the regime, the dollar that purchases a luxury automobile also purchases healthcare, education, housing, political-participatory access, environmental commons, public-research outputs, and the broader content of public-good provision; access to these goods is determined principally through the distribution of private purchasing power; and the structural-architectural arrangement has been treated, across the broader political-economic and constitutional-doctrinal apparatus, as the natural and unavoidable condition of contemporary economic life.

The argument against the regime runs through five analytical moves. The first is that the universal-fungibility regime is not the natural and unavoidable condition of contemporary economic life but a specific historical-political-architectural configuration constructed through particular institutional, doctrinal, and political-economic developments and contested across multiple analytical traditions. The second is the engagement with the political-philosophical tradition on sacred values, incommensurability, and the moral limits of markets — Tetlock and Atran on sacred values, Sandel on the moral limits of markets, Anderson on value pluralism and the corruption thesis, Radin on contested commodities, Satz on noxious markets, Polanyi on the embedded economy and the commodity fictions — that articulates the moral-political distinctions the universal-fungibility regime erases. The third is the engagement with the Indigenous articulation of the sacred-versus-commodified distinction at land, ecological commons, and Indigenous economic philosophy that the multi-eyed-seeing commitment `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6` requires. The fourth is the engagement with the historical record of the universal-fungibility regime's operation under chattel slavery, articulated through Saidiya Hartman's *Scenes of Subjection* (1997) and Walter Johnson's *River of Dark Dreams* (2013), as the constitutive case against the universal-fungibility commitment rather than illustration of its excess. The fifth is the architectural transition from diagnostic recognition to the architectural separation of public-good provision from the universal-fungibility regime through the unidirectional dual-currency architecture Part Four develops.

### 1.2 Sacred values, incommensurability, and the moral limits of markets

`VIII.01_Minds_Markets_Sacred_v6` developed the analytical-philosophical apparatus on sacred values and incommensurability at length; the present section engages the contributions the apparatus supplies for the architectural move from incommensurability recognition to architectural separation.

Philip Tetlock and Scott Atran's empirical research on sacred values demonstrates that human cognitive engagement with moral commitments includes a structurally distinct category — sacred values — whose content is treated by the subjects engaging them as not commensurable with monetary or other instrumental tradeoffs. The empirical research has documented systematic patterns through which the proposal of monetary or instrumental tradeoffs against sacred values produces specific psychological and political responses — moral outrage, the perception of taboo violation, structural-political resistance to the proposed tradeoff — whose content the political-philosophical literature has engaged as the empirical basis for the analytical-philosophical case on incommensurability. The architectural implication is that the universal-fungibility regime requires the structural-political treatment of moral commitments as commensurable with monetary tradeoffs across the domains of social and economic life, and that the empirical content of sacred-values research articulates the psychological and political-architectural inadequacy of this treatment.

Michael Sandel's *What Money Can't Buy: The Moral Limits of Markets* (2012) articulates the contemporary political-philosophical case against the universal-fungibility regime. Sandel's contribution is the demonstration that the penetration of market mechanisms into domains of social and political life — healthcare, education, citizenship, civic obligation, public goods, political-participatory access — produces specific structural-political consequences that cannot be adequately analyzed through the standard welfare-economic apparatus alone. The consequences include the corruption of the non-market values the domains have constituted (the corruption thesis), the structural-political reproduction of inequality through the monetization of access (the inequality thesis), and the broader transformation of the political-economic configuration through the penetration of market mechanisms. Sandel's case articulates the political-philosophical foundation for the architectural separation: the architectural commitment that healthcare, education, housing, environmental commons, democratic-participation infrastructure, and the broader content of public-good provision shall not be determined through the universal-fungibility regime's monetary-purchasing-power-distribution mechanism is the constitutional response to Sandel's analytical case.

Elizabeth Anderson's *Value in Ethics and Economics* (1993) supplies the apparatus on value pluralism and the corruption thesis. Anderson's contribution is the demonstration that human evaluative engagement operates through plural modes of valuation — use, respect, love, appreciation, consideration — whose content is not adequately specified through the single mode of market-monetary valuation alone, and that the treatment of goods whose appropriate evaluative mode is non-monetary as if their appropriate mode is monetary produces specific corruptions of evaluative engagement. The architectural implication is that the universal-fungibility regime requires the structural-political treatment of plural-evaluative goods as if their appropriate mode is uniformly monetary, and that the corruption Anderson articulates is the structural-political consequence of the regime.

Margaret Radin's *Contested Commodities* (1996) supplies the apparatus on the personhood-property distinction. Radin's contribution is the demonstration that the content of certain goods — bodily integrity, sexual intimacy, reproductive capacity, parental relationships, the content of personhood-constitutive goods — is constitutively bound up with the personhood of the persons engaging them, and that the commodification of personhood-constitutive goods produces specific structural-political consequences. Debra Satz's *Why Some Things Should Not Be for Sale: The Moral Limits of Markets* (2010) supplies the apparatus on noxious markets — markets whose operation produces specific structural-political consequences including extreme harm to participants, extreme vulnerability of participants, weak agency of participants, or extreme and unequal impacts on third parties — and articulates the political-philosophical case for the structural-political constraint of such markets' operation. Karl Polanyi's *The Great Transformation: The Political and Economic Origins of Our Time* (1944) supplies the analytical-historical apparatus on the embedded economy and the commodity fictions. Polanyi's contribution is the demonstration that the emergence of the modern market economy required the treatment of land, labor, and money as commodities — the treatment of three things whose content is not commodity-form but whose treatment as commodity-form has been constitutive of the modern market-economic configuration — and that the commodity fictions articulate the structural-political conditions under which the universal-fungibility regime operates.

The integration of Tetlock and Atran, Sandel, Anderson, Radin, Satz, and Polanyi articulates the political-philosophical foundation for the architectural move: the recognition that the universal-fungibility regime is inadequate to the evaluative, personhood-constitutive, and political-philosophical content of the goods whose provision it currently organizes, and that the architectural response is the separation of public-good provision from the universal-fungibility regime through the architectural specification Part Four develops.

### 1.3 The Indigenous articulation: the sacred and the commodified

### 1.4 The historical record: universal fungibility under chattel slavery

Saidiya Hartman's *Scenes of Subjection: Terror, Slavery, and Self-Making in Nineteenth-Century America* (1997) and Walter Johnson's *River of Dark Dreams: Slavery and Empire in the Cotton Kingdom* (2013) supply the analytical-historical apparatus on the historical record of the universal-fungibility regime's operation under chattel slavery. The contribution of these works is consequential: the historical record is not the illustration of the universal-fungibility regime's excess but the constitutive case against the universal-fungibility commitment.

The argument runs as follows. The contemporary universal-fungibility regime is a specific historical-political-architectural configuration constructed through particular institutional, doctrinal, and political-economic developments. The content of these developments includes the structural-political configuration of chattel slavery in the American context — the treatment of human persons as commodity-form across the domains of plantation economy, financial accumulation, and political-economic constitution — and the content of the configuration is constitutive of the regime rather than aberration from it. The historical record of the universal-fungibility regime's operation under chattel slavery is therefore not the illustration of how the regime's excess produces harmful outcomes; it is the evidence that the regime has been constituted through the treatment of human persons as commodity-form, and that contemporary universal fungibility carries the historical-political inheritance of the chattel-slavery configuration.

Hartman's contribution operates through the demonstration that the content of chattel slavery's operation engaged the contradictions of the universal-fungibility regime in directions that the standard political-economic and constitutional-doctrinal apparatus has systematically erased. The slave's legal-architectural status — the constitution of the enslaved person as property whose content is appropriate object of monetary purchase, sale, mortgage, insurance, inheritance, and broader commodity-form treatment — articulated the operative content of the universal-fungibility regime under conditions in which the object of fungibility was the human person. The content of the slave's resistance to the legal-architectural apparatus — the resistance itself, the structural-political conditions under which the resistance operated, the contemporary scholarly apparatus through which Hartman's work engages the resistance — articulates the political-philosophical case that the content of human persons is constitutively non-commodity content whose treatment under the commodity-form apparatus produces the structural-political harms the historical record documents.

Walter Johnson's *River of Dark Dreams* extends the analytical-historical case through the demonstration that the contemporary American capitalist political-economic configuration was constituted through the cotton-kingdom plantation economy and that the configuration cannot be adequately analyzed without engagement with the chattel-slavery configuration's operation. Johnson's contribution is the demonstration that contemporary financial architecture, contemporary commodity-form treatment of economic goods, and the political-economic configuration the universal-fungibility regime operates within were constituted through the chattel-slavery configuration. The contemporary inheritance the configuration carries is therefore not an external residue that the contemporary configuration could shed through marginal reform; it is the constitutive content of the contemporary configuration, and the architectural response requires the structural-architectural reconstruction the universal-fungibility regime's transformation supports.

The integration of Hartman's and Johnson's apparatus with the broader cross-traditional analytical apparatus articulates the constitutive case against the universal-fungibility commitment. The case is consequential for the architectural reconstruction: the architectural separation of public-good provision from the universal-fungibility regime through the unidirectional dual-currency architecture Part Four specifies is the constitutional response to the historical-political record, integrated with `XII.04_Intergenerational_Rectification_v6`'s multi-generational rectification architecture, with `XII.05_Neurodivergent_Polis_v6`'s neurodivergent commitments, with the Part II caste, oligarchy, racial, and embodiment-axis diagnostics, and with the broader project's constitutional-architectural reconstruction.

### 1.5 The multi-traditional engagement at primary-articulator standing

The political-philosophical tradition the previous sections engaged stands within the Western analytical apparatus. Part Six develops the multi-traditional engagement at length; the present section names the traditions and their critical contribution to the diagnostic apparatus Part One develops.

Buddhist economics, articulated principally by E. F. Schumacher in *Small Is Beautiful: Economics as if People Mattered* (1973) — the foundational Western articulation of Buddhist-economic principles — and developed across the subsequent corpus including David Loy's *A Buddhist History of the West: Studies in Lack* (2002) and *Money, Sex, War, Karma: Notes for a Buddhist Revolution* (2008), Sulak Sivaraksa's *The Wisdom of Sustainability: Buddhist Economics for the 21st Century* (2009), and the broader International Network of Engaged Buddhists (INEB) documentation, supplies a critical apparatus on the universal-fungibility regime that operates from a different ontological substrate than the Western political-philosophical tradition. Schumacher's articulation of Buddhist economics specifies that economic activity is not appropriately read through the maximization of consumption as primary good but through the cultivation of human flourishing under conditions of sufficiency, that the right livelihood specifies a substantive standard for economic activity that the universal-fungibility regime's content evaluates inadequately, and that the institutional architecture of economic life should support the cultivation of right livelihood rather than the maximization of accumulation. Loy's analytical extension specifies the structural-psychological content of the universal-fungibility regime's operation as the institutionalization of *taṇhā* (craving, thirst) as the operative principle of economic life, with the resulting structural-political-economic consequences the contemporary Buddhist economics literature has engaged. Sivaraksa's extension specifies the structural-political-economic content of the universal-fungibility regime as inconsistent with the cultivation of *kalyāṇa-mitra* (spiritual friendship) and *sangha* (community) under conditions of contemporary global political economy. The Buddhist-economics critique stands at primary-articulator standing: the universal-fungibility regime is read through the Buddhist apparatus on the operation of *taṇhā*, the cultivation of right livelihood, and the institutional conditions for human flourishing under conditions of sufficiency.

The Islamic *waqf-and-zakat* tradition supplies a parallel critical apparatus from a different ontological substrate. Wael Hallaq's *The Impossible State: Islam, Politics, and Modernity's Moral Predicament* (2013) articulates the structural-political-economic incompatibility between the inherited Islamic moral-political tradition and the contemporary nation-state-and-market-economy configuration; the analytical case engages the universal-fungibility regime as a specific manifestation of the modernist political-economic configuration whose content the Islamic moral-political tradition contests at the constitutive level. Murat Çizakça's *A History of Philanthropic Foundations: The Islamic World from the Seventh Century to the Present* (2000) supplies the historical-institutional record of the *waqf* tradition — the Islamic charitable endowment institution that has operated across Islamic civilization for more than a millennium as the institutional architecture for the provision of public goods (mosques, madrasas, hospitals, hospices, water systems, urban infrastructure) outside the market-economic configuration. The *waqf* tradition's structural-architectural significance for the present argument is direct: the tradition demonstrates that the institutional separation of public-good provision from the market-economic configuration has been operationally implemented across multiple Islamic civilizations across more than a millennium, that the institutional architecture of the separation includes specific design features (perpetual endowment, designated trustees, specified beneficiary classes, juridical protection against subsequent alienation) that the contemporary architectural reconstruction can engage as cultivation-condition vocabulary, and that the cultivation work of the unidirectional dual-currency architecture operates within a much longer cross-civilizational tradition of institutional architectures separating public-good provision from market-economic operation. The *zakat* institution — the obligatory wealth transfer constituting one of the five pillars of Islamic practice — articulates a parallel structural-political-economic commitment: the structural-political-economic configuration of Islamic economic life requires the systematic transfer of wealth from accumulation to specified categories of need (the poor, the indebted, travelers, those in bondage), with the institutional architecture operating across multiple Islamic juridical schools and across multiple Islamic civilizations.

Timur Kuran's *The Long Divergence: How Islamic Law Held Back the Middle East* (2011) engages the *waqf* tradition with a critical-historical-economic apparatus that articulates the operational limitations the tradition encountered under the conditions of long-run economic development. Kuran's analytical case is that the *waqf* tradition's institutional features — the perpetuity commitment, the restrictions on subsequent reorganization of the endowment, the juridical apparatus surrounding the designated beneficiary classes — produced operational limitations under conditions of long-run economic transformation that contributed to the divergence between Middle Eastern and European economic trajectories from the early modern period forward. The critical engagement is consequential for the present argument's institutional design: the *waqf* tradition supplies cultivation-condition vocabulary the unidirectional dual-currency architecture engages, but the cultivation-condition design must address the operational limitations Kuran's analytical apparatus identifies — the architecture must support the institutional capacity for adaptive cultivation across multi-generational time horizons without producing the perpetuity-rigidities the historical *waqf* tradition encountered.

The integration of the Buddhist, Islamic, and reciprocity traditions Part Six engages with the Western political-philosophical tradition Sections 1.2 through 1.4 engaged articulates the multi-traditional foundation for the architectural move. The recognition that the universal-fungibility regime is structurally inadequate is not the contribution of any single tradition; it is the cross-traditional analytical convergence that the multi-eyed-seeing methodology surfaces.

## PART TWO — PUBLIC MONEY: THE HETERODOX MONETARY TRADITION

### 2.1 The architectural move

The architectural move from the diagnostic case against universal fungibility to the architectural specification of the unidirectional dual-currency architecture engages the heterodox monetary tradition's analytical apparatus on public money. The contemporary heterodox monetary tradition includes Modern Monetary Theory (the operational reality of sovereign currency), the sovereign-money tradition (reform of issuance authority), the public-banking tradition (Brown), the Minsky-Mehrling financial-stability tradition (Hyman Minsky on financial instability and Perry Mehrling on the money view), and Mariana Mazzucato on the entrepreneurial state and the value of everything. The traditions supply the Western analytical foundation for the public-currency channel; the integration with the multi-traditional canon Part Six engages supplies the broader cross-civilizational foundation.

### 2.2 Modern Monetary Theory: the operational reality of sovereign currency

Modern Monetary Theory, articulated principally by Warren Mosler, L. Randall Wray, Stephanie Kelton, Pavlina Tcherneva, and the broader MMT scholarly community, supplies the operational analysis of sovereign-currency operation that the public-currency channel requires. Stephanie Kelton's *The Deficit Myth: Modern Monetary Theory and the Birth of the People's Economy* (2020) supplies the contemporary articulation accessible to non-specialist readers; L. Randall Wray's *Modern Money Theory: A Primer on Macroeconomics for Sovereign Monetary Systems* (2012, second edition 2015) supplies the academic articulation. The contribution of MMT for the present argument is the operational analysis: sovereign-currency issuance is not constrained by the prior-taxation-or-debt-issuance constraint the contemporary fiscal-policy narrative articulates; the constraint on issuance is the availability of real resources (labor, materials, infrastructure, institutional capacity) for the activity the issuance funds, and the inflationary pressure that issuance in conditions of resource constraint produces.

The operational analysis is consequential for the present argument because it articulates the macroeconomic conditions under which the public-currency channel operates. The public-currency channel's provision of public goods is not constrained by prior taxation or debt issuance; it is constrained by the availability of real resources for the public-good provision the channel funds. The architectural commitments the document advances on healthcare, education, housing-as-right, environmental commons, democratic-participation infrastructure, public-research outputs, and public-employment provision operate under the operational conditions MMT articulates: the resource-availability question is the binding constraint, not the prior-taxation-or-debt-issuance constraint, and the architectural design addresses the resource-availability question directly.

Pavlina Tcherneva's *The Case for a Job Guarantee* (2020) articulates the job-guarantee architecture as constitutive of the macroeconomic operation under the public-currency channel: the systematic availability of public-employment positions at a living wage operates as macroeconomic stabilizer (replacing the contemporary use of unemployment as inflation-management mechanism), as automatic resource-mobilization mechanism for the public-good provision the public-currency channel funds, and as structural-political-economic commitment to the equal moral and legal standing of all persons against the contemporary configuration in which involuntary unemployment functions as systematic structural-political-economic subordination.

### 2.3 The sovereign-money tradition: reform of issuance authority

The sovereign-money tradition — articulated through Joseph Huber's *Sovereign Money: Beyond Reserve Banking* (2017), through the Positive Money organization's policy work, and through the historical-analytical apparatus including Irving Fisher's *100% Money* (1935) and the Chicago Plan literature — supplies a parallel but distinct analytical apparatus on monetary architecture. The sovereign-money tradition's contribution is the proposal that monetary issuance authority be vested in the sovereign monetary authority rather than distributed across the private commercial-banking apparatus through the contemporary fractional-reserve mechanism. The proposal addresses what the sovereign-money tradition identifies as the structural-political-economic concern of private money creation: the contemporary configuration in which commercial banks create the majority of money supply through lending operations produces structural-political-economic outcomes whose content includes the misallocation of investment capital toward asset-price inflation (housing, financial assets) rather than productive investment, the structural concentration of financial-sector power that the sovereign-money tradition identifies as constitutive of contemporary inequality, and the broader content of structural-political-economic concerns the tradition engages.

The integration with the present argument is partial. The unidirectional dual-currency architecture engages the sovereign-money tradition's analytical apparatus on the structural-political-economic content of monetary issuance authority; the architectural specification differs from the sovereign-money tradition's reform-of-issuance-authority commitment within a single currency because the dual-currency architecture operates through the structural separation of two currency channels rather than through the reform of issuance authority within a single currency configuration.

### 2.4 The public-banking tradition

Ellen Brown's *Web of Debt: The Shocking Truth About Our Money System and How We Can Break Free* (2007) and *The Public Bank Solution: From Austerity to Prosperity* (2013), and the Public Banking Institute's broader policy work, articulate the public-banking tradition's case for the establishment of public-banking institutions at federal, state, and local levels as the structural-architectural reconstruction of the contemporary banking configuration. The tradition's contribution is the operational analysis of public-banking institutions: the Bank of North Dakota (established 1919) operates as the long-running American instance of state-level public banking, and the international record including German public-banking institutions (Sparkassen and Landesbanken), the Bank of Japan's developmental finance operations, and the broader international public-banking record supplies the operational-empirical apparatus on the operational reality of public banking. The integration with the unidirectional dual-currency architecture operates through the public-banking institutions' role in the operational architecture of the public-currency channel: the public-currency channel's distribution and operational architecture operates through public-banking institutions at multiple jurisdictional levels.

### 2.5 The Minsky-Mehrling financial-stability tradition

Hyman Minsky's *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986, reissued 2008) and his broader corpus on financial-instability hypothesis, and Perry Mehrling's *The New Lombard Street: How the Fed Became the Dealer of Last Resort* (2011) and *Money and Empire: Charles P. Kindleberger and the Dollar System* (2022), supply the financial-stability analytical apparatus the unidirectional dual-currency architecture engages. Minsky's contribution is the analytical case that financial instability is the structural-political-economic consequence of the contemporary financial configuration rather than the result of exogenous shocks: the configuration produces the conditions under which speculative finance gives way to Ponzi finance and the eventual collapse Minsky's analytical apparatus articulates. Mehrling's contribution is the money-view analytical apparatus on the operational reality of contemporary monetary operations, including the central-bank-as-dealer-of-last-resort framework and the broader money-view analytical apparatus on the operational reality of contemporary monetary operations. The integration with the present argument operates through the financial-stability architectural commitments the unidirectional dual-currency configuration engages: the structural separation of the public-currency channel from the private-currency channel addresses the structural-political-economic content of contemporary financial instability through the architectural separation of public-good provision from the speculative-financial operations the financial-instability hypothesis identifies as constitutive of contemporary financial-economy operation.
