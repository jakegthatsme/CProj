## Thread B — Publicly Funded Research Returns as Bounded Decision Right

The publicly funded research returns right is specified as a bounded decision right with the four-element format. The right operates over the disposition of outputs — data, intellectual property, methodology, publications, materials — from publicly funded research at habitat-and-population scales, specifying that outputs return to public substrate rather than to private appropriation.

**Scope.** The right operates over the disposition of outputs from publicly funded research. The scope includes data (research datasets, observational records, experimental protocols), intellectual property (patents, copyrights, trademarks, and the broader intellectual-property substrate), methodology (research methods, protocols, computational tools, materials and reagents), and publications (the published research record itself). The scope specifies that publicly funded research outputs return to public substrate; the public funding establishes the public-substrate disposition as foundational architectural commitment rather than as supplementary licensing question. The scope is bounded: the right does not specify the content of any particular research output, nor the content of any particular research-funding decision, nor the operational architecture by which research is conducted. The right specifies the disposition commitment.

**Scale.** Habitat-and-population, where public-research funding operates and where the research-output disposition affects community-and-locality cultivation. Public research funding at the habitat-and-population scale band — federal research funding through agencies like the National Institutes of Health, the National Science Foundation, the Department of Energy, and the broader public-research-funding apparatus; state and municipal research funding; the broader public-research-funding architecture — is subject to the disposition commitment the right establishes. The cross-scale coupling specifies that research outputs at finer scales (organism-and-organ-system scale Document XII.12 operates at) are conditioned by the right's specification, and that research outputs at biospheric scale (the broader research-architecture-as-foundational-commitment register) are conditioned by Document XII.09's foundational architecture.

**Feedback condition.** The disposition-of-outputs regime operates through disclosure, license, and public-access architecture. The Bayh-Dole Act of 1980 (35 U.S.C. § 200 et seq.) operates as the contemporary American statutory architecture governing the disposition of intellectual property from federally funded research; the act establishes that recipient institutions may retain title to inventions made with federal funding, subject to specified conditions. The contemporary scholarly literature engages the act critically: Robin Feldman's "Patent and Antitrust: Differing Shades of Meaning" (*Virginia Journal of Law and Technology* 13, 2008) and her broader work on the patent-and-pharmaceutical-pricing terrain articulates the substantive ways the Bayh-Dole architecture has produced public-substrate-erosion outcomes that the act's original architectural intent did not anticipate. Janet Freilich's analytical work extends the critique into the broader intellectual-property-and-public-funding terrain. The right's feedback condition specifies that the disposition architecture is monitored continuously, with the Bayh-Dole architecture and its successors subject to ongoing review against the public-substrate-disposition commitment the right establishes.

The open-science movement has begun to articulate the architectural commitments public-substrate disposition requires. Michael Nielsen's *Reinventing Discovery: The New Era of Networked Science* (Princeton University Press, 2011) articulates the analytical apparatus for networked science as collective public-substrate enterprise — open data, open methodology, open publications, open materials, and the collective infrastructure that conditions the disposition. Yochai Benkler's *The Wealth of Networks: How Social Production Transforms Markets and Freedom* (Yale University Press, 2006) articulates the broader commons-based-peer-production tradition, with implications across the publicly funded research terrain. The contemporary open-science movement — the Plan S coalition (the European-led commitment to open-access publishing of publicly funded research), the COAR (Confederation of Open Access Repositories) infrastructure, the broader institutional and disciplinary archives — carries the contemporary practice of public-substrate disposition. The right's feedback condition incorporates the open-science architecture as substantive content, with the public-substrate-disposition specifications drafted through the open-science-architecture commitments.

**Sunset-or-adaptation condition.** The disposition regime is subject to scheduled review under the eco-mimetic legislative-cycle framework. The review cycle is matched to the dynamics the disposition-regime operates within — the technological substrate of research evolves at fast cycles, requiring rapid-adaptation review conditions for the technical disposition architecture; the institutional substrate evolves at slower cycles, requiring multi-year review conditions for the institutional disposition commitments. The sunset-or-adaptation condition specifies that the disposition regime is not engineered-fixture statutory architecture but cultivation-condition architecture under continuous adaptive review.

*Right-to-food.* The right operates over adequate food provisioning, coordinated with the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights Article 11 (1966) and the FAO Right to Food framework (the 2004 Voluntary Guidelines to Support the Progressive Realization of the Right to Adequate Food in the Context of National Food Security). ## Thread D — Sexuality, Gender, and Reproduction: The Material Conditions of Reproductive Autonomy as a Provisioning Decision Right (Sixth-Pass Addition)

Document XII.09 specifies bodily and reproductive autonomy as foundational decision rights at biospheric scale. Document XII.10 specifies scale-matched public check on decisions bearing on bodily, reproductive, and gender autonomy at ecosystem-and-habitat scale, with administrative violence as paradigm terrain. The present document specifies the material conditions of reproductive autonomy as provisioning decision right at habitat-and-population scale — the material provisioning the autonomy depends on, without which the foundational commitment and the public-check architecture cannot operate as cultivation conditions.

The reproductive-justice movement, through Loretta J. Ross and Rickie Solinger's *Reproductive Justice: An Introduction* (University of California Press, 2017) and the broader SisterSong reproductive-justice corpus, supplies the foundational analytical apparatus on reproductive autonomy as provisioning question rather than as negative-rights question. The reproductive-justice framing — articulated initially by Black women organizers in the 1990s and codified through SisterSong in 1997 — specifies three principal architectural commitments: the right to have children (which requires material conditions for safe pregnancy, birth, and postnatal care; for prenatal nutrition and healthcare; for environmental conditions that do not produce reproductive harm; for economic conditions sufficient to support childbearing); the right not to have children (which requires material conditions of access to contraception, abortion, and the broader reproductive-health infrastructure); and the right to raise children in safety and dignity (which requires material conditions of housing, food, education, healthcare, community-safety, environmental-safety, and the broader infrastructure within which child-raising is conducted). The reproductive-justice framing was articulated precisely against the reproductive-rights framing that secured the negative right while leaving the material conditions unprovided; the architectural implication is that reproductive autonomy at habitat-and-population scale cannot be specified as the negative-rights framing has articulated it, but must be specified as provisioning architecture.

Adrienne Rich's *Of Woman Born: Motherhood as Experience and Institution* (Norton, 1976) supplies the foundational analytical articulation of motherhood as institution distinguishable from the experience the institution presupposes and partially constitutes. Rich's analytical work specifies that the institution of motherhood operates under architectural commitments that condition the content of motherhood as experience; the institutional architecture conditions whether the experience of mothering operates under cultivation-discipline conditions or under coerced-extraction conditions. The architectural implication for the provisioning right is that the material conditions of mothering — economic conditions, healthcare conditions, community-support conditions, the broader infrastructure within which mothering is conducted — are themselves constitutive of the content of the right, not external supports attached to a more fundamental autonomy.

Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Sexual Politics: African Americans, Gender, and the New Racism* (Routledge, 2004) carries the analytical work into the racialized provision and denial of reproductive support. Collins's analytical work specifies that the contemporary American architecture has provided reproductive support unequally across racial configurations, with Black women and women of color carrying the substantial historical record of reproductive support being denied through architectural commitments operating from the chattel-slavery configuration forward — the architecture of involuntary sterilization across the twentieth century (the eugenics movement and its codification in state-level forced-sterilization programs; the contemporary record of involuntary sterilization in immigration detention and in the broader carceral apparatus); the architecture of differential maternal-and-infant mortality across racial configurations; the architecture of differential reproductive-healthcare access; the broader architectural record of reproductive-provisioning inequality. The architectural implication for the provisioning right at habitat-and-population scale is that the right must be specified with explicit engagement with the racialized-provisioning record, rather than under the assumption of formally-equal provisioning that the dominant constitutional discourse has often presupposed.

Eli Clare's *Brilliant Imperfection: Grappling with Cure* (Duke University Press, 2017) carries the analytical work into the disability-justice dimension of reproductive provisioning and the history of reproductive coercion against disabled people. Clare's analytical work specifies that the disability-justice tradition has carried the substantial historical record of reproductive coercion against disabled people — the eugenics movement's specific architectural commitments against disabled reproduction, the contemporary architecture of reproductive coercion in institutional settings, the broader architectural record of reproductive-provisioning denial across disability configurations. The architectural implication for the provisioning right is that the right must be specified with explicit engagement with the disability-justice record, with disability-justice articulations at primary-articulator standing for the analytical work on reproductive provisioning across disability configurations.
