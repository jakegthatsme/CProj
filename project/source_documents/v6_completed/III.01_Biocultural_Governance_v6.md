# III.01 — Biocultural Governance: Constitutional Ontology and the Nested-Scales Reference Frame
## Part III — Constitutional Ontology: Biocultural Governance

*Fifth-pass designation: Document M. This document is the sole document of Part III and the load-bearing ontology document of the sixth-pass constitutional project's entire arc.*

*A document in the sixth-pass constitutional project, articulating biocultural governance as the constitutional ontology against which the rest of the arc reads the diagnostic gap, specifies the material substrate, and proposes the cultivation-protocol architecture. The document grounds the ontology in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights literatures of Luisa Maffi, Fikret Berkes, Darrell Posey, Kabir Bavikatte, and Daniel Robinson; engages eight primary-articulator traditions at the standing the project's load-bearing commitment specifies — the Haudenosaunee Great Law tradition, the broader Indigenous-ecological tradition of Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi), Tyson Yunkaporta (Apalech Clan), Daniel Wildcat (Yuchi), and Kyle Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi), the Daoist relational ontology of the* Daodejing *and* Zhuangzi*, the Buddhist dependent-origination tradition of Nāgārjuna's* Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, the African Ubuntu tradition of Mogobe Ramose and the* Makwanyane *jurisprudence, the Andean ayllu and ayni traditions and their Ecuadorian and Bolivian constitutional incorporation, the Pacific Islander Talanoa and Vā and Maori Whakapapa traditions and their articulation in* Matike Mai Aotearoa, *and the Black-radical ecological thought of Octavia Butler, adrienne maree brown, and the Black-agrarian corpus; engages the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, regenerative-design, and disturbance-ecology canon as recent and partial recovery of framings the primary-articulator traditions have long carried; and on that foundation specifies three apex analytical commitments: the case against design-imposition framings in constitutional theory, the nested-scales reference frame as constitutional category, and the renovation-versus-decolonial question read under the cultivation-rather-than-design-renovation framing. The document is the load-bearing ontology document of the entire arc; what it specifies, the rest of the arc operationalizes.*

*The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad. **Biocultural governance** as constitutional ontology — the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a biocultural formation, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled at constitutive depth rather than at the level of correlation, grounded in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights literatures. **Eco-mimetic policy-making** as operational method — ecological, complex-adaptive-systems-theoretic, biomimetic, and disturbance-ecology concepts inspire how laws are passed, written, and tested at constitutional and statutory scale. **Disturbance-adapted implementation** as temporal orientation — intergenerational architecture cultivated to absorb, metabolize, and recover from a plural disturbance landscape comprising five named regimes (cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic) and their interactions. The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the principle's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery of framings these traditions have long specified. "Grown, not built" is the project's colloquial summary. This document articulates the first element of the triad at scholarly analytical depth and provides the ontological grounding the other two elements presuppose.*

The document proceeds in five movements. The first grounds the ontology in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights scholarship that supplies its foundational vocabulary and its legal-and-normative framework. The second engages eight primary-articulator traditions at the standing they require — not as comparative supplement to a Western theoretical apparatus, not as data points for a synthesis, not collapsed into a single biocultural-governance ontological vocabulary, but in their own analytical registers, with each tradition's apparatus engaged and the content each articulates carried at the constitutional scale the project operates. The third reads the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, regenerative-design, and disturbance-ecology canon as recent and partial recovery — substantive engagement with each principal contributor, naming what each recovers, what each extends, and where each falls short of the primary articulation. The fourth specifies arts, language, and cultural production as a load-bearing element of the biocultural formation. The fifth — the apex of the document and the place where the document's distinctive analytical work is concentrated — specifies the analytical case for biocultural governance at the level of constitutional ontology rather than at the level of policy preference, articulates the nested-scales reference frame as constitutional category, and engages the renovation-versus-decolonial question under the cultivation-rather-than-design-renovation framing as substantive analytical move. A brief closing identifies what the rest of the arc operationalizes from what is specified here.

# Part One — Scholarly Grounding

## The Biocultural-Diversity and Biocultural-Rights Literatures

The proposal that biocultural governance can serve as the constitutional ontology of a polity rests, at the level of scholarly foundation, on two adjacent literatures that have developed across the past three decades and that the project draws on as the load-bearing scholarly inheritance for the constitutional move. The biocultural-diversity literature, opened by Luisa Maffi's edited volume *On Biocultural Diversity: Linking Language, Knowledge, and the Environment* (2001), specified at descriptive-empirical depth that biological and cultural diversity are coupled across the Earth's surface at constitutive levels — that the regions of highest linguistic diversity overlap closely with the regions of highest biological diversity, that the Indigenous and traditional knowledge corpora that have sustained those regions are inseparable from the ecosystems in which they were developed, and that the loss of one is reliably accompanied by the loss of the other. Maffi's introduction to the volume specifies the framing that the biocultural-diversity literature has carried forward: the human species is a biological species whose evolution proceeded under conditions of cultural plurality coupled to ecological specificity, and the species-level inheritance — what genealogically descended human populations have to work with — is biocultural rather than biological-then-cultural-on-top. Each of the volume's twenty-six chapters substantiates the framing across a different terrain: Stephen Wurm on the joint distribution of biological and linguistic diversity; Hugh Possingham on the conservation-biology stakes; Janis Alcorn on the Indigenous-territorial knowledge corpora that sustain biodiversity; Eugene Hunn on the ethnobotanical inheritance; Darrell Posey on the Kayapó. The volume's analytical move was not to argue for an aesthetic appreciation of cultural plurality but to specify that biological and cultural diversity are not separable analytical objects, that the conservation of either depends on the conservation of the other, and that the dominant twentieth-century separation of biological-conservation work from cultural-anthropology work has produced unstable categorial commitments in both literatures.

Fikret Berkes's *Sacred Ecology: Traditional Ecological Knowledge and Resource Management* (Routledge, 1999; fourth edition 2018) extended the framing into an analytical-systematic engagement with the traditional-ecological-knowledge corpora across world regions. Berkes specified four interrelated levels at which traditional ecological knowledge operates: empirical observations of local environments, resource-management practices, social institutions that organize the practices, and worldviews that situate the practices in cosmological and ethical commitments. The four levels are not separable, in Berkes's analytical apparatus; the worldview is what makes the social institutions intelligible, the social institutions are what sustain the management practices, the management practices are what generate the empirical observations, and the empirical observations are what refine the worldview. The traditional-ecological-knowledge corpus is therefore not an inventory of empirical findings that can be extracted from a worldview and incorporated into a science; it is a way of knowing whose elements depend on one another for their analytical integrity. Berkes's specification of the four levels has carried through the broader biocultural-knowledge literature — through the work of Eugene Hunn on ethnobiology, of Madhav Gadgil on traditional resource management in India, of Henry Lewis on Aboriginal fire ecology, of the broader Society of Ethnobiology corpus — and supplies the project with a substantive specification of why biocultural coupling operates at constitutive depth rather than at the level of correlation. The ecosystem, in the biocultural-knowledge specification, includes the cultural apparatus through which a community knows the ecosystem; the ecosystem and the knowing-apparatus are co-constituted, and neither can be analytically isolated from the other without distorting both.

Darrell Posey's *Cultural and Spiritual Values of Biodiversity* (1999), the UNEP-commissioned synthesis volume that Posey edited shortly before his death, extended the analytical framing into the spiritual and ethical registers that the biocultural-knowledge corpora carry. Posey's specification was that the cultural and spiritual values of biodiversity are not ornamentation added to a fundamentally biological-economic object; they are constitutive of why the communities that have sustained biodiversity have done so, of how they have done so, and of what they take the biodiversity to be. The volume gathered contributions from across the world — from Inuit Elder Mary Simon on Arctic traditional knowledge, from Yoruba scholar Olabiyi Babalola Yai on West African biocultural traditions, from Indian scholars on the Indian biocultural inheritance, from Andean scholars on the *Pachamama* framing, from Pacific Islander scholars on the oceanic-relational framing — and substantiated at scale the framing that the world's biocultural inheritance is plural, locally specified, and not subsumable under a single ethical-spiritual register. The decolonial implications of Posey's framing are explicit: the biocultural inheritance cannot be conserved by being extracted from the communities whose practices have sustained it and reformulated in the conservation-science vocabulary of the global North. The communities and the practices are the conservation; without them, the inheritance erodes regardless of how it is described.

Kabir Bavikatte's *Stewarding the Earth: Rethinking Property and the Emergence of Biocultural Rights* (Oxford, 2014) carried the biocultural-knowledge framing into the legal-and-normative register that the project draws on as the proximate scholarly foundation for the constitutional move. Bavikatte's analytical work specified that biocultural rights — the rights of Indigenous peoples and local communities to the integrated stewardship of their territories, knowledge systems, and ways of life — cannot be adequately specified through the standard human-rights apparatus, the standard property-rights apparatus, or the standard environmental-rights apparatus, because each of those apparatuses presupposes analytical separations (between cultural rights and environmental rights, between individual and collective rights, between rights of persons and rights of relationships) that the biocultural-rights framing rejects. Bavikatte's specification proceeded from the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit-Sharing (adopted 2010 under the Convention on Biological Diversity, entered into force 2014) and from the international-legal recognition of community protocols as the operative legal-and-normative instrument through which biocultural rights are articulated and enforced. The community protocol is, in Bavikatte's analytical apparatus, the legal-and-normative form that the biocultural-rights framing authorizes: a community's own specification of the conditions under which its territory, its knowledge, and its way of life can be engaged by others, generated through the community's own deliberative processes and recognized by external parties as the operative legal-and-normative instrument. Daniel Robinson's parallel work on biocultural community protocols (*Confronting Biopiracy*, 2010; later collaborations with Bavikatte on the implementation of the Nagoya Protocol) extended the framing to the implementation record. The ICCA Consortium (the Indigenous Peoples' and Community Conserved Territories and Areas consortium) and the International Indigenous Forum on Biodiversity (IIFB) have documented the implementation of biocultural rights and community protocols across the world's regions and supply the documentary archive against which the analytical framing can be tested.

The analytical move from biocultural diversity (descriptive-empirical) and biocultural heritage (legal-and-normative) to biocultural governance (constitutional) requires specification. The biocultural-diversity literature documents that biological and cultural diversity are coupled. The biocultural-rights literature specifies the legal-and-normative framework through which communities articulate their stewardship of the coupling. Biocultural governance — the project's constitutional move — specifies the polity itself as a biocultural formation at constitutional ontology and the polity's governing architecture as cultivated under conditions appropriate to a biocultural formation rather than imposed under design specifications appropriate to an engineered object. The project does not claim that the biocultural-diversity or biocultural-rights literatures authorize the constitutional move on their own terms; both literatures operate at the level of communities and territories rather than at the level of constitutional polities. The constitutional extension is the project's analytical proposal, grounded in the prior scholarly work and consistent with what that work specifies, but extending the framing into the constitutional register in a way that requires the analytical engagement the rest of this document conducts. The biocultural-rights apparatus that Bavikatte and Robinson specify operates within constitutional polities that have, in most cases, not been constituted on biocultural-governance principles. The project's constitutional move is to ask what a polity would look like that *had* been constituted on biocultural-governance principles, and what the path from the present constitutional implementations to such a polity could plausibly be. The Indigenous and Eastern primary articulators engaged across Threads B through I, and the analytical apex of Threads K, L, and M, supply the content of that ask.

# Part Two — Primary Articulators

The eight threads of Part Two engage the primary-articulator traditions whose work the project carries at the standing the load-bearing commitment specifies. The traditions are engaged in their own analytical registers, with each tradition's apparatus engaged on its own terms and the content each articulates carried at the constitutional scale the project operates. The order proceeds from the Haudenosaunee Great Law — the world's longest continuously practiced eco-mimetic constitutional architecture — through the broader Indigenous-ecological tradition that the Anglophone reader encounters first through Robin Wall Kimmerer and Tyson Yunkaporta, then through the Daoist and Buddhist traditions whose relational ontologies supply the philosophical depth at which biocultural governance operates, then through the African Ubuntu tradition with its constitutional-jurisprudential record in postapartheid South Africa, then through the Andean ayllu and ayni traditions with their constitutional-incorporation record in Ecuador and Bolivia, then through the Pacific Islander and Maori traditions with the *Matike Mai* constitutional-transformation document and the Whanganui legal-personhood case, then to Black-radical ecological thought as the tradition that articulates cultivation-under-disturbance with the analytical force the project's disturbance-adapted-implementation orientation requires. None of these traditions is treated as supplement or as ornament; each articulates substantive content the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries.

## Thread B — The Haudenosaunee Great Law Tradition

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy — the political union of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca peoples, with the Tuscarora joining in the early eighteenth century — operates under the Kaianerekowa, the Great Law of Peace, a constitutional architecture that the oral-historical record dates from somewhere between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries and that has continued to govern Haudenosaunee political life through European contact, removal pressure, allotment, the Indian Reorganization Act, the assimilationist policies of the mid-twentieth century, and the contemporary self-determination period. The Kaianerekowa is therefore not a historical artifact engaged for comparative-constitutional interest; it is an operative constitutional architecture with a continuous practical record longer than any European or post-European constitution. John Mohawk (Seneca; 1945–2006), in *Utopian Legacies: A History of Conquest and Oppression in the Western World* (Clear Light, 2000), specified the analytical framing through which the Haudenosaunee record must be engaged: the Western imagination has consistently treated non-Western constitutional traditions as either pre-political (and therefore in need of European political organization) or as exotic alternatives (and therefore available for selective extraction of useful elements), and both framings prevent engagement with the content the traditions articulate at the constitutional scale. Mohawk's analytical work proceeded from the alternative starting point: the Haudenosaunee constitutional architecture is a substantive constitutional achievement whose substantive content can be engaged on its own terms, and the engagement is what allows the political imagination of the Western reader to be expanded rather than confirmed.

The Kaianerekowa's substantive constitutional content, as engaged by the Haudenosaunee scholarly tradition through Oren Lyons (Onondaga; Faithkeeper of the Onondaga Nation), John Mohawk, Doug George-Kanentiio (Akwesasne Mohawk; *Iroquois On Fire: A Voice from the Mohawk Nation*, Praeger, 2008), and the broader corpus including the contributors to *Exiled in the Land of the Free: Democracy, Indian Nations, and the U.S. Constitution* (Clear Light, 1992), articulates four elements that biocultural governance carries forward at constitutional scale. The first is the seven-generations principle. The Kaianerekowa specifies that decisions of the Confederacy must be made with regard to their effects on the seventh generation hence — that is, with regard to descendants whom the deciders will not meet, in conditions the deciders cannot fully anticipate, on terms that require the deciders to consider the long-term continuance of the conditions under which Haudenosaunee life is possible. The principle is not a heuristic for ethical reflection; it is a procedural commitment of the constitutional architecture, operative in the Great Council's deliberation, and it constitutes a temporal-scale specification of constitutional decision-making that no Western constitutional tradition has adopted. Oren Lyons, addressing the United Nations Working Group on Indigenous Populations in 1977 and subsequently the broader international fora across the following four decades, has specified the principle as substantive constitutional commitment rather than as ethical aspiration: the question at every decision is what the decision will mean for the children's children's children's children's children's children's children, and the question structures the decision rather than ornamenting it.

The second element is the clan-mother and Three Sisters architecture. The Kaianerekowa specifies that the Confederacy's chiefs (Hoyaneh, "the good men" or "the peace chiefs") are nominated by the clan mothers of each clan and confirmed by the broader clan, hold their positions at the will of the clan, and can be removed by the clan mothers if they fail in their responsibilities. The architecture distributes constitutional authority across scales: the household and the clan at the local scale, the nation (the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and later Tuscarora) at the mid-scale, the Confederacy at the encompassing scale, with the Three Sisters (corn, beans, and squash) as the agricultural and symbolic grounding of the architecture at the level of subsistence and meaning. The architecture is a scale-matched decision-rights specification — different decisions are made at different scales by different bodies, with the clan mothers operating at the scale of the clan and the household, the nations operating at the scale of the people, and the Confederacy operating at the scale of the encompassing union. The Three Sisters are not metaphor; they are the agricultural foundation of Haudenosaunee life and the practical-ecological referent through which the constitutional architecture's grounding in subsistence is maintained. The corn provides the structural support for the beans; the beans fix nitrogen for the corn and the squash; the squash provides ground cover that retains moisture and suppresses weeds. The constitutional architecture is, in the analytical apparatus of the tradition, a polycultural architecture in the same way that the Three Sisters garden is a polyculture — each element supplies what the others require, the system is more productive than any element in isolation, and the architecture is sustained by the relationships among its elements rather than by the imposition of a structural blueprint over them.

The third element is the wampum-belt record. The Kaianerekowa is articulated through wampum belts — patterns of purple and white shell beads woven into belts that record the constitutional commitments and the treaties of the Confederacy. The Hiawatha Belt records the founding of the Confederacy and the unity of the five (later six) nations. The Two Row Wampum (Guswenta) records the early-seventeenth-century treaty with the Dutch and subsequently with other European powers, specifying that the Haudenosaunee and the European settlers will travel down the river of life in two parallel vessels — the Haudenosaunee canoe and the European ship — neither attempting to steer the other's vessel, with the principles of friendship, peace, and forever as the rivers of meaning that flow between them. The wampum belts function as constitutional documentation, but the documentation is not analogous to the parchment-and-ink documentation of the Western constitutional tradition; the belts are produced through a craft practice (the gathering of shells, the production of beads, the weaving of patterns) that involves the maker in the constitutional commitment in a way that the signing of a parchment does not, and the reading of the belts is a performative-recitative practice that re-enacts the constitutional commitment each time the belt is read. The constitutional architecture is therefore documented in a form that is itself part of the practice of constitutional continuance, and the practice is part of the documentation. The wampum belts supply the project with a substantive specification of what cultivation-condition documentation looks like — documentation whose form participates in the cultivation rather than substituting for it.

The fourth element is the Great Tree and the Council Fire. The Kaianerekowa specifies that the Confederacy meets under a Great Tree of Peace, whose roots extend in the four directions and offer shelter to all peoples who would come and sit beneath them. The Great Tree's branches reach upward to receive the light, its trunk stands rooted in the earth, and the eagle perched at its top sees afar and gives warning of approaching danger. The Council Fire at the base of the tree is the place where the chiefs deliberate, and the deliberation proceeds in a specified order — the Mohawks and Senecas on one side as the Elder Brothers, the Oneidas and Cayugas on the other side as the Younger Brothers, with the Onondagas at the center as the Firekeepers, holding the fire and (where the Elder Brothers and Younger Brothers cannot agree) supplying the casting analytical voice. The architecture is not a metaphor decorated onto a deliberative procedure; it is a substantive specification of how deliberation across scales should proceed, with the spatial-symbolic positioning of the deliberators serving as continuous reminder of the constitutional commitments under which the deliberation operates. The Great Tree and the Council Fire are cultivation-protocol metaphors with substantive analytical content: the protocol is grounded in a place, the place organizes the relations among the deliberators, the deliberation proceeds under the conditions the place specifies, and the constitutional outcome is the cultivation of the conditions of Haudenosaunee life across the temporal horizon the seven-generations principle specifies.

The Haudenosaunee tradition supplies biocultural governance with the content of what cultivation-as-constitutional-method looks like in a tradition that has practiced it continuously for centuries. The seven-generations principle is the temporal-scale specification the project carries throughout. The clan-mother and Three Sisters architecture is the scale-matched decision-rights specification the project's nested-scales reference frame (Thread L) operationalizes. The wampum-belt record is the model for cultivation-condition documentation. The Great Tree and the Council Fire supply the cultivation-protocol metaphor with substantive analytical content that the project's positive constitutional architecture (Document XIII.01) carries forward. The engagement is conducted in the tradition's own analytical register; the Western recovery canon (Thread J) is positioned downstream of the Haudenosaunee articulation, not the other way around. The question of how non-Haudenosaunee scholarly engagement with the tradition is to be conducted — with what methodological discipline, under what conditions of authorization, with what acknowledgment of the tradition's continued life and the contemporary Haudenosaunee scholarly community's own work — is engaged at depth in Document VII.01's specification of multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline. The constitutional content the tradition articulates is the substance this document carries; the methodological discipline under which it is engaged is Document VII.01's terrain.

The contested question of the Haudenosaunee influence on the eighteenth-century framers of the United States Constitution — engaged across the historiography through Donald Grinde and Bruce Johansen's *Exemplar of Liberty: Native America and the Evolution of Democracy* (1991), through the contributions to Lyons et al.'s *Exiled in the Land of the Free* (1992), through Robert Venables's later work on the Iroquois federal model, and through the Congressional resolution H. Con. Res. 331 of 1988 acknowledging "the contribution of the Iroquois Confederacy of Nations to the development of the United States Constitution and to the further development of the federal model" — is engaged by the project at the level the historiographic record supports: the framers were familiar with the Haudenosaunee constitutional architecture, the architecture supplied an example of a confederation that worked, and the framers borrowed selectively (the federal structure, the executive-legislative-judicial division at the confederation level, the separation between the council of nations and the council of the union) while declining to borrow the elements that constituted the Haudenosaunee architecture's substantive constitutional commitments (the clan-mother authority, the seven-generations principle, the consensus-deliberation procedure, the integration of the constitutional architecture into the agricultural and subsistence practices of the polity). The selective borrowing is part of what makes the 1787 implementation a partial implementation — the framers borrowed the institutional skeleton without borrowing the cultivation conditions under which the institutional skeleton was sustained, and the consequences of that selective borrowing have run through the constitutional history the project's diagnostic engages.

## Thread C — The Broader Indigenous-Ecological Tradition

The broader Indigenous-ecological tradition the Anglophone reader is most likely to encounter in the contemporary moment runs through four scholars whose work the project engages at primary-articulator standing: Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi), Tyson Yunkaporta (Apalech, of the Wik-Mungkan and Wik-Munkan peoples of Cape York, north Queensland), Daniel R. Wildcat (Yuchi member of the Muscogee Creek Nation), and Kyle Powys Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi). Each carries a distinct analytical apparatus, articulates constitutional-relevant content the others do not, and operates from a tribal and intellectual location whose specificity must be preserved if the analytical engagement is to honor the primary-articulator commitment rather than collapse into a composite "Indigenous-ecological tradition" that flattens the differences among the scholars and the traditions they speak from. The four are engaged in sequence, each on the analytical apparatus the scholar has specified, with the project's constitutional move identified where it draws on the substance the scholar articulates.

Robin Wall Kimmerer's *Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants* (Milkweed, 2013) and her earlier *Gathering Moss: A Natural and Cultural History of Mosses* (Oregon State University Press, 2003) articulate an analytical apparatus that the project carries forward at constitutional scale through three substantive moves. The first is the move from inventory-of-resources to community-of-relations as the analytical primitive of the human-ecological relationship. In Kimmerer's specification, the conventional ecological-scientific apparatus inventories the species, populations, and biogeochemical flows of an ecosystem as objects of analysis, and the conventional environmental-policy apparatus translates the inventory into resources, services, and externalities for management. The Potawatomi analytical apparatus Kimmerer is writing from begins differently: the maple trees, the strawberries, the cedar, the salmon, the corn, the sweetgrass are persons whose flourishing the human community participates in, whose gifts the human community receives, and whose reciprocity the human community is responsible for sustaining. The shift is not aesthetic and not metaphorical. The plants in the Potawatomi analytical apparatus carry out work — the maple makes sugar, the strawberry makes fruit, the cedar makes its medicinal bark — that the human community depends on, and the human community's task is to reciprocate, to receive the gift with gratitude, to take only what is given, to leave enough for the others, to share what one has received, and to give back. The analytical apparatus is one of reciprocity at the level of constitutive relations rather than at the level of ethical aspiration overlaid on a fundamentally extractive relationship. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Kimmerer's move forward: the polity is not the human community in extractive relation to a resource-bearing environment but a biocultural formation whose human and other-than-human members are co-constituted in relations of mutual flourishing that the polity's constitutional architecture is responsible for sustaining.

The second move is the grammar of animacy. In *Braiding Sweetgrass*, Kimmerer reflects on her work to recover the Potawatomi language and on the analytical-cognitive shift that the language enacts. The grammatical structure of Potawatomi treats most natural objects — rivers, mountains, plants, animals, the sun, the moon, the wind — as animate beings, with verbal forms and pronominal references that the English language reserves for human persons. The grammar of animacy is not a primitive animism that the language-speaker overlays on a real world of inanimate objects; it is, in Kimmerer's specification, a precise tracking of which entities in the world act, which have agency, which sustain relationships, which generate and respond, and which therefore warrant the analytical apparatus appropriate to animate beings. The English language and the analytical apparatuses developed within it treat the natural world as predominantly inanimate, and the policy and constitutional apparatuses developed within those analytical apparatuses inherit the grammatical commitment. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries forward the alternative commitment: the polity is constituted in relations among animate beings, the rivers and forests and watersheds are not inanimate substrates of human political action but participants in the polity's life, and the constitutional architecture's task is to sustain the conditions under which the participation can be maintained. The Rights of Nature constitutional incorporation in Ecuador (2008) and Bolivia (2009), the legal-personhood architecture of *Te Awa Tupua* (2017), and the Atrato River decision of the Colombian Constitutional Court (T-622/16) — engaged in Threads G and H — are the contemporary constitutional-incorporation cases through which Kimmerer's grammar-of-animacy commitment has reached constitutional standing in working polities.

The third move is the integration of Indigenous scientific knowledge with what the Western academy calls scientific knowledge, on terms that do not subordinate either. Kimmerer is a Ph.D. botanist and a SUNY-ESF Distinguished Teaching Professor; she is also a member of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation working from the Potawatomi analytical inheritance her grandfathers were forbidden to speak. The integration is not a synthesis under one analytical register; the two ways of knowing operate in parallel, each contributing what the other cannot, with the integration occurring at the level of the practitioner who has been trained in both and at the level of the institution that can hold both without subordinating one. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries forward Kimmerer's specification of what integration of plural ways of knowing at the level of polity looks like — not the collapse of plurality into a single epistemic apparatus, not the relegation of Indigenous knowledge to advisory status while Western scientific knowledge supplies the operative analytical apparatus, but the cultivation of conditions under which plural ways of knowing operate in parallel at the constitutional scale, each contributing what the other cannot. Document VII.01 (Part VII) specifies the methodological discipline at depth; this document carries the constitutional-ontology commitment.

Tyson Yunkaporta's *Sand Talk: How Indigenous Thinking Can Save the World* (HarperOne, 2019) articulates an analytical apparatus the project carries forward at constitutional scale through different substantive moves than Kimmerer's. Yunkaporta writes from the Apalech clan of the Wik-Mungkan and Wik-Munkan peoples of Cape York and from his academic position at Deakin University, and he works in a register that is at once scholarly and Indigenous-pedagogical, with the marks of yarn-circle conversation and sand-talk visual reasoning carried into the prose form. The first move *Sand Talk* makes is the specification of eight nodes of cognition through which Indigenous reasoning proceeds: kinship-mind, story-mind, dreaming-mind, ancestor-mind, pattern-mind, place-mind, time-mind, and yarn-mind. Each node operates as a distinct cognitive-analytical apparatus and contributes to the others; reasoning that proceeds through only one or two nodes is, in Yunkaporta's analytical apparatus, impoverished reasoning, and the dominance of pattern-mind-without-place-mind in the Western analytical tradition is part of what produces the Western tradition's distinctive failure modes — the production of abstract patterns disconnected from the places that would correct and ground them. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Yunkaporta's move forward at the level of constitutional-cognitive architecture: the polity's deliberative-cognitive apparatus must operate through multiple nodes of cognition rather than through a single dominant one, and the constitutional architecture's task is to cultivate the conditions under which the plural cognitive apparatuses can operate together at the scale of constitutional decision-making. The neurodivergent-polis architecture of Document XII.05 operationalizes the commitment at the level of cognitive-architectural specification.

The second move *Sand Talk* makes is the specification of yarn-circle deliberation as the substantive form of Indigenous democratic practice. The yarn circle is a deliberative form in which participants sit in a circle, speak in turn, return to the topic across multiple passes, listen at depth before speaking, allow silence to do analytical work, and proceed toward decision through gradual accumulation of shared understanding rather than through majority vote or executive determination. Yunkaporta describes the yarn-circle form as the principal deliberative architecture across Indigenous societies of the Australian continent, with variants in other Indigenous traditions, and contrasts it with the adversarial-argumentative form that dominates the Western deliberative tradition. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology does not propose to replace adversarial argumentation with yarn-circle deliberation in working polities — the working polities the project addresses operate at scales and across pluralities at which yarn-circle deliberation cannot be the principal deliberative form. But the constitutional ontology proposes that the yarn-circle form be available at the local scales where it is appropriate, that the architecture cultivate conditions under which the form can be sustained at those scales, and that the Western polity's adversarial deliberative form be understood as one deliberative apparatus among the deliberative apparatuses the polity's plural cultural inheritance authorizes rather than as the universal form that other forms either approximate or fall short of. Documents XII.03 (e-democracy) and XII.05 (neurodivergent polis) operationalize the commitment at the level of deliberative-architectural specification.

The third move *Sand Talk* makes is the specification of pattern-thinking grounded in place. Yunkaporta describes Indigenous reasoning as fundamentally pattern-oriented — the apprehension of pattern across phenomena is what allows the reasoner to understand the phenomena — but specifies that the pattern-apprehension is grounded in place and in the relationships among the entities in the place. Pattern-thinking divorced from place produces the abstraction that allows the genocidal-extractive project of European colonization to proceed; pattern-thinking grounded in place produces the cultivation that sustains Indigenous communities across millennia. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Yunkaporta's specification forward at the level of constitutional-method: the constitutional architecture's pattern-recognition (its capacity to identify regularities across phenomena, to specify scale-matched decision rights, to articulate cultivation conditions) must remain grounded in the specific places where the polity's life proceeds, and the architecture that produces patterns abstracted from place reliably produces the design-imposition failure modes Thread K specifies.

Daniel R. Wildcat's *Red Alert! Saving the Planet with Indigenous Knowledge* (Fulcrum, 2009), drawing on his earlier collaboration with Vine Deloria Jr. on *Power and Place: Indian Education in America* (American Indian Graduate Center / Fulcrum, 2001), articulates the analytical apparatus of what Deloria called Indigenous Knowledge of Place. Wildcat specifies that the Indigenous Knowledge of Place is not a corpus of traditional ecological observations that can be extracted from the place and inventoried in a knowledge database. The knowledge is the place — the cumulative pattern of relationships among the entities that have lived in the place across generations, the seasonal cycles that organize the place's life, the conditions under which the place sustains the human and other-than-human members of its community, the disturbances the place has absorbed and the responses it has developed, the stories that record the cumulative pattern in a form that is itself part of the place's life. The Indigenous Knowledge of Place is, in Wildcat's analytical apparatus, ontological commitment rather than epistemic resource: the place is a knowing entity, the human community is one of the place's modes of knowing, and the knowledge is the relationship between them across time. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Wildcat's specification forward at the level of constitutional ontology: the polity is a place-grounded entity, the constitutional architecture is responsible for the place's continued capacity to know itself through its human and other-than-human members, and the design-imposition framings of Western constitutional theory that abstract the polity from the places that constitute it produce the systemic failures the project's diagnostic engages. Documents VI.05 (regenerative built environment), VI.03 (energy as cultivation), and VI.02 (food, soil, agriculture) operationalize the commitment at the level of material-substrate specification.

Kyle Powys Whyte's body of work — across his articles on Indigenous environmental justice, on kinship time, on climate colonialism, and on the contemporary stakes of Indigenous traditional knowledge in environmental policy (collected through his Arizona State University and University of Michigan affiliations, including "Indigenous Climate Change Studies: Indigenizing Futures, Decolonizing the Anthropocene" in *English Language Notes*, 2017; "Indigenous Science (Fiction) for the Anthropocene" in *Environment and Planning E*, 2018; "Settler Colonialism, Ecology, and Environmental Injustice" in *Environment and Society*, 2018) — articulates two substantive analytical commitments the project carries forward at constitutional scale. The first is kinship time as the temporal apparatus appropriate to Indigenous environmental analysis. In Whyte's specification, the dominant Western temporal apparatus operates on linear futurity: the present moves forward in time, the future is what comes next, the policy task is to project consequences forward and discount them at the appropriate rate. The Indigenous temporal apparatus Whyte specifies operates through kinship: the present is bound by relationships to those who came before (the ancestors) and to those who are coming (the descendants), and the policy task is to maintain the conditions of the relationships across the temporal span the kinship structure specifies. The seven-generations principle of the Haudenosaunee tradition (Thread B) is one specification of kinship time; the broader Indigenous temporal apparatus carries variants across traditions. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Whyte's specification forward at the level of constitutional-temporal commitment: the polity is bound by kinship relations across generations, the constitutional architecture is responsible for maintaining the conditions of those relations, and Document XII.04 (intergenerational rectification) operationalizes the commitment at the level of substantive constitutional commitment.

The second analytical commitment Whyte articulates is climate colonialism — the specification that the climate crisis is, for Indigenous peoples, the continuation of a colonial dispossession that has been underway for centuries rather than a novel crisis arriving in the contemporary moment. Whyte's analytical work specifies that the conditions Indigenous peoples now face under climate change — the disruption of ancestral foodways, the destruction of sacred sites, the forced relocation from territories that have sustained communities for centuries, the loss of the species and ecosystems that constitute the community's analytical inheritance — are the conditions Indigenous peoples have been facing under colonial dispossession for centuries. The novelty of the climate crisis is the novelty of its arrival in non-Indigenous communities and constitutional polities; the content is the continuation of what Indigenous peoples have been navigating throughout. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Whyte's specification forward at the level of disturbance-regime analysis: the disturbances the polity must absorb (cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic, specified across Documents II.02, II.04, II.05, II.06, II.07) are not novel arrivals in a previously stable polity but the continued disturbances under which the polity has been operating, with the colonial-dispossession dynamic supplying the historical record of what disturbance under inadequate constitutional architecture looks like. The Indigenous traditions are not consulting from outside the disturbance landscape; they are operating from within it, with analytical apparatus developed under it, and the engagement is on those terms.

The four scholars are engaged here in sequence rather than synthesized. Each carries an analytical apparatus the others do not: Kimmerer's grammar of animacy and reciprocity-as-constitutive-relation, Yunkaporta's eight cognitive nodes and yarn-circle deliberation, Wildcat's place as knowing entity, Whyte's kinship time and climate colonialism. The substantive content the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries forward is plural — each scholar's contribution is engaged on its own terms, with the constitutional implications named where the apparatus authorizes them. The thread does not produce a composite Indigenous-ecological synthesis; the synthesis would be precisely the move Thread M argues against under the renovation-versus-decolonial framing. What the thread produces is engagement with plural primary articulators whose work the constitutional ontology depends on and whose registers must be preserved if the dependence is to honor what each articulates.

## Thread D — The Daoist Relational Ontology

The Daoist tradition — engaged here through the *Daodejing* attributed to Laozi (the received text dating from approximately the fourth or third century BCE, with antecedents earlier) and the *Zhuangzi* (compiled across the fourth through second centuries BCE), through Roger T. Ames and David L. Hall's translation and philosophical commentary in *Daodejing: Making This Life Significant* (Ballantine, 2003) and their broader work on Chinese philosophy in *Thinking from the Han: Self, Truth, and Transcendence in Chinese and Western Culture* (SUNY, 1998), through Brook Ziporyn's *Zhuangzi: The Complete Writings* (Hackett, 2020), and through James Miller's *China's Green Religion: Daoism and the Quest for a Sustainable Future* (Columbia, 2017) on the contemporary environmental implications — articulates an analytical apparatus the project carries forward at constitutional scale through the concepts of *wu wei* and *ziran*. The engagement does not treat the Daoist tradition as one source among many in a comparative-political-philosophy survey; the tradition is engaged as primary articulator of cultivation-not-design ontology, with substantive constitutional content the project carries forward.

The concept of *wu wei* (無為) is conventionally rendered in English as "non-action," "effortless action," or "non-coercive action," and each rendering captures a partial sense of what the term specifies in the Daoist analytical apparatus. Ames and Hall, in their philosophical translation and commentary, render *wu wei* as "non-coercive action" and specify the analytical commitment the rendering reflects: *wu wei* is not the absence of action but the form of action that proceeds in accord with the inherent tendencies of what is being acted upon, that does not force its object into a predetermined shape, that allows the object's own self-organizing tendencies to do the substantive work, and that intervenes only to remove obstacles to the self-organizing tendencies rather than to impose the desired outcome through structural coercion. The cook in the *Zhuangzi* who has used the same butcher's knife for nineteen years without dulling it, because the knife passes through the spaces in the ox rather than through the bone, is the canonical image: the action proceeds along the grain of what is being acted upon, the action's effectiveness depends on apprehending the inherent structure, and the action that forces an outcome against the inherent structure produces both wasted effort and damaged tools. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *wu wei* commitment forward at the level of constitutional method: the constitutional architecture operates as non-coercive cultivation of the polity's inherent tendencies, removing obstacles to the polity's self-organizing dynamics rather than imposing the desired institutional shape through coercive structural specification, and the architecture that operates by forcing the polity into a predetermined institutional shape produces wasted effort and damaged polity in the same way the cook's forced knife produces wasted effort and damaged blade.

The concept of *ziran* (自然) is conventionally rendered as "naturalness" or "spontaneity," and Ames and Hall render it as "so-of-itself" or "spontaneously self-becoming" to preserve the analytical sense the term carries in the Daoist apparatus. *Ziran* specifies the mode of being of phenomena that come into being and continue in being through their own internal dynamics, without external imposition, in relation to the conditions that sustain them. The Daoist analytical commitment is that the natural world operates by *ziran*, that human life flourishes when it operates by *ziran*, and that the constitutional task is to cultivate the conditions under which the polity can operate by *ziran* rather than under conditions of forced specification. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *ziran* commitment forward at the level of constitutional ontology: the polity is a self-organizing biocultural formation whose flourishing depends on the cultivation of the conditions of its self-becoming, the constitutional architecture's task is to specify and maintain those conditions rather than to specify the polity's institutional shape in advance, and the architecture that operates by detailed institutional specification at the expense of cultivation-condition specification produces the brittle, contradictory, and increasingly ungovernable polities that Document II.01's diagnostic engages.

The Daoist analytical apparatus must be distinguished from the Western laissez-faire reading into which it is sometimes flattened. The laissez-faire reading, developed in the European Enlightenment and carried forward through the classical-liberal and libertarian political-economic traditions, holds that the state should refrain from intervening in the economy and in the lives of citizens because intervention produces inefficiency and constraints on individual liberty. The Daoist analytical apparatus is doing different analytical work. *Wu wei* is not absence of intervention; it is action that proceeds along the grain of inherent tendencies and removes obstacles to their unfolding. The Daoist state, in the analytical apparatus of the *Daodejing* and the *Zhuangzi*, is responsible for the conditions of its people's flourishing — for the equitable distribution of agricultural land, for the maintenance of irrigation systems, for the cultivation of conditions under which the people's natural capacities can develop, for the prevention of the concentration of wealth that produces the social pathologies the Daoist texts identify. The Daoist sage-ruler does not refrain from action; the sage-ruler acts continually, but the action proceeds by *wu wei* rather than by structural imposition, and the action is responsible for the conditions of flourishing rather than for the prevention of intervention. James Miller's *China's Green Religion* (2017) traces the contemporary environmental implications: the Daoist analytical apparatus authorizes substantive environmental protection, substantive cultivation of the conditions under which the natural world's *ziran* can proceed, and substantive intervention against the structural forcings (concentrated industrial extraction, large-scale infrastructural imposition) that disrupt the natural world's self-becoming. The laissez-faire reading reads *wu wei* as absence of action and therefore as authorization for the precise structural forcings the Daoist apparatus identifies as the principal problem; the misreading is a category error.

The substantive constitutional commitments the Daoist ontology authorizes that laissez-faire framings do not include, first, the substantive responsibility of the constitutional architecture for the conditions of the polity's flourishing — the cultivation of conditions is not laissez-faire withdrawal but continuous action along the grain of the polity's inherent tendencies; second, the substantive constraint on the concentration of wealth and power that the *Daodejing* identifies as the principal source of social-political pathology, with passages like the eightieth chapter's specification that "the people are difficult to govern because their rulers do too much" pointing not to libertarian withdrawal but to the recognition that imposed structural specification produces ungovernability; third, the substantive commitment to scale-matched intervention — the *Daodejing*'s ideal polity is a small community whose people know one another and whose constitutional architecture operates at scales appropriate to the relationships, with the recognition that imposed unification across scales produces the ungovernability the text addresses; and fourth, the substantive ecological commitment that the polity's flourishing is bound up with the flourishing of the natural world's *ziran* and that disturbance to the natural world's self-becoming produces disturbance to the polity. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries all four commitments forward. The eco-mimetic policy-making method of Document XII.01 operationalizes the *wu wei* commitment at the level of legislative process — laws drafted to remove obstacles to the polity's self-organizing tendencies rather than to impose institutional specifications in advance. The bounded decision rights of Documents XII.09 through XII.12 operationalize the scale-matched-intervention commitment. The currency architecture of Document XII.02 operationalizes the constraint on the concentration of wealth. The material-substrate work of Documents VI.05, VI.03, VI.02, and VI.04 operationalizes the ecological commitment.

The Daoist apparatus carries distinctive content the other primary-articulator traditions do not. The Haudenosaunee Great Law (Thread B) supplies the constitutional architecture of a continuously practiced eco-mimetic polity; the Daoist tradition supplies the philosophical specification of what cultivation-not-design ontology requires of constitutional method. The broader Indigenous-ecological tradition (Thread C) supplies the relational ontology in the analytical registers of the Indigenous scholars writing from within it; the Daoist tradition supplies the relational ontology in the register of a continuously elaborated philosophical-textual tradition with two-and-a-half millennia of commentary. The two registers contribute distinctive substantive content that the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries forward without collapsing them into a single relational-ontology vocabulary.

## Thread E — The Buddhist Dependent-Origination Tradition

The Buddhist dependent-origination tradition (*pratītyasamutpāda* in Sanskrit, *paṭiccasamuppāda* in Pali, dependent co-arising or dependent origination in English) supplies the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with the philosophical specification of relational existence at the level of metaphysical depth that the project's constitutional ontology operates. The tradition is engaged here through Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (the *Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way*, dating from approximately the second century CE), the foundational Madhyamaka text, accessed through Jay Garfield's translation and philosophical commentary in *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way* (Oxford, 1995) and through Mark Siderits and Shōryū Katsura's *Nāgārjuna's Middle Way* (Wisdom, 2013); through Thich Nhat Hanh's articulation of interbeing in *The Heart of Understanding* (Parallax, 1988); through Joanna Macy's *Mutual Causality in Buddhism and General Systems Theory* (SUNY, 1991), which specifies the contemporary translation between the dependent-origination tradition and the systems-theoretic apparatus; and through David Loy's *The Great Awakening: A Buddhist Social Theory* (Wisdom, 2003), which extends the analytical apparatus to the social-political register. The engagement preserves the analytical sophistication of the tradition rather than reducing it to a relational-thinking tag, and identifies the constitutional content the tradition authorizes that the project's ontology carries forward.

The doctrine of *pratītyasamutpāda* in its Madhyamaka articulation specifies that all phenomena arise in dependence on conditions and have no inherent existence (*svabhāva*) independent of those conditions. Nāgārjuna's analytical work in the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* proceeds through twenty-seven chapters of philosophical investigation, each chapter taking up a category — causation, motion, the senses, the aggregates, the elements, the self, time, fire and fuel, the Tathāgata, suffering, action and its fruit — and demonstrating that the category lacks inherent existence and arises only in dependence on its constitutive conditions. The analytical apparatus is sophisticated philosophical investigation, conducted in dialogue with the philosophical schools of the period (the Sarvāstivāda, the Sautrāntika, the Sāṃkhya, the Nyāya, and other traditions), and is not the pre-philosophical animism into which Western readers sometimes flatten the dependent-origination commitment. The Madhyamaka tradition that grows from Nāgārjuna's work — through Candrakīrti in the seventh century, through the Tibetan dGe lugs pa tradition's Tsongkhapa in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, through contemporary scholarship represented by Garfield, Siderits, and Katsura — supplies a sustained philosophical tradition that has elaborated the dependent-origination apparatus across nearly two millennia of continuous engagement.

The substantive constitutional content the dependent-origination ontology authorizes proceeds through three commitments the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries forward. The first is that the polity is relational rather than substantive. In the analytical apparatus of substance-oriented Western political philosophy — the Lockean tradition the founders worked within, the Hobbesian and Rousseauean alternatives that shaped European constitutional thought, the broader social-contract tradition that has dominated Western political philosophy since the seventeenth century — the polity is a substantive entity composed of individual substantive subjects who exist prior to and independently of the polity, and the polity's task is to coordinate the activities of these prior substantive subjects through institutional arrangements that protect their individual existence and prerogatives. In the analytical apparatus of dependent-origination, the polity is constituted in the relations among its members, the members are constituted in their relations to one another and to the polity that holds them, and the analytical priority of substantive individual subjects over their relations is a category error that the substance-oriented analytical apparatus carries forward and that produces systematic distortions when applied at the constitutional scale. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the dependent-origination commitment forward: the polity is constituted in relations among biocultural members, the relations are constitutive of the members as the members are constitutive of the relations, and the constitutional architecture is responsible for the conditions under which the constitutive relations are sustained.

The second commitment is that the individual subject of rights is constituted through relations rather than as ontologically prior to them. In the social-contract tradition, the individual subject possesses rights prior to the formation of the polity, and the polity is formed to protect those antecedent rights. The Lockean version specifies the rights as natural — life, liberty, and property — and treats the polity as instituted to secure them. The constitutional commitment is therefore that the polity exists to serve the antecedent individual subject, and the individual subject's flourishing is independent of and prior to the polity's existence. The dependent-origination analytical apparatus produces a different commitment. The individual subject is constituted through the relations the subject participates in — with kin and clan, with the human community and the broader-than-human community, with the territory and the practices that sustain the subject's life, with the linguistic and cultural inheritance that gives the subject's experience analytical structure. The subject's rights are therefore not antecedent attributes the subject possesses prior to relations but conditions of the relations through which the subject is constituted, and the constitutional task is to maintain the conditions of the constitutive relations rather than to protect a fictive antecedent subject from the relations that would constitute it. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the commitment forward: the polity's constitutional commitments protect the conditions of the constitutive relations through which biocultural subjects come into being and continue in being, and the design-imposition framings of Western constitutional theory that protect a fictive antecedent subject at the expense of the constitutive relations produce the systematic exclusions Thread K and Document II.01 specify.

The third commitment is the analytical case against the Lockean possessive-individualist framing on terms the dependent-origination apparatus makes available. C. B. Macpherson's *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism: Hobbes to Locke* (Oxford, 1962) specified the analytical structure of the possessive-individualist tradition that the Lockean framing exemplifies: the individual is the proprietor of the individual's own person and capacities, owes nothing to society for them, and the society is essentially a series of relations among proprietors who exchange capacities and goods. The dependent-origination analytical apparatus identifies the framing as ontologically incoherent. The individual's person and capacities are not given to the individual antecedent to relations; they are constituted in the relations the individual participates in. Kinship, language, cultivation of capacities through teaching and apprenticeship, the territorial and ecological substrate that sustains the individual's life, the cultural and spiritual inheritance through which the individual's experience is given analytical structure — none of these is antecedent to the individual in the way the possessive-individualist framing requires. The framing's analytical structure is therefore not a description of how individuals actually exist but a normative specification that human existence be understood *as if* the individual were a substantive proprietor antecedent to relations, and the specification produces the systematic alienations the broader critical literature traces — alienation from labor, from kin, from territory, from cultural inheritance, from the constitutive conditions of the individual's own existence. The dependent-origination apparatus does not require the analytical move to be made through Buddhist apparatus alone; the Indigenous traditions of Threads B, C, F, G, and H make parallel analytical moves through their own apparatuses, and the convergence across the primary-articulator traditions is part of what authorizes the project's constitutional commitment. But the Madhyamaka tradition supplies the philosophical depth at which the move is articulated as substantive philosophical critique of the substance-oriented apparatus, and the depth is part of what the constitutional ontology requires.

Thich Nhat Hanh's articulation of interbeing in *The Heart of Understanding* (1988) and the broader corpus of his teaching on engaged Buddhism translates the dependent-origination commitment into a contemporary register accessible to readers who do not work in the technical Madhyamaka tradition. Hanh's specification — that to look deeply at a sheet of paper is to see the clouds whose rain fell on the tree from which the paper is made, the sunlight that the tree absorbed, the soil in which the tree grew, the labor of the people who made the paper, the trucks that transported it, and that to look deeply at any phenomenon is to see the conditions that constitute it — supplies the contemporary register through which the dependent-origination commitment can be carried into policy and constitutional discourse without requiring the reader to engage the Madhyamaka philosophical-technical apparatus. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Hanh's specification forward at the level of constitutional pedagogy: the polity's members can be brought into recognition of the constitutive conditions of their existence through the interbeing register, and the constitutional architecture's pedagogical and deliberative practices can incorporate the recognition without requiring technical philosophical engagement.

Joanna Macy's *Mutual Causality in Buddhism and General Systems Theory* (1991) specifies the contemporary translation between the dependent-origination tradition and the systems-theoretic apparatus of Western complex-systems science. Macy's analytical work identifies the structural parallels — the rejection of linear causality, the commitment to feedback and circular causation, the analytical priority of relations over substantive entities, the recognition that systems organize themselves through their internal dynamics rather than through external imposition — and specifies where the parallels break down. The dependent-origination tradition operates as a soteriological apparatus aimed at the liberation of the practitioner from the suffering produced by ignorance of dependent-origination, where the systems-theoretic apparatus operates as a descriptive-analytical apparatus aimed at understanding the dynamics of complex systems. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Macy's analytical work forward in two registers. The first is the constitutional-method register: the dependent-origination apparatus authorizes the constitutional commitments the systems-theoretic apparatus (Thread J and Document V.01) also authorizes, with the dependent-origination apparatus operating at greater philosophical depth and the systems-theoretic apparatus operating with greater empirical-mathematical detail; the constitutional architecture draws on both. The second is the soteriological register: the dependent-origination tradition's specification of the suffering produced by ignorance of dependent-origination supplies the analytical apparatus through which the project understands the systemic suffering produced by ignorance of biocultural coupling — the alienation, the ecological collapse, the constitutional dysfunction the broader arc engages — as suffering produced by analytical-philosophical error in the ontological commitments through which the polity has been constituted, and the constitutional renovation as substantive intervention in the conditions that produce the error.

David Loy's *The Great Awakening: A Buddhist Social Theory* (2003) extends the dependent-origination apparatus to the social-political register the project operates at. Loy's analytical work identifies the institutional-systemic counterparts to the Three Poisons of individual ignorance, greed, and aversion: institutional ignorance (the production of analytical apparatus that fails to track the constitutive relations on which the institution's operation depends), institutional greed (the systematic accumulation of resources in ways that disrupt the constitutive relations through which the system as a whole sustains itself), and institutional aversion (the systematic exclusion of those whose presence would correct the institution's analytical apparatus). The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Loy's specification forward at the level of constitutional diagnostic: the constitutional dysfunctions Document II.01 and Documents II.02 through II.07 engage are institutional manifestations of the Three Poisons operating at constitutional scale, and the constitutional renovation operates as substantive intervention in the conditions that produce the institutional pathologies. The Buddhist analytical apparatus authorizes the diagnostic on its own terms; the parallel diagnostic from the other primary-articulator traditions (Threads B, C, F, G, H, I) and from the Western recovery canon (Thread J) supplies convergent confirmation, but each tradition is engaged on its own analytical apparatus, and the convergence is what authorizes the project's confidence in the diagnostic without requiring the synthesis the renovation-versus-decolonial engagement of Thread M argues against.

## Thread F — The African Ubuntu Tradition

The Ubuntu tradition — articulated through the Bantu language family (with its principal contemporary terms *ubuntu* in Zulu, Xhosa, and Ndebele; *botho* in Sesotho and Setswana; *umunthu* in Chichewa; *vumunhu* in Tsonga; and parallel terms across the wider Bantu-speaking region of central and southern Africa) and through the philosophical, jurisprudential, and constitutional engagements that have emerged from the African philosophical tradition and from the postapartheid South African constitutional project — supplies the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with the specification of relational personhood at the level of constitutional jurisprudence and with the case-study record of what the translation of relational ontology into constitutional terms accomplishes and what it does not. The tradition is engaged here through Mogobe Ramose's *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (Mond Books, 1999, second edition 2002), the most sustained philosophical articulation in English; through Drucilla Cornell and Karin van Marle's *Ubuntu and the Law: African Ideals and Postapartheid Jurisprudence* (Fordham, 2011) and their earlier article "Exploring Ubuntu: Tentative Reflections" (*African Human Rights Law Journal*, 2005); through *S v Makwanyane and Another* 1995 (3) SA 391 (CC), the South African Constitutional Court decision that established Ubuntu as substantive constitutional content; through Augustine Shutte's *Ubuntu: An Ethic for a New South Africa* (Cluster Publications, 2001); and through Thaddeus Metz's *A Relational Moral Theory: A New African Ethic* (Oxford, 2022), the recent systematic philosophical articulation. The thread distinguishes Ubuntu's relational ontology from the Buddhist dependent-origination and Daoist relational ontologies engaged in Threads E and D, naming what each tradition articulates that the others do not.

Mogobe Ramose's analytical apparatus in *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* specifies that Ubuntu is best apprehended through the Nguni proverb *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* — variously rendered as "a person is a person through other persons," "a human is a human through other humans," or "I am because we are." Ramose's analytical work specifies that the proverb is not a sentimental affirmation of community values but a substantive ontological commitment: personhood is constituted in the relations the person participates in, the person comes into being through the other persons with whom the relations are constituted, and the content of what it is to be a person is given by the quality of the relations rather than by attributes the person possesses antecedent to relations. The ontological commitment carries through to the substantive ethical-political commitment that Ramose specifies as the principal contribution of the African philosophical tradition: the human community's task is to cultivate the conditions under which the constitutive relations through which persons come into being can sustain the persons they constitute, and the failure of those conditions is the failure of personhood at the community scale rather than the deprivation of antecedent persons of attributes they would otherwise have possessed. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Ramose's commitment forward at the level of constitutional ontology: the polity's members are constituted in relations the polity is responsible for sustaining, the constitutional architecture is responsible for the cultivation of the relations, and the design-imposition framings of Western constitutional theory that protect antecedent rights-bearing individuals from the relations that constitute them produce the systematic distortions Thread K specifies.

The South African Constitutional Court's invocation of Ubuntu in *S v Makwanyane* (1995) — the decision that struck down the death penalty in postapartheid South Africa — provides the substantive jurisprudential case study of what constitutional translation of Ubuntu accomplishes. The Court's majority opinion, written by Justice Arthur Chaskalson, and the concurring opinions of Justices Yvonne Mokgoro, Pius Langa, Thembile Skweyiya, and others, drew on the Ubuntu tradition as substantive constitutional content rather than as ornamental reference. Justice Mokgoro's concurring opinion specified that Ubuntu carries the constitutional commitments of group solidarity, communal interdependence, respect for human dignity, and the cultivation of conditions under which the African community can sustain itself, and that the constitutional incorporation of Ubuntu in the interim constitution's postamble (specifying that "there is a need for understanding but not for vengeance, a need for *ubuntu* but not for victimization") supplies substantive constitutional content that the constitutional adjudication must engage. The majority's analytical move proceeded through three commitments: first, that human dignity (constitutionally protected) is constituted in relations and depends on the human community's commitment to sustaining the relations; second, that the death penalty is the most absolute repudiation of the relational commitment Ubuntu specifies, removing the condemned person from the community of persons in a way that no other punishment does; and third, that the cultivation of the postapartheid constitutional polity requires the rejection of the apartheid state's instrumental treatment of human beings as objects of state coercion. The *Makwanyane* decision supplies the substantive case study of what constitutional translation of relational ontology into constitutional jurisprudence looks like at the level of decided cases with binding constitutional weight.

The broader jurisprudential incorporation of Ubuntu in postapartheid South Africa has proceeded across cases involving customary law (where Ubuntu has been invoked to authorize the recognition of African customary practices on terms that the apartheid-era jurisprudence had refused to grant), restorative justice (where Ubuntu has supplied the analytical apparatus through which restorative-justice practices have been integrated with the constitutional commitment to dignity and reconciliation), labor jurisprudence (where Ubuntu has been invoked in cases involving the relations among workers, employers, and the broader community), and the broader constitutional jurisprudence of the Constitutional Court. Cornell and van Marle's analytical work in *Ubuntu and the Law* and in "Exploring Ubuntu" specifies what the jurisprudential incorporation has accomplished and what it has not. The accomplishment is the constitutional weight Ubuntu now carries in South African jurisprudence — Ubuntu is not advisory but substantive, not ornamental but operative, and the constitutional adjudication that engages Ubuntu engages it as content rather than as reference. The limitation is the jurisprudential tendency to flatten Ubuntu into a generic communitarian-ethical commitment that loses the specific analytical apparatus the African philosophical tradition supplies — to read Ubuntu as "African values" generally rather than as the specific ontological commitment Ramose, Shutte, Metz, and the broader philosophical literature articulates. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology takes both the accomplishment and the limitation as substantive content. The constitutional architecture can carry Ubuntu's relational ontology at substantive constitutional weight (the accomplishment confirms it); the constitutional architecture must preserve the analytical specificity of the tradition rather than flattening it into generic communitarian language (the limitation specifies the failure mode to guard against).

Augustine Shutte's *Ubuntu: An Ethic for a New South Africa* (2001) articulates the analytical apparatus through which Ubuntu translates into a substantive ethics for the contemporary polity. Shutte specifies four constitutive commitments. The first is that the human person comes into being through participation in community, with the community's cultivation of the person operating as the content of what the philosophical tradition means by "becoming a person." The second is that the community itself is constituted through the relations among its members, with the community having no existence antecedent to the relations and no analytical priority over them. The third is that the human person's substantive flourishing depends on the quality of the relations the person participates in, with the cultivation of high-quality relations as the principal substantive task of the community. The fourth is that the broader-than-human relations — with ancestors, with the territory, with the natural world, with the cosmological-spiritual order — are constitutive of the human person's existence and not separable from the human-to-human relations. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Shutte's articulation forward at the level of constitutional commitment: the polity's task is the cultivation of high-quality constitutive relations across the human-and-broader-than-human relational landscape, and the constitutional architecture is responsible for the conditions under which the cultivation can proceed. The disability-justice commitments of Document XII.05 operationalize the substantive-flourishing-depends-on-relations commitment at the level of cognitive-architectural specification.

Thaddeus Metz's *A Relational Moral Theory: A New African Ethic* (Oxford, 2022) supplies the recent systematic philosophical articulation that engages the analytical literature on Ubuntu at depth and produces a defensible relational-moral apparatus the project can engage in dialogue with the broader contemporary moral philosophy. Metz's specification — that the morally right action is the action that produces friendly relationships, where a friendly relationship is constituted by identifying with others' ends and exhibiting solidarity with others' lives — supplies a precise analytical apparatus through which the Ubuntu commitment can be engaged in conversation with the Western moral-philosophical tradition without flattening either. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Metz's specification forward at the level of constitutional pedagogy: the constitutional commitments the project advances can be engaged through the Western moral-philosophical tradition's analytical apparatus where the engagement is productive, with Metz's relational-moral theory supplying one of the principal bridges; the engagement does not require the abandonment of the African philosophical tradition's specificity, and the constitutional commitments are not derivative of the bridge but supported by it.

The Ubuntu tradition carries distinctive content the other primary-articulator traditions do not. The Buddhist dependent-origination apparatus (Thread E) operates at the level of metaphysical-philosophical specification and produces the soteriological commitment to liberation from the suffering produced by ignorance of dependent-origination; the Daoist apparatus (Thread D) operates at the level of cosmological-philosophical specification and produces the commitment to cultivation in accord with the natural world's *ziran*; the Ubuntu apparatus operates at the level of social-ontological specification and produces the commitment to the cultivation of the conditions under which constitutive relations among persons can sustain the persons they constitute. The constitutional-jurisprudential record of the postapartheid South African project is, among the primary-articulator traditions engaged in this document, the most developed contemporary record of the translation of relational ontology into constitutional jurisprudence at the scale of a working national polity, and the content the record supplies — both the accomplishments and the limitations — is part of what the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries forward.

## Thread G — The Andean Ayllu and Ayni Traditions

The Andean philosophical tradition — articulated through the Quechua and Aymara languages and the broader Andean cultural inheritance of the highland Andes, with continuous practice from pre-Inca antiquity through the Inca period (roughly the thirteenth through sixteenth centuries), through the colonial-Andean period that saw the suppression and partial preservation of the tradition under Spanish rule, and into the contemporary period in which Quechua- and Aymara-speaking communities sustain the tradition in everyday practice and in which the constitutional architectures of Ecuador (2008) and Bolivia (2009) have incorporated substantive elements of the tradition at constitutional standing — supplies the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with two principal contributions. The first is the philosophical apparatus of *ayllu* (the relational kin-territorial unit) and *ayni* (reciprocal labor and exchange) as the constitutive social form of Andean life, with cosmological-philosophical depth in concepts of *Pacha Mama* (the earth mother as constitutive entity), *sumak kawsay* in Quechua (rendered as *buen vivir* in Spanish, "the good life" or "the good way of being") and *suma qamaña* in Aymara (the parallel Aymara concept). The second is the contemporary case-study record of constitutional incorporation in Ecuador and Bolivia, which together supply the closest existing approximations to biocultural-governance constitutional implementation at the level of working national polities. The thread engages both at depth, including the implementation record's significant unevenness, and identifies what the analytical apparatus authorizes that the implementations have only partially realized.

The Andean tradition is engaged here through Josef Estermann's *Andean Philosophy: A Philosophical Investigation Across Cultures* (Spanish 1998, English Wipf & Stock 2006), the most sustained English-language philosophical articulation of the Andean conceptual apparatus; through Eduardo Gudynas's *Derechos de la Naturaleza: Ética biocéntrica y políticas ambientales* (Tinta Limón, 2014) on the Rights of Nature movement and its Andean philosophical grounding; through Marisol de la Cadena's *Earth Beings: Ecologies of Practice across Andean Worlds* (Duke, 2015), an ethnographic-philosophical work whose engagement with Andean ontologies through her long collaboration with the Quechua intellectual Mariano Turpo specifies what is at stake in taking the Andean tradition's ontological commitments at the philosophical depth they require; through Alberto Acosta's *Buen Vivir: Vivir bien* (Editorial Universitaria Abya-Yala, 2013), the analytical synthesis by one of the principal architects of the Ecuadorian constitutional incorporation; and through the constitutional texts of the Republic of Ecuador (2008) and the Plurinational State of Bolivia (2009) themselves, in their original Spanish and in the official English translations.

The *ayllu* is the relational kin-territorial unit of Andean social organization. In Estermann's analytical apparatus, the *ayllu* is constituted by the integrated relations among its human members, its territorial location (the specific land-form, water-form, and microclimate the *ayllu* occupies), its agricultural and pastoral practices that are particular to that location, its ancestral lineages and the spirits of its territory, and the cosmological-spiritual order that gives the entire complex its analytical structure. The *ayllu* is not a kinship group that occupies a territory; it is the integrated unit of kin-and-territory-and-practice-and-cosmos, and its members are constituted as members through their participation in the integrated unit. The analytical primitive of Andean social organization is therefore not the individual person and not the kinship group but the *ayllu* as a relational-territorial-cosmological unit, and the content of social life is given by the cultivation of the integrity of the *ayllu* across the dimensions that constitute it. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *ayllu* concept forward at the level of constitutional category: the polity's nested-scales reference frame (Thread L) operates at scales that include *ayllu*-equivalent units — relational-territorial-cosmological units whose integrity the constitutional architecture is responsible for sustaining — and the constitutional decision rights specified at those scales are decision rights of the *ayllu*-equivalent unit rather than decision rights of individual persons aggregated through electoral procedures.

*Ayni* is the form of reciprocity that organizes labor and exchange across the *ayllu* and among *ayllus*. Estermann specifies *ayni* not as a transactional exchange of equivalents but as a substantive commitment to reciprocity over the long temporal horizon: the work given today will be reciprocated when needed, the labor contributed to one household's harvest will be returned at another household's harvest, the gift offered will be returned in some form, and the failure to reciprocate is the failure of social standing in a way that has substantive material consequences for the failed reciprocator's place in the *ayllu*. *Ayni* is therefore not gift economy in the Western anthropological sense (which sometimes treats the gift as opposed to the commodity exchange) but a substantive economic-cosmological commitment that the relations among persons are the principal economic form and that the substantive economy operates through the cultivation of the relations rather than through the aggregation of transactions. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *ayni* commitment forward at the level of constitutional economic specification: the currency architecture of Document XII.02 operationalizes the commitment to reciprocity as substantive economic form, the political-economic disturbance diagnostic of Document II.07 engages the failures of the contemporary political-economic order as failures of substantive reciprocity at the constitutional scale, and the cultivation-protocol architecture of Document XIII.01 specifies the conditions under which substantive reciprocity at the polity scale can be sustained.

Marisol de la Cadena's *Earth Beings: Ecologies of Practice across Andean Worlds* (2015) supplies the substantive ethnographic-philosophical record of what taking the Andean ontological commitments seriously requires. De la Cadena's long collaboration with the Quechua intellectual Mariano Turpo — across decades of work in the Cuzco region of the southern Peruvian highlands — produces a substantive account of how the Andean ontological commitments operate in everyday practice, in political conflict with the Peruvian state over land and resource extraction, and in the negotiation between Andean and non-Andean analytical apparatuses that the contemporary moment requires. The *tirakuna* (the earth-beings, the mountains-as-persons with whom the Quechua community is in continuous relation) are not, in de la Cadena's analytical apparatus, beliefs the Quechua community holds; they are entities whose ontological standing the Quechua practice presupposes and whose engagement with the Quechua community is the operative form of Andean social-political life. The Peruvian state's analytical apparatus does not recognize the *tirakuna* as entities with ontological standing; the conflict between the state and the Quechua community over (for instance) the proposed Tia Maria copper mine in the Cocachacra valley is therefore not a conflict over the distribution of resources but a conflict over what entities have ontological standing in the polity, and the analytical apparatus available to either side does not adequately register what the other side is doing. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries de la Cadena's specification forward at the level of constitutional ontology: the polity's constitutional architecture must be capable of recognizing entities at ontological standing that the prevailing analytical apparatus does not, and the cultivation-protocol architecture of Document XIII.01 specifies the conditions under which such recognition can become operative without flattening the recognized entities into the recognizing apparatus's categories.

The Ecuadorian Constitution of 2008 incorporates substantive Andean philosophical content at constitutional standing. Article 71 specifies that "Nature, or *Pacha Mama*, where life is reproduced and exists, has the right to integral respect for its existence and for the maintenance and regeneration of its life cycles, structure, functions and evolutionary processes." Articles 72 through 74 elaborate the content of the Rights of Nature, the obligations of the state, and the right of any person or community to demand respect for the rights of Nature from any public authority. Article 14 specifies that "the right of the population to live in a healthy and ecologically balanced environment, which guarantees sustainability and the good way of living, *sumak kawsay*, is recognized," with *sumak kawsay* carried in the Quechua original as substantive constitutional content. The Bolivian Plurinational Constitution of 2009 carries parallel commitments through its specification of *suma qamaña* / *vivir bien* as constitutional commitment and its subsequent legislation — including the *Ley de Derechos de la Madre Tierra* (Law of the Rights of Mother Earth, Law 071 of 2010) and the *Ley Marco de la Madre Tierra y Desarrollo Integral para Vivir Bien* (Framework Law of Mother Earth and Integral Development for Living Well, Law 300 of 2012) — that operationalizes the constitutional commitments at the level of substantive policy.

The implementation record is uneven. Gudynas's analytical work in *Derechos de la Naturaleza* (2014) and his subsequent publications has tracked the implementation gap in both Ecuador and Bolivia. The constitutional commitments to *Pacha Mama* and *sumak kawsay* in Ecuador have been read against the policy decisions of the Correa government (2007–2017) on large-scale extractive industry — particularly oil extraction in the Yasuní National Park and mining in the southern Amazonian provinces — that have proceeded against the constitutional commitments. The Bolivian constitutional commitments to *suma qamaña* and to the Rights of Mother Earth have been read against the policy decisions of the Morales government (2006–2019) on extractive industry, on the TIPNIS highway through the Indigenous Territory and National Park Isiboro Sécure, and on the broader development model that has prioritized GDP growth funded by extractive revenues. The constitutional commitments are substantive — they are not ornamental and they have been invoked in constitutional litigation with substantive legal effect — but the implementations have repeatedly failed to operationalize what the commitments authorize, and the failures have produced the broader contemporary debate within Latin American political-philosophical circles about whether the constitutional incorporation has accomplished what its architects hoped or has produced a constitutional-progressive cover under which substantive extraction continues.

The honest engagement the project requires of Thread G is therefore three-fold. First, the philosophical content of the Andean tradition — the *ayllu*, the *ayni*, the *Pacha Mama*, the *sumak kawsay* and *suma qamaña* — is engaged at the philosophical depth Estermann, de la Cadena, Acosta, and Gudynas articulate, and that content supplies the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with substantive specifications that the rest of the arc operationalizes. Second, the constitutional incorporation in Ecuador and Bolivia is engaged as the closest existing approximation to biocultural-governance constitutional implementation at the scale of working national polities, with the accomplishments named (the constitutional standing of *Pacha Mama* and *sumak kawsay*, the Rights of Nature jurisprudence that has emerged and that continues to develop, the legislative architecture that has carried the commitments into substantive law) and the limitations named honestly (the implementation gap, the political-economic dynamics that have produced the gap, the analytical questions about what kinds of state-level political-economic transformation would be required to close the gap, and what the gap teaches about the conditions under which biocultural governance can be cultivated into existence at constitutional scale). Third, the Andean traditions' contributions are engaged in their own analytical registers, with the Quechua and Aymara terms preserved as substantive philosophical content (not glossed away into Spanish or English equivalents that flatten the analytical specificity) and with the Andean philosophical tradition positioned as primary articulator of substantive constitutional content rather than as case study within a Western theoretical apparatus.

What the implementation record teaches about the conditions under which biocultural governance can be cultivated at constitutional scale is partly negative: constitutional incorporation alone is not sufficient. The substantive content can carry constitutional standing without producing the substantive transformation in political-economic dynamics that would be required for the constitutional commitments to be operationalized in practice. The cultivation of biocultural governance therefore requires more than constitutional drafting; it requires the cultivation of conditions across the disturbance-regime landscape (Documents II.02, II.04, II.05, II.06, II.07), across the political-economic substrate (Documents X.01 and II.07), across the cognitive-informational substrate (Documents VIII.01 and II.06), and across the material-environmental substrate (Documents IX.01 and II.05) that the constitutional commitments alone cannot produce. The Andean constitutional incorporation supplies both the existence proof — that biocultural-governance constitutional standing is achievable in working polities — and the cautionary record — that constitutional standing alone does not produce the substantive transformation the commitments would authorize. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries both lessons forward.

## Thread H — Pacific Islander Talanoa and Vā, and Maori Whakapapa

The Pacific Islander and Maori philosophical and constitutional traditions supply the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with the substantive contribution of oceanic-relational ontology — the constitutive framing of personhood, community, and polity that proceeds from the analytical primacy of the ocean as constitutive entity rather than as background environment — and with the recent constitutional-transformation document and legal-personhood case study that, together with the Andean incorporations engaged in Thread G, constitute the closest existing approximations to biocultural-governance constitutional implementation at the scale of working polities. The Pacific Islander traditions are engaged through Sitiveni Halapua's writings on *talanoa* as governance practice (developed across his East-West Center papers and policy publications, with key contributions in *Talanoa Process: The Case of Fiji* and subsequent applications to Pacific Climate diplomacy); through Albert Wendt's "Tatauing the Post-Colonial Body" (*Span* 42–43, 1996) and his broader literary and theoretical work on the *vā* as Samoan relational ontology; through Konai Helu Thaman's "Decolonizing Pacific Studies: Indigenous Perspectives, Knowledge, and Wisdom in Higher Education" (*Contemporary Pacific* 15, no. 1, 2003) on the analytical apparatus appropriate to Pacific Islander scholarly engagement; and through the Pacific Climate Warriors corpus and the broader contemporary Pacific Islander climate-governance scholarship. The Maori tradition is engaged through Mason Durie's *Te Mana Te Kāwanatanga: The Politics of Māori Self-Determination* (Oxford NZ, 1998); through Margaret Mutu (Ngāti Kahu/Ngāti Whātua/Te Rarawa) and Moana Jackson (Ngāti Kahungunu/Ngāti Porou), chairs, *Matike Mai Aotearoa: The Report of Matike Mai Aotearoa — The Independent Working Group on Constitutional Transformation* (2016); through the *Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017* as the legal-personhood incorporation; and through Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (Zed, 1999, second edition 2012) on the methodological apparatus that has emerged from the broader kaupapa Māori research tradition.

The *talanoa* deliberative form, articulated principally through Sitiveni Halapua's analytical work, supplies a substantive Pacific Islander deliberative apparatus that operates across Fijian, Tongan, and Samoan traditions with variants and that has been carried into international diplomacy through its adoption as the Talanoa Dialogue under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. Halapua specifies *talanoa* as the open, inclusive, transparent dialogue without preset agenda, in which participants speak from their own experience and perspective, listen at depth to others' contributions, build shared understanding across the dialogue's duration, and proceed toward decisions through the gradual accumulation of relational and analytical resources rather than through adversarial debate and majority vote. The *talanoa* form is not equivalent to consensus deliberation in the Western political-theoretical sense (Habermas's deliberative democracy, the broader Western theoretical tradition); the form proceeds through different analytical commitments — the relations among participants are the principal substantive content, the storytelling and personal-experiential register is the principal analytical register, the temporal duration is structured to allow the relations to develop, and the outcome emerges through the development of the relations rather than through procedural aggregation of preferences. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *talanoa* commitment forward at the level of deliberative-architectural specification: the polity's deliberative apparatuses can include *talanoa*-form deliberation at scales and on substantive matters where the form is appropriate, with the relations-as-substantive-content commitment carried into the deliberative architecture. Documents XII.03 (e-democracy) and XII.05 (neurodivergent polis) operationalize the commitment at the level of deliberative-architectural specification.

The Samoan concept of *vā* — articulated by Albert Wendt and by the broader Samoan philosophical literature including Tui Atua Tupua Tamasese Ta'isi Efi's substantial corpus of articulations of Samoan and broader Polynesian relational ontology — specifies the relational space between entities as the content of relations. The *vā* is not the empty space between things but the relational substance that constitutes the things in relation; one does not have a relation that occupies a *vā*, one has a *vā* that constitutes the relation. The analytical move is parallel to the analytical moves of dependent-origination (Thread E) and of Ubuntu (Thread F) — relations are constitutive of entities rather than supervenient on antecedently existing entities — but the *vā* concept carries distinctive analytical content: the relational substance is itself analytically primary, with the entities being the loci at which the relational substance is articulated. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *vā* commitment forward at the level of constitutional category: the polity's relational substance — the cultivated conditions of relation among its members — is the principal constitutional content, and the entities the polity comprises (citizens, communities, regions, broader-than-human members) are the loci at which the relational substance is articulated rather than the content of which the relations are aggregations.

The oceanic-relational framing the Pacific Islander traditions supply is distinctive among the primary-articulator traditions engaged in this document. The Indigenous-North-American traditions (Threads B and C) operate from continental-ecological grounding, with the analytical apparatus shaped by relationships with the lands, forests, rivers, and prairies that the Indigenous peoples of North America have lived with. The Eastern traditions (Threads D and E) operate from the philosophical-textual traditions of continental East Asia. The Ubuntu tradition (Thread F) operates from the philosophical and social-political tradition of continental sub-Saharan Africa. The Andean tradition (Thread G) operates from the highland-Andean ecological grounding. The Pacific Islander traditions operate from the oceanic-ecological grounding, with the ocean as the principal entity through which the analytical apparatus is shaped. The implications for biocultural governance are substantive. The Pacific Islander analytical apparatus does not treat the polity as a bounded land-based entity with its members concentrated in a bounded territory; the polity's substantive content includes the ocean as constitutive entity, with the polity's members distributed across an oceanic space whose connections among them are the principal substantive content. The contemporary Pacific Islander climate-governance scholarship — through the Pacific Climate Warriors corpus and the broader regional climate-policy scholarship — has carried the oceanic-relational framing into the substantive engagement with climate change as an existential crisis for the Pacific Islander polities whose territories are most directly threatened by sea-level rise, and the analytical apparatus the traditions supply has reshaped the contemporary international climate-governance discourse in ways the broader scholarly literature has not yet fully registered. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the oceanic-relational framing forward at the level of constitutional category: the polity's nested-scales reference frame (Thread L) must accommodate scales at which the principal connective substance is oceanic rather than territorial, and the disturbance-regime landscape (Documents II.02, II.04, II.05, II.06, II.07, II.08) must engage the climate disturbance as constitutive threat to oceanic-grounded polities rather than as one disturbance among many.

The Maori tradition supplies the contemporary moment with two substantive contributions the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries forward at scale. The first is the analytical apparatus of *whakapapa* — the genealogical-relational mapping of the polity through kin, land, ancestors, and cosmos that constitutes the operative analytical apparatus of Maori social-political life. The *whakapapa* is not a family tree in the Western genealogical sense; it is the substantive relational mapping that situates the person, the *iwi* (tribe), the *hapū* (sub-tribe), the *whānau* (extended family), the *marae* (ancestral meeting ground), and the broader-than-human relations (the rivers, mountains, ancestors, gods) in their constitutive relations with one another. Mason Durie's analytical work in *Te Mana Te Kāwanatanga* specifies the content the *whakapapa* apparatus supplies: the polity's substantive content is the relational mapping of its constituents across the dimensions the *whakapapa* tracks, and the polity's task is to sustain the integrity of the mapping across time. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *whakapapa* commitment forward at the level of constitutional category: the polity's nested-scales reference frame operates at scales the *whakapapa* mapping specifies (the *whānau*, *hapū*, *iwi*, and broader scales), and the cultivation of the polity is the cultivation of the relational mapping rather than the management of an institutional apparatus abstracted from the mapping.

*Matike Mai Aotearoa: The Report of Matike Mai Aotearoa — The Independent Working Group on Constitutional Transformation* (2016), chaired by Margaret Mutu and Moana Jackson, is the contemporary constitutional-transformation document of the Maori political tradition. The report — the result of more than two hundred hui (deliberative gatherings) across Aotearoa New Zealand from 2012 through 2015, with substantive Maori participation and authorship — proposes a constitutional transformation that would give substantive constitutional expression to *Te Tiriti o Waitangi* (the Treaty of Waitangi) as the foundational constitutional document of Aotearoa, with the Maori and Pakeha (non-Maori) constitutional spheres operating as parallel and equal rather than with the Pakeha constitutional sphere subordinating the Maori. The report's analytical apparatus proceeds through the *whakapapa* framing — the constitutional architecture is the relational mapping of the polity, and the transformation is the restoration of the relational mapping that the colonial implementation has obscured — and produces a substantive constitutional-architectural proposal whose elements include co-equal Maori and Pakeha constitutional spheres, substantive Maori sovereignty over Maori affairs, substantive Pakeha sovereignty over Pakeha affairs, and a third sphere of shared deliberation on matters that cross the two. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *Matike Mai* framing forward as substantive contemporary articulation of what biocultural-governance constitutional transformation looks like in a polity with a Treaty foundation: the constitutional architecture must accommodate the content the Indigenous polity articulates without subordinating it to the colonial polity's framing, and the cultivation of the conditions under which the two spheres can operate in parallel and equal relation is the constitutional work. The closest existing approximations to biocultural-governance constitutional implementation are therefore the *Matike Mai* proposal (not yet implemented but substantively articulated as deliberatively developed constitutional content), the Ecuadorian and Bolivian incorporations (implemented but unevenly operationalized, engaged in Thread G), and the *Te Awa Tupua* incorporation discussed below.

*Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017* incorporated substantive Maori philosophical-relational content at the level of New Zealand statute, with constitutional weight that has shaped the broader contemporary Maori and broader Aotearoa constitutional discourse. The Act recognizes *Te Awa Tupua* — the Whanganui River, in the Maori framing of the river as a substantive entity with its own life, history, and relations — as a legal person, with substantive rights and with two guardians (*Te Pou Tupua*) appointed jointly by the Maori iwi of the Whanganui catchment and by the Crown to speak and act for the river's interests. The legal-personhood incorporation operates as the closest existing approximation, in a working national polity, to the constitutional translation of the Indigenous animacy commitment (engaged in Kimmerer's analytical apparatus in Thread C and in de la Cadena's analytical apparatus in Thread G) at the level of substantive legal effect. The Act's operative provisions specify that the *Te Pou Tupua* guardians can bring legal action on behalf of the river, that the river's interests must be considered in administrative decisions affecting the catchment, and that the Maori and Crown spheres operate jointly in the guardianship in a way that gives substantive constitutional weight to the Maori spheres' analytical apparatus. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the *Te Awa Tupua* incorporation forward as substantive case study of what legal-personhood-as-ecological-recognition accomplishes and what it does not. The accomplishment is the substantive legal recognition of the river as constitutive entity with constitutional standing; the limitation is the operational restriction of the legal-personhood incorporation to a specific river rather than its extension across the broader ecological landscape, and the analytical questions about what the scaling of the framework to the broader landscape would require remain open. The Colombian Constitutional Court decision T-622/16 — the Atrato River decision — extends the analytical move into Latin American jurisprudence and supplies a parallel case study from a non-Anglophone constitutional tradition.

The *Te Tiriti o Waitangi* / Treaty of Waitangi tradition of constitutional analytical apparatus — developed across the Waitangi Tribunal jurisprudence, through the substantial Maori and non-Maori scholarly literature on Treaty interpretation, and through the contemporary constitutional debates — supplies the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with the specification of how constitutional commitments to relations between settler and Indigenous polities can be operationalized when the original constitutional commitments were articulated in two languages (English and Maori) with substantive differences in what each version commits the parties to, when the implementation has consistently subordinated the Maori-language commitments to the English-language ones, and when the contemporary constitutional task is the restoration of the relational commitments the Maori-language Treaty articulates. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the framing forward at the level of constitutional pedagogy: the project's engagement with the 1776 foundational normative texts of the American constitutional tradition (Documents I.02, I.03, and I.04) proceeds in parallel — the normative commitments the texts articulate have been consistently subordinated by the 1787 implementation and its subsequent constitutional history, the contemporary constitutional task is the restoration of the normative commitments, and the *Matike Mai* framing of the constitutional transformation supplies the contemporary articulation of what such restoration looks like in a polity with a documentary constitutional foundation comparable to (though substantively different from) the American case.

Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (Zed, 1999; second edition 2012) — though developed principally as methodological apparatus for research in Indigenous communities rather than as constitutional theory — supplies the substantive framing through which non-Indigenous engagement with Indigenous traditions can be conducted on terms that do not reproduce the colonial extraction the Indigenous traditions have been subjected to across centuries. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology engages Smith's analytical apparatus principally in conjunction with Document VII.01's specification of multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline, with the recognition that the substantive engagement with the Pacific Islander and Maori traditions in this document requires the methodological discipline Smith articulates and that the engagement's substantive integrity depends on the discipline's substantive application. The thread's preservation of the Maori-language and Pacific-Islander-language terms (rather than their displacement by English-language glosses), the engagement with Maori and Pacific Islander scholars as the principal articulators (rather than with non-Maori and non-Pacific-Islander commentators who have written about the traditions), and the contemporary scholarly mediation through which the engagement proceeds is conducted under Smith's specification of what decolonizing methodological discipline requires.

## Thread I — Black-Radical Ecological Thought

The Black-radical ecological tradition supplies the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with the analytical specification of cultivation-under-disturbance — the content of how a community cultivates the conditions of its life and constitution under conditions of structural disturbance that the design-imposition framings of Western constitutional theory have been unable to register, in part because the framings have produced the very disturbances the Black-radical tradition has been navigating. The tradition is engaged here through Octavia E. Butler's *Parable of the Sower* (Four Walls Eight Windows, 1993) and *Parable of the Talents* (Seven Stories, 1998) as substantive imaginative-political documentation of cultivation-under-disturbance; through adrienne maree brown's *Emergent Strategy: Shaping Change, Changing Worlds* (AK Press, 2017) as substantive contemporary articulation that reads Butler explicitly as cultivation-protocol specification; through Robin D. G. Kelley's *Freedom Dreams: The Black Radical Imagination* (Beacon, 2002) on the longer Black-radical tradition's substantive imaginative-political work; and through the Black-agrarian tradition's contemporary articulators including Leah Penniman's *Farming While Black: Soul Fire Farm's Practical Guide to Liberation on the Land* (Chelsea Green, 2018) and Monica M. White's *Freedom Farmers: Agricultural Resistance and the Black Freedom Movement* (UNC, 2018), with the broader Afrofuturist and Black-speculative-fiction tradition supplying additional substantive content through Sun Ra's analytical-musical-cosmological corpus and Samuel R. Delany's substantial literary and theoretical writings.

Octavia Butler's *Parable of the Sower* (set in a near-future California whose social order has been disrupted by climate change, water scarcity, economic collapse, and political authoritarianism) and *Parable of the Talents* (set across the subsequent decade as the protagonist Lauren Olamina cultivates her Earthseed community across the social-political collapse) supply the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with substantive content the project carries forward at constitutional scale. Butler's analytical work in the *Parable* sequence is not utopian projection of a desired future but substantive documentation of the analytical apparatus a community must develop and the practices it must sustain to cultivate the conditions of its life under structural disturbance. The Earthseed community Olamina cultivates is constituted through substantive practices: the gathering of seeds and the cultivation of food, the sharing of knowledge and the teaching of children, the development of analytical apparatus for understanding the conditions the community faces (the *Earthseed: Books of the Living* the protagonist composes), the navigation of relations with adjacent communities (including hostile ones), the cultivation of internal practices of conflict resolution and decision-making, the integration of the community across racial, cultural, and class differences that the dominant society's design-imposition framings have not been able to integrate, the absorption of disturbances when they arrive (the Camp Christian capture in *Parable of the Talents* and Olamina's substantive analytical response across the years of its operation), and the long-horizon cultivation of conditions the community recognizes will not be fully realized in the lifetimes of its current members.

The analytical apparatus Butler specifies through the Earthseed cultivation is not a fictional embellishment of standard political-theoretical concepts. Butler's substantive analytical work is the specification of cultivation-under-disturbance as substantive constitutional content — what the cultivation requires of the cultivators, what the conditions the cultivation must sustain consist of, what the long-horizon temporal commitment looks like at the scale of practical decision-making, what the integration of plural members under conditions of mutual non-trust requires, and what the absorption of substantial disturbance does to the cultivation as it continues. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries Butler's substantive analytical work forward at the constitutional scale: the cultivation of the polity under conditions of structural disturbance is the constitutional task, the conditions the cultivation must sustain include the conditions for the cultivation itself across generations, the long-horizon temporal commitment must be operationalized at the scale of practical constitutional decision-making, and the absorption of disturbance must be the architecture's substantive content rather than a secondary capacity overlaid on a fundamentally design-imposition architecture.

adrienne maree brown's *Emergent Strategy: Shaping Change, Changing Worlds* (2017) reads Butler explicitly as cultivation-protocol specification and supplies the contemporary articulation of what the cultivation-under-disturbance analytical apparatus requires of organizational and political practice. brown's analytical work proceeds through the specification of emergent-strategy principles drawn from the natural world (the principles of small ferns growing in disturbed soil, of mycelial networks underlying forest communication, of the murmuration patterns of starlings, of the iterative pattern-building of biological evolution) and translated into the practices of contemporary movement-building, organizational design, and community cultivation. brown's specification — that "small is good, small is all," that "what we practice at the small scale sets the patterns for the whole system," that "we are in an imagination battle" with the design-imposition framings of contemporary politics, that "trust the people," that "less prep, more presence," that "there is always enough time for the right work" — supplies a substantive contemporary articulation of cultivation-protocol that the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries forward at constitutional scale. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology does not require the analytical apparatus to remain at the scale of movement-building and community cultivation; it carries the apparatus forward at the level of constitutional commitment, with the cultivation-protocol architecture of Document XIII.01 operationalizing brown's specifications at the scale of the polity's substantive constitutional commitments.

The reading of Butler and brown the project carries forward is at substantive constitutional standing, not at the standing of activist self-help or genre fiction. # Part Four — Arts, Language, and Cultural Production as a Load-Bearing Element of the Biocultural Formation

## Thread N — Cultural Production at Constitutional Weight

The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology specified across Parts One through Three couples biological substrate and cultural plurality at constitutive depth. The coupling raises a question the analytical apex must take up before it proceeds: on the cultural side of the coupling, what is doing the load-bearing work? The answer the project commits to is that arts, language, and cultural production are not ornamentation on the cultural side of the biocultural formation but a load-bearing element of it — the activity through which a culture articulates what it is, transmits that articulation to those who come after, revises it under changed conditions, and continues itself across generations. Cultural production is, in the biocultural-governance ontology, a constitutional rather than a discretionary matter, because a polity that cannot sustain the conditions of its own cultural production cannot sustain the cultural side of the coupling the ontology specifies. The thread is engaged here, after the Western recovery canon and before the analytical apex, because the apex threads — the analytical case, the nested-scales reference frame, and the renovation-versus-decolonial engagement — each presuppose that cultural production carries constitutional weight, and the presupposition must be made explicit before the apex can rest on it.

Raymond Williams's *Culture and Society, 1780–1950* (1958) and *The Country and the City* (1973) supply the analytical foundation for the claim that culture is load-bearing rather than ornamental. Williams traced "culture" as a keyword across a century and a half of English usage, from a mark of distinction — the cultivation of the few, the accomplishment that separated an educated class from the people — toward the meaning the cultural-studies tradition has carried since: culture as a whole way of life of a people. Williams's formulation that culture is ordinary is the formulation the biocultural-governance ontology depends on. Culture, in Williams's recovery, is the lived practice of a people in its work, its language, its relationships, and its making of meaning; it is not a sphere of refined production sealed off from the polity's material life. *The Country and the City* extended the analysis to the cultural imagination of place, showing that the pastoral, the georgic, and the contrasting images of the rural and the urban are not free-floating aesthetic inheritances but representations structured by political-economic relations — that the way a people pictures its own landscape is part of how that landscape is owned, worked, and governed. For the biocultural-governance ontology, Williams's recovery of culture as ordinary and as a whole way of life is precisely the recovery of culture as the thing the coupling specifies: the lived practice through which the polity's biocultural formation is continued, not an adornment upon it.

Stuart Hall, across *Cultural Studies 1983: A Theoretical History* (2016) and the wider Birmingham-school corpus, specified cultural production as a site of meaning-making and contestation rather than as a one-way transmission from producers to an audience. Hall's account of encoding and decoding established that meaning is not simply deposited in a cultural artifact and received intact; it is encoded under one set of conditions and decoded under another, and the gap between them is where cultural struggle happens. The popular, in Hall's analysis, is not a debased residue beneath high culture; it is the terrain on which consent is won, contested, and withheld. For the biocultural-governance ontology, Hall's specification matters because it establishes that cultural production is not a settled inheritance a polity simply possesses and passes down unchanged. It is an ongoing activity, continuously made and contested, and the conditions under which the making and the contesting proceed are conditions the constitutional architecture is responsible for cultivating. A polity whose cultural production has been captured — its meaning-making concentrated in a few hands, its contestation foreclosed — has lost not an amenity but a constitutive capacity.

Language is the clearest case of cultural production as a load-bearing element, and the case where the constitutional stakes are most exposed. A language is not a neutral medium through which a culture is expressed; it is the carrier of a people's epistemology, its law, its relation to place, and its kinship structure — the structure within which the biocultural inheritance is actually held. The biocultural-diversity literature engaged in Part One established that linguistic diversity and biological diversity are coupled across the Earth's surface; Thread N specifies the constitutional implication that the literature leaves implicit. The institutions through which a language reproduces across generations are constitutional cultivation conditions, and a people deprived of those institutions has had the cultural side of its biocultural coupling severed at the root. The Indigenous-language-revitalization movements stand at primary-articulator standing on this point, engaged here in their own register rather than as illustrations of the Williams-Hall cultural-studies frame. The Kōhanga Reo movement — Māori-language "language nests" founded in 1982, in which kaumātua immerse the youngest children in te reo Māori from infancy — and the Kura Kaupapa Māori schooling movement that followed rebuilt the intergenerational transmission of te reo after a century of colonial schooling had broken it. The engagement here coordinates with the Maori Whakapapa material of Thread H, where *Matike Mai Aotearoa* (2016) specifies the constitutional transformation within which te reo revitalization sits: language revitalization is not a cultural program adjacent to constitutional transformation but part of its substance. The Hawaiian-language-revitalization movement, through the Pūnana Leo immersion preschools founded in 1983 and the Hawaiian-medium schooling that followed, rebuilt the transmission of ʻōlelo Hawaiʻi after its near-elimination under a century of suppression. Leanne Hinton's *How to Keep Your Language Alive* (2002) documents the master-apprentice method through which languages with very few remaining fluent speakers are transmitted directly, elder to learner, outside the institutions of mass schooling. These movements specify language not as heritage to be preserved in an archive but as a living cultivation condition: the institutions of transmission are what keep a language a carrier of the biocultural inheritance rather than a record of one.

The Black Arts Movement, through Amiri Baraka and the movement's wider corpus of the 1960s and 1970s, articulated cultural production as constitutive of political self-determination — the insistence that a people's art is part of how it constitutes and governs itself rather than a supplement to its politics. Gloria Anzaldúa's *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza* (1987), writing across English, Spanish, and Nahuatl within a single text, refused the monolingual capture of the cultural substrate and specified the borderland and the mixed tongue as sites of cultural production that no single national culture can enclose. The specification bears directly on the biocultural-governance ontology's commitment to plurality, since the borderland is where the cultural side of the coupling is most visibly plural and least reducible to a single register — and a constitutional architecture that recognizes cultural production as load-bearing must recognize the borderland's mixed production as constitutional rather than as a failure to assimilate. bell hooks's *Art on My Mind: Visual Politics* (1995) specified the making and the seeing of art as political practice and refused the separation of aesthetic life from constitutional life, insisting that a people's access to the making and the seeing of art is a matter of political consequence. Each of these articulations is engaged in its own register; none is absorbed into the Williams-Hall cultural-studies frame, and the Indigenous-language-revitalization tradition in particular is engaged as a primary articulator of what language-as-cultivation-condition means rather than as an instance of a theory specified elsewhere.

What Thread N specifies for the analytical apex that follows can now be stated. Cultural production is one of the conditions a constitutional architecture under the cultivation framing must cultivate. It belongs in the nested-scales reference frame of Thread L as a cultivation condition with its own scale-matched institutions — the language nest at the scale of the household and the youngest children, the school at the scale of the community, the publishing and broadcasting institutions at larger scales — and it belongs in the analytical case of Thread K as one of the living, adaptive components whose self-organizing activity the architecture must cultivate rather than specify in advance. And the project's refusal of synthesis-into-architecture, which Thread M defends under the renovation-versus-decolonial framing, is itself a commitment about cultural production: to refuse to synthesize the primary-articulator traditions into a single architectural register is to commit to maintaining the conditions under which plural cultural production continues, each tradition continuing to make and revise its own meaning in its own register rather than being absorbed into a meaning the synthesizer controls. Thread N's diagnostic counterpart — how the cultural-disturbance regime commodifies and captures cultural production — is the analytical work of Document II.02; the built spaces and environments in which cultural production happens are the analytical work of Document VI.05; and the comparison gestures across the traditions Thread N engages are housed in Document XIV.01. Thread N's work here is the ontological one: to establish that cultural production carries constitutional weight as a load-bearing element of the biocultural formation, and therefore that its cultivation conditions are constitutional conditions the apex threads must treat as such.

## Thread K — The Analytical Case for Biocultural Governance as Constitutional Ontology

The proposal that biocultural governance can serve as the constitutional ontology of a polity requires analytical defense at the level of constitutional ontology rather than at the level of policy preference, normative aspiration, or empirical hypothesis. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology is not a recommendation that the polity's constitutional architecture incorporate biocultural considerations alongside its existing commitments, that the polity's policy apparatus give greater weight to biocultural concerns than it currently does, or that the polity's normative commitments be expanded to include biocultural sensibilities. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology is the claim that the polity *is* a biocultural formation — a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales whose biological substrate and cultural plurality are coupled at constitutive depth — and that the polity's constitutional architecture must operate from recognition of what the polity is rather than from a competing ontological framing whose substantive content the polity's actual existence contradicts. The analytical case proceeds through three moves: specification of the failure modes of design-imposition framings in constitutional theory, specification of the analytical limits of social-contract reasoning at scale, and specification of the necessity of feedback and cultivation conditions in any system whose components are themselves living and adaptive. Each move operates as analytical commitment rather than as policy critique.

### The Failure Modes of Design-Imposition Framings

Design-imposition framings of constitutional theory hold that the constitutional architecture is the product of design work by constitutional architects who specify the institutional arrangements the polity will operate under, that the content of the constitutional commitments is given by the design specifications the architects produce, and that the polity's task is to operate within the institutional arrangements the design specifies. The framing carries substantial historical pedigree across the Western constitutional tradition — from the design-imposition work of the American framers at Philadelphia in 1787, through the design-imposition work of the French revolutionary constitutional projects of the 1790s, through the constitutional projects across nineteenth and twentieth centuries that have proceeded from the same premise that constitutional architecture is the product of design work. The framing carries intuitive appeal because its proximate analogues in the broader Western technical-design tradition — engineering, architecture, software design — appear to operate successfully on the same basis. The constitutional architect specifies the structure, the polity operates within it, and the content of the institutional arrangements is given by what the architect specified.

The framing's failure modes proceed through three analytical pathways. The first is the imposed institutional architecture's inability to track the polity's adaptive substrate. The polity is constituted in the relations among its biocultural members — the relations through which persons are constituted, through which communities are constituted, through which broader-than-human relations are constituted, through which cultural inheritance is constituted, through which the economic-material substrate is constituted. The relations are the content of what the polity is rather than a substrate over which institutional arrangements operate. The content evolves continuously as the polity's members navigate their conditions, develop new practices, encounter new disturbances, respond to new opportunities, integrate new members, and lose members through death and migration. The evolution proceeds at multiple temporal scales — the daily and seasonal scales at which immediate practical decisions are made, the generational scale at which cultural and demographic transitions occur, the longer scales at which ecological and political-economic transformations unfold. The imposed institutional architecture is, by the design-imposition framing's own commitments, specified in advance of the evolution and is not capable of tracking the evolution as it proceeds. The consequence is cumulative drift between specified institutional arrangements and the actual content of the polity's existence — a drift that accumulates across the constitutional period and that produces, over generations, an institutional apparatus whose operation is increasingly disconnected from the polity it nominally governs. The American case across the post-1787 constitutional period supplies the empirical record: the polity has transformed across two-and-a-half centuries through industrialization, urbanization, demographic transformation, the Civil War and Reconstruction, the New Deal, the civil-rights transformations, the contemporary political-economic restructurings; the institutional architecture specified in 1787 has been amended twenty-seven times across the period but has substantially preserved its fundamental design specifications; and the disconnection between institutional apparatus and polity has produced the systemic dysfunctions that the project's diagnostic engages across the broader arc.

The second analytical pathway is the path-dependence of design choices made under assumptions the polity then outgrows or undermines. The constitutional architects of 1787 made design choices — the equal-state Senate, the Electoral College, the Article V amendment threshold, the lifetime federal judiciary, the three-fifths compromise, the structure of the executive and legislative branches — under assumptions about the polity they were governing. The assumptions included that the polity was a federation of substantially equal states whose political-economic life was conducted at the state scale, that the citizenry was a relatively homogeneous Protestant white male propertied population, that the economy operated through agricultural production with some commercial development, that the territorial extent would not substantially expand beyond the original thirteen states, and that the operating conditions of the polity would remain within parameters the architects could anticipate. Each of these assumptions was substantially falsified across the constitutional period — the polity expanded continentally across the nineteenth century, the population transformed through immigration and demographic change, the economy transformed through industrialization and post-industrial restructuring, the state-scale assumption was undermined by the national-scale political-economic dynamics that emerged across the period — but the design specifications made under the assumptions persisted because the Article V amendment process required supermajority political coalitions to revise specifications that increasingly served minority interests against the contemporary polity's expressed preferences. The consequence is a constitutional architecture whose design specifications were chosen for a polity that no longer exists and whose modification requires the consent of the minority interests the design specifications now disproportionately serve. The path-dependence is a feature of the design-imposition framing rather than a contingent failure of the American case: any constitutional architecture specified in advance of the evolution of the polity it governs will produce path-dependence problems as the polity evolves beyond the conditions under which the specifications were chosen.

The third analytical pathway is the inadequacy of design specifications relative to the complexity of the polity. The polity is, as the primary-articulator traditions and the Western complex-adaptive-systems corpus together specify, a complex adaptive system whose content includes interactions across multiple scales, emergent dynamics that cannot be predicted from analysis of constituent elements, feedback loops at multiple scales and temporal horizons, non-linear responses to changing conditions, and co-evolution among the polity's biological-cultural-political-economic components. The design specifications a constitutional architect can produce — whether the architect operates from the technical-design tradition of contemporary policy analysis or from the intellectual tradition of constitutional design — are, in analytical content, simpler than the complexity of the polity the specifications are meant to govern. The consequence is that the design specifications cannot adequately specify the operations of the polity under the conditions it actually faces, and the gap between the specifications and the polity must be filled by practices the specifications do not specify — customary practices, bureaucratic-administrative practices, interpretive practices through which the formal specifications are made operational. The practices that fill the gap are the content of how the constitutional architecture actually operates, but the practices are not themselves specified by the constitutional architecture; they emerge as the practical adaptation of the design specifications to the conditions the polity actually faces. The design-imposition framing therefore systematically underspecifies the content of how the constitutional architecture operates, and the content that fills the underspecification is shaped by the interests and analytical apparatuses of the actors who fill it — disproportionately, the interests of the minority groups whose institutional access advantages allow them to shape the customary, bureaucratic-administrative, and interpretive practices that the design specifications underspecify.

The analytical conclusion the three pathways together support is that design-imposition framings of constitutional theory produce failure modes the framings cannot internally address. The disconnect between specified architecture and actual polity is not a contingent failure of particular design specifications but a feature of the design-imposition approach itself, and the remedy is not the production of better design specifications but the transition to a different constitutional ontology — one that does not specify the institutional architecture in advance of the polity's evolution and that operates instead through the cultivation of conditions under which the polity's content can continue to develop while constitutional commitments are sustained across the development. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology supplies the alternative, with the content of the alternative supplied by the primary-articulator traditions whose constitutional practice across centuries has operated through cultivation of conditions rather than through specification of architecture.

### The Analytical Limits of Social-Contract Reasoning at Scale

The constitutional ontology the design-imposition framing principally operates under in the Western tradition is the social-contract framework. The framework holds that the polity is constituted through an agreement (real or hypothetical) among the polity's members, with the content of the agreement supplying the content of the polity's constitutional commitments. The framework's articulation varies across its principal articulators — Hobbes specifying the agreement as the transfer of natural rights to the sovereign in exchange for the sovereign's protection of security; Locke specifying the agreement as the instituting of government for the protection of antecedent natural rights; Rousseau specifying the agreement as the constitution of the general will through which the content of the polity is produced; Rawls specifying the agreement as the product of deliberation behind the veil of ignorance — but the structural commitment is shared: the polity is constituted through agreement, the content of the agreement supplies the content of the polity, and the constitutional task is the specification of the agreement's terms.

The framework operates with analytical force at the scale of small-group decision-making — small communities, voluntary associations, small-scale political units where face-to-face deliberation among the members can produce agreement on terms. The analytical limits emerge at the scale of modern polities whose populations exceed thresholds beyond which face-to-face deliberation is no longer practicable. The limits proceed through four pathways. The first is the cognitive-and-deliberative impossibility of contract-formation across the population scales modern polities operate at. The population of the contemporary American polity is roughly 335 million, beyond the scale at which face-to-face deliberation can occur among the polity's members, beyond the scale at which the members can know one another, beyond the scale at which the interests and analytical apparatuses of the members can be articulated in deliberation through which the contract would be formed, and beyond the scale at which the consent of the members can be obtained. The contract-formation the framework presupposes cannot occur at the scale of the polity it is meant to govern, and the analytical work the framework purports to do — supplying constitutional commitments through agreement — cannot be performed at the scale required. The framework operates at the scale of modern polities as analytical fiction whose function is the legitimation of constitutional commitments produced through other processes that the framework does not specify.

The second pathway is cognitive-bias amplification in large-population deliberative settings. The empirical work on cognitive bias in deliberation — through Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect-theory corpus, through Cass Sunstein's work on group polarization, through the broader empirical literature on deliberative dynamics — supplies the analytical apparatus through which cognitive biases are amplified rather than corrected in large-population deliberative settings. The consequence is that the deliberation through which the contract would be formed, even if the scale problem were somehow overcome, would produce contract terms shaped by cognitive-bias amplification dynamics rather than by the interests and analytical apparatuses of the polity's members. The contract-formation would not produce commitments reflecting agreement; it would produce commitments reflecting cognitive-bias-amplified group dynamics, with the interests and analytical apparatuses of the members systematically distorted in deliberation. Document VIII.01 (Part VIII) engages the cognitive-bias-at-scale dynamics at depth as content for the broader arc.

The third pathway is the systematic exclusions the social-contract reasoning relies on. The contract-formation the framework presupposes requires participants who can participate in deliberation — adults of sound mind with standing to enter into contracts. The members of the polity who cannot participate under those requirements include the children whose interests are nonetheless bound by the contract, the future generations whose interests are likewise bound but who cannot participate, the non-human kin whose interests are bound but who cannot participate, the cognitively disabled members whose participation requires accommodations the framework does not specify, and the disenfranchised members whose standing has been denied for periods (the enslaved population through 1865, women through 1920, eighteen-to-twenty-year-olds through 1971, and the contemporary disenfranchisement of populations through felony disenfranchisement, voter-suppression dynamics, and the structural barriers to participation the project's diagnostic engages). Charles Mills's *The Racial Contract* (Cornell, 1997) supplies the analytical apparatus through which the racial-exclusion dynamics of the social-contract tradition can be named — the contract that has governed the modern polity has been a contract among racially-marked participants whose contract terms have excluded racially-marked others, and the analytical apparatus of the social-contract framework has obscured the exclusion through its presentation of the contract as universal. Carole Pateman's *The Sexual Contract* (Polity, 1988) supplies the parallel analytical apparatus through which the gender-exclusion dynamics can be named, and Pateman and Mills's collaborative *Contract and Domination* (Polity, 2007) supplies the integration of the two critiques. The consequence is that the social-contract framework systematically excludes members of the polity whose interests must nonetheless be bound by the constitutional commitments, and the exclusion is not a contingent failure of particular contract specifications but a feature of the contract-form itself.

The fourth pathway is the historical record of social-contract foundings as foundings of dispossession rather than as foundings of consent. The empirical record of constitutional foundings across the modern period — the American founding of 1787, the French revolutionary foundings of the 1790s, the Latin American post-colonial foundings of the early nineteenth century, the twentieth-century foundings across decolonization, the recent foundings in Eastern Europe and elsewhere — uniformly proceeds through dispossession of populations whose consent was not obtained: Indigenous dispossession at the American founding, enslaved-population dispossession at the American and French foundings, subaltern-population dispossession at the Latin American foundings, ethnic-and-class dispossessions at the twentieth-century foundings. The social-contract framework has operated as legitimation apparatus for the foundings rather than as analytical description of consent-formation, and the analytical work the framework purports to do is contradicted by the empirical record of how foundings actually proceed. The consequence for constitutional ontology: the social-contract framework cannot serve as the ontological grounding of the constitutional architecture because the ontological commitments the framework asserts (consent of the governed, equal participation of the members, antecedent natural rights protected through agreement) are contradicted by the empirical record of how constitutional polities are constituted.

The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology supplies the alternative grounding. The polity is constituted not through contract but through cultivation across generations of biocultural members whose constitutive relations among one another and with broader-than-human members supply the constitutional content. The constitutional architecture's task is not the specification of contract terms but the maintenance of the cultivation conditions under which the constitutive relations can continue. The constitutional commitments do not require contract-formation across impossible population scales because they emerge through cultivation across generations, do not require contract-formation through cognitive-bias-amplified large-population deliberation because they operate through multi-scale practices at practicable scales, do not systematically exclude members of the polity because they include all the members (children, future generations, non-human kin, the cognitively disabled, the disenfranchised) at the scale at which each member can participate, and do not legitimize foundings of dispossession because they reject the design-imposition framing under which foundings of dispossession have occurred.

### Feedback and Cultivation Conditions in Systems Whose Components Are Living and Adaptive

The analytical case for biocultural governance as constitutional ontology requires specification of why feedback and cultivation conditions are necessary in any system whose components are themselves living and adaptive. The specification proceeds through three bodies of analytical work the project draws on. The first is the complex-adaptive-systems literature on adaptive systems' dependence on feedback (Holling, the Resilience Alliance corpus, the broader Santa Fe Institute and complex-adaptive-systems literature engaged in Document V.01). The empirical content of the corpus is that adaptive systems maintain their functional integrity through feedback mechanisms that allow the systems to detect departures from functional states and adjust their operations to restore those states. The analytical content the corpus supplies is the specification that feedback mechanisms are not optional features that adaptive systems can operate without; they are constitutive features whose absence eliminates the adaptive capacity, and the system without feedback is not an adaptive system but an open-loop system whose operations cumulate departures from functional states until system collapse occurs. The consequence for constitutional ontology: the polity is a complex adaptive system, and the constitutional architecture must incorporate feedback mechanisms at scales appropriate to the feedback the polity's adaptive operations require. The design-imposition constitutional framings do not incorporate feedback at the scales required; they specify institutional arrangements at single moments (constitutional foundings, amendments) and operate periodic adjustments through electoral cycles and judicial review, with feedback occurring at coarser scales than the operational dynamics of the polity require. The consequence is the cumulative drift and systemic dysfunction the design-imposition critique above engages.

The second body of analytical work is the ecological literature on cultivation conditions for community succession. The empirical content of the corpus is that ecological communities develop their composition and functional integrity through succession dynamics in which earlier-successional species prepare site conditions under which later-successional species can establish, and the cultivation of site conditions appropriate to desired late-successional communities is the operational practice of ecological restoration. The analytical content the corpus supplies is the specification that ecological communities cannot be designed-into-existence through specification of the community's composition; they develop through succession under cultivation conditions, and the practical task is the cultivation of the conditions rather than the specification of the outcome. The consequence for constitutional ontology: the polity's constitutional commitments cannot be designed-into-existence through specification of institutional arrangements; they develop through cultivation under conditions the constitutional architecture is responsible for maintaining, and the constitutional task is the cultivation of the conditions rather than the specification of the institutional outcomes.

The third body of analytical work is the developmental-biology and developmental-systems literature on the emergence of structure under conditions rather than under blueprint. The empirical content — through contributions from C. H. Waddington on epigenetic landscapes, Susan Oyama on developmental-systems theory (*The Ontogeny of Information*, Cambridge, 1985), Mary Jane West-Eberhard on developmental plasticity (*Developmental Plasticity and Evolution*, Oxford, 2003), and the broader developmental-biology literature — is that biological structure emerges through developmental processes under conditions rather than being specified in advance through genetic blueprint. The analytical content the corpus supplies is the specification that biological systems whose components are themselves living entities — cells, tissues, organs, organisms — develop their structure through interactions among components under conditions, with the structure being the emergent product of cumulative interactions rather than the realized specification of an antecedent blueprint. The consequence for constitutional ontology: the polity, whose components are themselves living and adaptive members (persons, communities, broader-than-human members), develops its constitutional structure through interactions among the members under conditions, with the structure being the emergent product of cumulative interactions rather than the realized specification of antecedent constitutional design. The design-imposition constitutional framings operate under the analytical apparatus of blueprint-realization, which is appropriate to engineering of non-living artifacts and inappropriate to systems whose components are living and adaptive. The consequence is the analytical inadequacy the design-imposition framings produce when applied to polities.

The analytical case the three bodies of work together support is that the constitutional architecture of polities must operate as cultivation of conditions rather than as specification of institutional arrangements, and that the constitutional ontology under which the architecture operates must recognize the polity as a complex adaptive system constituted through living and adaptive members. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology supplies the specification: the polity is a biocultural formation, the constitutional architecture cultivates the conditions of the biocultural formation's continuance, and the constitutional commitments emerge and develop through cumulative practices under the cultivation conditions rather than being specified in advance through design imposition. The analytical case is therefore not that the biocultural-governance commitment is preferable on normative grounds; the case is that the commitment is required by the nature of the polity as a complex adaptive system, and the design-imposition alternatives fail because they operate under ontological commitments the polity's nature contradicts.

## Thread L — The Nested-Scales Reference Frame as Constitutional Category

The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates through a specific reference frame at the level of constitutional category: the nested-scales reference frame against which decision rights are matched. The reference frame is articulated through spatial scales (biosphere, ecozone, ecosystem, habitat, population, organism, organ system) and temporal analogs (geological time, ecological-succession time, generational time, lifetime, decade, year, decision-cycle time). The reference frame is not a metaphor extended from ecology to politics; it is a constitutional category that specifies what kinds of decisions are appropriately made at what scales, what kinds of decision-makers are appropriate to each scale, what kinds of feedback are required at each scale, and what kinds of cross-scale relations the constitutional architecture is responsible for sustaining. The thread engages the reference frame at the depth at which it operates as constitutional category, distinguishes it from scale-reduction (federalism's compromise) and from scale-blindness (the unitary state's overreach), and identifies what the reference frame authorizes that other constitutional ontologies do not.

The cross-scale-governance literature supplies the analytical foundation for the reference frame as constitutional category rather than as metaphorical extension. David Cash, William Adger, Fikret Berkes, Po Garden, Louis Lebel, Per Olsson, Lowell Pritchard, and Oran Young's "Scale and Cross-Scale Dynamics: Governance and Information in a Multilevel World" (*Ecology and Society* 11, no. 2, 2006) supplies the analytical specification of three failure modes that produce systematic mismatches between governance scales and the scales at which problems operate: ignorance (the governance apparatus does not recognize the scale at which the problem operates), mismatch (the governance apparatus operates at a scale different from the scale at which the problem operates), and plurality (the governance apparatus cannot address the multiplicity of scales at which the problem operates simultaneously). The empirical record Cash and colleagues assemble across the paper documents the failure modes across a substantial range of cases — fisheries management, biodiversity conservation, water resources, climate change. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology carries the analytical content forward at the level of constitutional architecture: the architecture must avoid scale ignorance through recognition of the scales at which polity-relevant dynamics operate, must avoid scale mismatch through matching of decision rights to operative scales, and must avoid the failure to address plurality through constitutional commitments at multiple scales operating in coordinated rather than conflicting relation.

Louis Lebel, Po Garden, and Masao Imamura's "The Politics of Scale, Position, and Place in the Governance of Water Resources in the Mekong Region" (*Ecology and Society* 10, no. 2, 2005) supplies the empirical-case study of how cross-scale governance dynamics operate in a substantial regional case — the Mekong basin, with its interactions among Chinese, Burmese, Lao, Thai, Cambodian, and Vietnamese governance apparatuses at the national scale, with river-basin-management apparatuses operating across national boundaries, with sub-national governance apparatuses operating at provincial and local scales, and with Indigenous and rural-community governance apparatuses operating at scales the formal-state apparatuses often do not recognize. The analytical content the paper develops — that scale, position, and place are political categories rather than purely empirical ones, that the choice of scale at which a problem is addressed is itself a political decision with consequences for which actors are recognized as relevant decision-makers, and that the constitutional architecture's task is the cultivation of conditions under which the multiple scales of governance can operate in coordinated rather than competitive relation — supplies the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology with content at the constitutional category of nested scales. Fikret Berkes and Carl Folke's edited *Linking Social and Ecological Systems: Management Practices and Social Mechanisms for Building Resilience* (Cambridge, 1998) supplies the broader analytical-empirical record of how social-ecological systems operate across nested scales, and Elinor Ostrom and Vincent Ostrom's corpus on polycentricity supplies the analytical specification of how multiple decision-centers at different scales can operate in coordinated rather than hierarchical relation.

The spatial scales the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates through are drawn from ecological-analytical apparatus and supply content the constitutional architecture operationalizes. The biosphere scale is the global-ecological scale at which planetary-system dynamics operate — carbon and water cycles, biogeochemical flows, climate dynamics, global-scale biological dynamics. The decision rights at the biosphere scale are global-scale commitments to the conditions of planetary-system continuance, with empirical decision-content that operates across multi-generational temporal horizons. Contemporary constitutional architectures do not incorporate biosphere-scale decision rights as constitutional commitments; the contemporary international-environmental-agreement apparatus operates as supplementary apparatus that the constitutional architectures of national polities can choose to engage or not engage. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates differently: the polity's constitutional commitments include biosphere-scale commitments to conditions of planetary continuance, with operational content carried through the constitutional architecture's commitments at the biosphere scale and through coordination among polities at the biosphere scale. Document IX.01 (Part III) engages the biosphere-scale climate-disturbance content at depth.

The ecozone scale is the sub-continental-ecological scale at which ecozone-characteristic dynamics operate — boreal-forest dynamics, temperate-broadleaf-forest dynamics, grassland dynamics, desert dynamics, coastal-marine dynamics, tropical-forest dynamics, arctic-and-alpine dynamics. The decision rights at the ecozone scale are ecozone-coordinated commitments to the conditions of ecozone continuance, with operational content that crosses political-boundary divisions and operates at multi-generational temporal horizons. Contemporary constitutional architectures operate principally at the national-political-boundary scale rather than at the ecozone scale, and the consequence is cumulative drift between national-political-boundary decisions and ecozone-coordinated requirements. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates with ecozone-scale decision rights as constitutional category, with operational coordination among polities sharing ecozones supplying the cross-polity coordination ecozone-scale dynamics require.

The ecosystem scale is the regional-ecological scale at which ecosystem-characteristic dynamics operate — watershed dynamics, coastal-estuary dynamics, mountain-and-valley-system dynamics, specific-ecosystem-type dynamics. The decision rights at the ecosystem scale are ecosystem-scale commitments to the conditions of ecosystem continuance, with operational content that operates at generational temporal horizons. Contemporary constitutional architectures operate principally at the provincial-and-state scale, which does not match the ecosystem scale, and the consequence is cumulative drift between provincial-and-state decisions and ecosystem-coordinated requirements. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates with ecosystem-scale decision rights as constitutional category, with watershed-and-coastal-and-mountain-and-other-ecosystem-scale apparatuses supplying the ecosystem-scale coordination.

The habitat scale is the sub-regional-ecological scale at which habitat-characteristic dynamics operate — forest-patch dynamics, wetland-area dynamics, grassland-patch dynamics, urban-habitat dynamics. The decision rights at the habitat scale are habitat-scale commitments to the conditions of habitat continuance, with operational content that operates at decadal-to-generational temporal horizons. Contemporary constitutional architectures operate principally at county-and-municipal scale, which does not match the habitat scale, and the consequence is failure to coordinate habitat-scale dynamics. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates with habitat-scale decision rights as constitutional category, with habitat-coordinated apparatuses supplying the habitat-scale coordination.

The population scale is the community-and-locality-scale at which community-characteristic dynamics operate — village-and-town dynamics, urban-neighborhood dynamics, community-of-place dynamics. The decision rights at the population scale are community-scale commitments to the conditions of community continuance, with operational content that operates at yearly-to-decadal temporal horizons. Contemporary constitutional architectures operate with community-scale governance through municipal and sub-municipal apparatuses, with substantial accomplishment in some cases but with failure modes the project's diagnostic engages. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates with population-scale decision rights as constitutional category, with enhanced community-scale apparatuses supplying the community-scale coordination Documents XII.09 through XII.12 specify.

The organism scale is the household-and-individual-life scale at which individual-and-household dynamics operate — household-economic dynamics, individual-life dynamics, family-and-extended-kin dynamics. The decision rights at the organism scale are individual-and-household-scale commitments to the conditions of individual-and-household continuance, with operational content that operates at yearly-and-lifetime temporal horizons. Contemporary constitutional architectures operate with individual-rights protection at the organism scale through constitutional-rights apparatuses, with substantial accomplishment in some cases and failures the project's diagnostic engages. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates with organism-scale decision rights that include individual-rights protection but extend to household-and-extended-kin commitments the design-imposition framings have not adequately recognized.

The organ-system scale is the within-organism scale at which within-individual dynamics operate — bodily-health dynamics, cognitive dynamics, psychological-spiritual dynamics. The decision rights at the organ-system scale are individual-bodily-and-cognitive-and-psychological-spiritual commitments, with operational content that operates at daily-to-yearly temporal horizons. Contemporary constitutional architectures protect bodily-integrity and freedom-of-conscience commitments at the organ-system scale, with substantial accomplishment and failures the project's diagnostic engages. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates with organ-system-scale commitments that include bodily-integrity and freedom-of-conscience but extend to cognitive-architectural commitments Document VIII.01 (Part VIII) operationalizes and psychological-spiritual commitments the design-imposition framings have not adequately recognized.

The temporal scales operate as analogs to the spatial scales rather than as independent categories. The geological-time temporal analog corresponds to the biosphere spatial scale — multi-millennial and geological-epoch temporal horizons over which biosphere-scale dynamics operate. The ecological-succession-time temporal analog corresponds to the ecozone and ecosystem spatial scales — century-to-millennial temporal horizons over which ecosystem-and-ecozone-scale dynamics operate. The generational-time temporal analog corresponds to the habitat-and-population spatial scales — multi-generational temporal horizons over which habitat-and-community-scale dynamics operate, with the Haudenosaunee seven-generations principle (Thread B) supplying the constitutional commitment to the temporal scale. The lifetime temporal analog corresponds to the organism spatial scale — multi-decadal temporal horizons over which individual-and-household dynamics operate. The decade-and-year temporal analogs correspond to the organism-and-organ-system spatial scales — yearly-to-decadal temporal horizons over which individual-and-household and within-individual dynamics operate. The decision-cycle-time temporal analog operates as shorter-than-yearly temporal horizon for operational decision-making at practicable scales.

The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates through scale-matching of decision rights — decisions at scales appropriate to the dynamics the decisions engage. The scale-matching is distinguished from two alternatives constitutional theories have operated with. The first is scale-reduction — the federalism compromise that reduces constitutional decision-making to two-or-three principal scales (the federal, the state, the local) and maps decisions onto the scales through subject-matter divisions rather than through scale-matching to dynamics. The American federal system operates through scale-reduction: decisions are assigned to federal, state, or local levels based on constitutional-textual assignments and subsequent judicial interpretations, with subject-matter divisions (interstate commerce, foreign affairs, taxation, and so on) governing the assignments. The consequence is systematic mismatch between decision scales and dynamics scales — decisions made at scales that do not match the scales at which the dynamics operate. The ecozone-scale climate dynamics, for instance, cross national boundaries and require coordination at scales the federal-state-local framework does not provide; the habitat-scale conservation dynamics cross municipal boundaries and require coordination at scales the framework does not provide. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates through scale-matching that respects the plurality of scales at which the dynamics operate, with constitutional commitments at each scale and coordination mechanisms among the scales that the scale-reduction framework does not produce.

The second alternative is scale-blindness — the unitary-state framework that operates decisions at the national scale without recognition of the plurality of scales at which the dynamics operate. The consequence is even more pronounced systematic mismatch — decisions at the national scale that do not match any of the scales at which the dynamics operate, with cumulative drift between national decisions and scale-specific requirements producing systemic dysfunction across scales. Contemporary unitary-state polities suffer the consequences. The biocultural-governance constitutional ontology operates through scale-matching that rejects the scale-blindness premise that constitutional decisions can be made at single scales without recognition of the plurality of scales at which the dynamics operate.

What the nested-scales reference frame authorizes that other constitutional ontologies do not is the constitutional category of scale-matched decision rights — decisions at scales appropriate to dynamics, with constitutional commitments at each scale, with coordination mechanisms among scales, with temporal commitments matching temporal-scale analogs, and with recognition that the constitutional architecture's operation across scales is the constitutional content the design-imposition framings at single scales cannot produce. The bounded-decision-rights architecture of Documents XII.09 through XII.12 operationalizes the scale-matched commitments at specific decision-domain scales. The cross-scale coordination mechanisms required for the operational integrity of the scale-matched commitments are cultivated through the constitutional architecture's ongoing operations rather than specified in advance through single design-imposition. The cultivation-protocol architecture of Document XIII.01 operationalizes the cultivation conditions under which the cross-scale coordination can continue.

## Thread M — Renovation-Versus-Decolonial Under Cultivation-Rather-Than-Design-Renovation Framing

The project's analytical move — proposing biocultural governance as the polity's constitutional ontology, drawing on Indigenous and Eastern traditions at primary-articulator standing, advanced from a non-Indigenous and non-Eastern authorial location — must engage the decolonial critique of synthesis-as-appropriation at analytical depth rather than at the level of gestural acknowledgment. The decolonial critique has been articulated across the past several decades through substantial scholarly work: Walter D. Mignolo's *The Darker Side of Western Modernity: Global Futures, Decolonial Options* (Duke, 2011) and his broader Modernity/Coloniality corpus; Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (Zed, 1999, second edition 2012); Aníbal Quijano's "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America" (*Nepantla* 1, no. 3, 2000); Boaventura de Sousa Santos's *Epistemologies of the South: Justice against Epistemicide* (Paradigm/Routledge, 2014); and Catherine Walsh's work in the Modernity/Coloniality tradition. The critique's analytical content is that the modern Western intellectual tradition has consistently engaged non-Western traditions through a structural pattern — appropriating selected elements while declining to engage the traditions on their own analytical terms, producing syntheses that subordinate the appropriated content to the Western analytical apparatus, and presenting the syntheses as universally valid contributions to a single intellectual tradition the Western analytical apparatus implicitly governs. The pattern produces what Quijano specifies as the coloniality of power and what Santos specifies as epistemicide — the structural subordination and ongoing eradication of non-Western ways of knowing under the cover of integration into a putatively universal Western intellectual tradition. The decolonial critique therefore holds that intellectual projects proposing to integrate Indigenous and Eastern traditions into substantive analytical-political frameworks advanced from non-Indigenous and non-Eastern authorial locations are, regardless of the authors' intentions, structurally participating in the pattern the critique identifies, and that the analytical work the projects purport to do is contradicted by the structural position the projects occupy. The critique's force is that good intentions and scholarly engagement are not sufficient to escape the pattern; the pattern operates at structural levels the intentions and engagement of individual authors cannot, on their own, alter.

The project must engage the strongest version of the critique honestly. The strongest version holds the following. First, the content the primary-articulator traditions articulate is not separable from the communities, territories, and ongoing practices that constitute the traditions, and any intellectual project that extracts the content from its constitutive context — even with scholarly care and attribution — is participating in the extraction pattern the critique identifies. Second, the intellectual project of integrating multiple primary-articulator traditions into a single constitutional ontology, however carefully conducted, produces a synthesis that the synthesizer governs analytically even where the synthesizer attempts to refrain from governing — because the choice of which traditions to engage, which elements to draw from each, how to relate them to one another, and what to do with the integrated content is made by the synthesizer and reflects the synthesizer's analytical commitments and structural position. Third, the presentation of the synthesis as a contribution to a universal-or-aspirationally-universal intellectual tradition — even one that explicitly acknowledges its plural sources — reinstates the universalist analytical apparatus the critique identifies as the structural form of coloniality, because the synthesis is presented as addressed to a universal-or-aspirationally-universal audience whose analytical apparatus is the Western apparatus the synthesizer operates from. Fourth, the distinction the project advances between design-imposition framings (rejected) and cultivation framings (advanced) does not, on its own, escape the critique; cultivation framings can be operated under the same structural pattern as design-imposition framings, with the synthesizer cultivating conditions under which the synthesizer's analytical apparatus governs the integration even as the analytical apparatus styles itself as merely cultivating rather than imposing.

The project's analytical response cannot be the dismissal of the critique on the grounds of the project's scholarly intentions or scholarly care; the critique's force is precisely that such grounds are insufficient. The analytical response operates through five commitments the project carries forward and through acknowledgments the project must honestly make.

The first commitment is that the cultivation framing, on its content, sits differently in relation to the decolonial critique than design-renovation framings did. Design-renovation framings — including, for instance, proposals that would draw selectively from Indigenous and Eastern traditions to construct a new architecture under analytical governance of a Western synthesizer — produce the synthesis-as-appropriation pattern the critique identifies because the analytical work is the production of a new architecture under the synthesizer's analytical apparatus. The cultivation framing the project advances is different. The constitutional architecture is not the product of design work; it is the cultivation of conditions under which the polity's plural traditions can continue to articulate and elaborate within their own analytical apparatuses, with the constitutional polity operating as the field-condition cultivated to support the plurality rather than as the integrated architecture synthesized from the plurality. The analytical move is therefore not the synthesis of elements from multiple traditions into a new architecture; it is the cultivation of conditions under which multiple traditions can continue to operate at constitutional standing without integration into a single analytical apparatus. The distinction is not merely semantic. The design-renovation framing produces a new architecture whose content the synthesizer's analytical apparatus governs; the cultivation framing produces conditions under which traditions whose analytical apparatuses the cultivation does not govern can continue to operate.

The commitment is therefore that the project's constitutional ontology does not produce an integrated biocultural-governance ontology under which the primary-articulator traditions are integrated; it produces conditions under which the primary-articulator traditions can continue at constitutional standing within their own analytical apparatuses. The constitutional architecture the project advances is therefore not a synthesis of Haudenosaunee, broader-Indigenous, Daoist, Buddhist, Ubuntu, Andean, Pacific Islander and Maori, Black-radical, and Western-recovery contributions into a single ontology; it is a cultivation-condition specification under which the primary-articulator traditions can continue to articulate their own constitutional content within their own communities, with the constitutional architecture supporting the plurality rather than governing it.

The second commitment is the recognition that the cultivation framing remains, by its structural position, vulnerable to the critique that even cultivation-as-conditions can serve as cover for synthesis-as-appropriation. The vulnerability operates through three principal pathways. First, the specification of what cultivation conditions are appropriate is itself an analytical move the synthesizer makes, and the content of the specification reflects the synthesizer's analytical commitments. The project's specification of the nested-scales reference frame (Thread L) as constitutional category is the project's analytical work, and the specification operates as constitutional governance over the plurality the cultivation framing purports to support. Second, the constitutional polity within which the cultivation is conducted operates with coercive capacities — the police power, the taxation power, the military power, the ordinary administrative powers — and the cultivation conditions are enforced through the coercive capacities. The coercive enforcement of cultivation conditions is structurally similar to the coercive enforcement of design-imposition specifications, and the cultivation framing's operational character may not, in practice, differ as much from the design-imposition character as the analytical distinction suggests. Third, the selection of which traditions to recognize at constitutional standing under the cultivation framing is itself an analytical-political move that the synthesizer-and-the-constitutional-architecture make, and the selection necessarily excludes some traditions and includes others, with the selection reflecting the analytical commitments of those making it rather than the analytical commitments of the traditions themselves.

The analytical-political response to the vulnerability is not the abandonment of the project but the recognition of the vulnerability as structural condition under which the project operates and the ongoing commitment to addressing the vulnerability rather than concealing it. The cultivation conditions the project specifies are advanced as proposal subject to contestation rather than as specification beyond contestation; the coercive enforcement of the cultivation conditions is bounded by the cultivation framing's commitment to plurality, with constitutional limits on the coercive capacities that the design-imposition framings have not maintained; the selection of which traditions to recognize at constitutional standing is conducted with openness to contestation and expansion, with the constitutional architecture's ongoing operations including mechanisms through which contestation can produce expansion or revision of the recognition. The analytical position of the project is therefore that the vulnerability is addressed through ongoing practice rather than resolved through single analytical move, and that the ongoing practice operates under recognition of the vulnerability rather than under denial of it.

The third commitment is the acknowledgment of what the project does and does not authorize itself to claim. The project does not authorize itself to claim that the biocultural-governance constitutional ontology is the synthesis of the primary-articulator traditions; it claims that the ontology is the cultivation-condition specification under which the traditions can continue at constitutional standing, with the specification advanced from a non-Indigenous and non-Eastern authorial location whose analytical apparatus is shaped by the Western intellectual tradition the authorial location operates within. The ontology is therefore not neutral among the traditions; it is advanced from a structural position whose analytical apparatus the specification reflects. The claim the project does advance is that the specification is consistent with the analytical content the primary-articulator traditions articulate, that the specification draws from the analytical apparatuses the traditions supply, and that the specification operates under the recognition that the traditions' ongoing operations are not governed by the specification. The distinction between claiming that the ontology *is* the synthesis of the traditions and claiming that the ontology is *consistent with* and *draws from* the traditions is analytically material. The former claim places the synthesizer's analytical apparatus in analytical governance over the traditions; the latter claim places the synthesizer's analytical apparatus in accountability to the traditions, with the traditions' ongoing operations continuing as independent analytical-political work the synthesizer's specification can be measured against.

The fourth commitment is the ongoing operational practice of engagement with primary-articulator scholars and primary-articulator communities on terms that recognize the primacy of their analytical work and ongoing practice over the specifications the project advances. The operational practice operates through multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline specified in Document VII.01 (Part VII) at depth. The content of the discipline includes engagement of primary-articulator scholars at primary-articulator standing rather than at the standing of supplementary contributors, recognition of the primary-articulator communities' ongoing analytical-political work as primary content rather than as resource for the synthesizer's specifications, and accountability mechanisms through which the synthesizer's specifications can be contested and revised by the primary-articulator scholars and communities the specifications engage. The accountability mechanisms are essential to the integrity of the cultivation framing; without them, the cultivation framing collapses into the synthesis-as-appropriation pattern the decolonial critique identifies. The operational reality is that such accountability mechanisms are imperfect, difficult to operationalize at scales, vulnerable to structural failures of the constitutional architecture, and dependent on ongoing work by primary-articulator scholars and communities whose labor the accountability mechanisms depend on. The analytical-political response is the ongoing commitment to the operational practice rather than the presumption that the analytical commitment alone is sufficient.

The fifth commitment is the recognition that the renovation-versus-decolonial question is not resolved by the cultivation framing the project advances; the question is continued in ongoing practice. The cultivation framing operates as analytical-political proposal that responds to the decolonial critique with analytical work, but the analytical work does not close the question. The question of whether the cultivation framing escapes the synthesis-as-appropriation pattern, reproduces it under a different name, or partially escapes it in some respects while reproducing it in others is answered in ongoing practice rather than in analytical-political proposal. The answer depends on the operational integrity of the accountability mechanisms specified in Document VII.01, on the ongoing engagement with primary-articulator scholars and communities, on the content of the constitutional architecture's cultivation conditions as implemented and revised across generations, and on the judgments of primary-articulator scholars and communities about whether the cultivation framing serves the purposes the traditions and communities pursue or interferes with them. The analytical position of the project is that the question is answerable only in practice, that the practice operates under ongoing vulnerability to failure modes the decolonial critique identifies, and that the integrity of the practice depends on ongoing recognition of the vulnerability rather than presumption that the analytical commitment resolves it.

The conversation Thread M has with Document VII.01 — and the distinction between what Thread M does and what Document VII.01 does — operates as follows. Document VII.01 (Part VII) specifies multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline at depth: the operational practices through which non-Indigenous and non-Eastern scholarly engagement with primary-articulator traditions can be conducted, the accountability mechanisms through which the engagement can be subject to contestation and revision, the epistemological commitments under which the engagement operates. Thread M does not duplicate Document VII.01's methodological work; it engages the ontological-commitment question that Document VII.01's methodological work depends on the resolution of. The question Thread M engages is the ontological-commitment question of whether the cultivation framing constitutes a different relation to the decolonial critique than the design-renovation framing does, and what constitutional ontology the cultivation framing operates under. The question is substantial enough to require Thread M engagement rather than deferral to Document VII.01's methodological work, and the engagement is distinct from Document VII.01's engagement rather than duplicative of it. The integration of the content across the documents is that Document VII.01's methodological discipline operationalizes the ontological commitments Thread M articulates, and Thread M's ontological commitments operate under the methodological discipline Document VII.01 specifies.

The ongoing analytical-political situation the project operates within is therefore this. The decolonial critique is powerful and addresses structural patterns that intellectual projects from non-Indigenous and non-Eastern authorial locations reproduce, including despite scholarly intentions and scholarly care. The cultivation framing the project advances sits differently in relation to the critique than design-renovation framings do, but does not escape the critique entirely. The ongoing operational practice the project commits to — engagement with primary-articulator scholars and communities at primary-articulator standing, accountability mechanisms through which the specifications can be contested and revised, recognition of the vulnerability under which the practice operates — is the content of the project's analytical-political response to the critique. The response is incomplete by its nature; it operates under ongoing vulnerability rather than under resolution of the vulnerability. The integrity of the response depends on ongoing practice rather than on analytical-political proposal alone, and the practice's integrity is answerable to primary-articulator scholars and communities whose judgments constitute the answer to the question the critique raises.

# Closing — What the Rest of the Arc Operationalizes

The document has articulated biocultural governance as constitutional ontology through five movements. The first grounded the ontology in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights scholarship of Maffi, Berkes, Posey, Bavikatte, and Robinson, with the analytical move from biocultural diversity (descriptive) and biocultural heritage (legal) to biocultural governance (constitutional) specified as the project's extension and not as the prior literatures' direct authorization. The second engaged eight primary-articulator traditions at the standing the project's load-bearing commitment requires — the Haudenosaunee Great Law in its own constitutional terms; the broader Indigenous-ecological tradition through Kimmerer, Yunkaporta, Wildcat, and Whyte, each on the analytical apparatus the scholar specifies; the Daoist tradition through the *Daodejing* and *Zhuangzi* and their Ames-Hall, Ziporyn, and Miller commentaries; the Buddhist dependent-origination tradition through Nāgārjuna, Garfield, Hanh, Macy, and Loy; the Ubuntu tradition through Ramose, Cornell-and-van-Marle, *S v Makwanyane*, Shutte, and Metz; the Andean ayllu and ayni traditions through Estermann, Gudynas, de la Cadena, Acosta, and the Ecuadorian and Bolivian constitutional incorporations; the Pacific Islander and Maori traditions through Halapua, Wendt, Thaman, Durie, Mutu-and-Jackson's *Matike Mai*, *Te Awa Tupua*, and Smith; and Black-radical ecological thought through Butler, brown, Kelley, Penniman, and White. The third read the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, regenerative-design, disturbance-ecology, and agrarian-cultivation canon as recent and partial recovery — substantive engagement with Holling, Ostrom, Benyus, Alexander, Mang-and-Reed, Pickett-and-White, Folke-and-Walker, Todd, and Berry, naming what each has recovered, what each extends, and where each falls short of the primary articulation. The fourth specified arts, language, and cultural production as a load-bearing element of the biocultural formation (Thread N). The fifth specified the analytical apex of the document — the case for biocultural governance at the level of constitutional ontology rather than at the level of policy preference (Thread K), the nested-scales reference frame as constitutional category (Thread L), and the renovation-versus-decolonial question engaged under the cultivation-rather-than-design-renovation framing (Thread M).

What this document has specified is the constitutional ontology against which the rest of the arc reads the diagnostic gap, specifies the material substrate, and proposes the cultivation-protocol architecture. The substantive content the rest of the arc operationalizes is plural. The disturbance-regime diagnostics of Documents II.02 (cultural), II.04 (biological), II.05 (physical-environmental), II.06 (cognitive-informational), and II.07 (political-economic) engage the disturbance landscape the polity cultivated as biocultural formation must absorb, with Document II.08 specifying the disturbance-regime interactions. The material-substrate work of Documents VI.05 (regenerative built environment), VI.03 (energy as cultivation), VI.02 (food, soil, agriculture), and VI.04 (cradle-to-cradle material flows) operationalizes the cultivation commitments at the level of the polity's material conditions. The systems-science substrate of Document V.01 and the deep-substrate work of Document IV.01 supply the analytical apparatus through which the biocultural-governance ontology's complex-adaptive-systems and panarchical-scale commitments are operationalized. The cognitive substrate of Document VIII.01 supplies the analytical apparatus through which the polity's cognitive-architectural commitments are operationalized. The eco-mimetic policy-making method of Document XII.01 operationalizes the cultivation-as-method commitment at the level of legislative process. The bounded-decision-rights architecture of Documents XII.09 through XII.12 operationalizes the scale-matched decision-rights commitment at the level of specific decision-domains — foundational decision rights, public-check decision rights, provisioning decision rights, and cognitive-and-intergenerational decision rights. The currency architecture of Document XII.02 operationalizes the political-economic commitments. The e-democracy and structural-public-check architecture of Document XII.03 operationalizes the deliberative-democratic commitments. The intergenerational rectification work of Document XII.04 operationalizes the substantive temporal commitments at the level of historical-recovery practice. The neurodivergent-polis architecture of Document XII.05 operationalizes the cognitive-architectural commitments at the level of substantive accommodation. The multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01 operationalizes the primary-articulator-standing commitments at the level of scholarly and political practice. And Document XIII.01 integrates the architecture as cultivation protocol, with the content of biocultural-governance constitutional ontology articulated here serving as the substantive ontological grounding the integrated cultivation protocol operates under.

The "grown, not built" commitment of the project's colloquial summary names what the substantive ontological commitments require at the substantive level of practical constitutional work. The polity is not built; it is grown. The constitutional architecture is not designed; it is cultivated. The constitutional commitments are not specified in advance; they emerge through substantive practices under substantive cultivation conditions across substantive generations. The substantive analytical work of this document has been to specify what such an ontological commitment requires, what scholarly traditions articulate the commitment at substantive primary-articulator depth, and what substantive analytical apparatus supports the commitment against the substantive design-imposition alternatives the contemporary moment has substantially exhausted. The substantive constitutional architecture the project advances proceeds from this ontological grounding. What this document has specified, the rest of the arc operationalizes.
