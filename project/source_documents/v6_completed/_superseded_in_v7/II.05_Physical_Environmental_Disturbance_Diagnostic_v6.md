# II.05 — The Physical-Environmental-Disturbance Diagnostic: Climate, Water, Fire, Geology, Contamination

## Part II — Disturbance-Regime Diagnostics and the Implementation Gap

*Fifth-pass designation: Document T. This document is the fifth document of Part II, specifying the physical-environmental-disturbance regime as one of the five regimes the constitutional architecture must absorb.*

The physical-environmental-disturbance regime engaged in this document is the regime acting on the physical and environmental substrate of the biocultural polity — the atmosphere, the hydrosphere, the fire regimes of the world's biomes, the lithosphere and its hazards, and the chemical environment that human industry has dispersed into the polity's air, water, soils, and bodies. The document specifies this regime through six analytical dimensions and the cross-regime couplings through which it acts, and it carries the Indigenous water-and-fire-sovereignty traditions and the climate-justice canon at primary-articulator standing.

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad — biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation — articulated in full in Document III.01 and not re-argued here. For this document the triad bears in the direction Part II foregrounds: the disturbance-adapted-implementation orientation specifies that intergenerational constitutional architecture must absorb a plural disturbance landscape, and the physical-environmental regime of this document is among the disturbance landscape's central and most consequential elements.

The document assumes the constitutional ontology of Document III.01, the disturbance-ecology and systems-science substrate of Document V.01, the biological-evolutionary-anthropological critique of Document II.01, and the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01. It coordinates with Document IX.01, which carries climate as the paradigmatic stress test of the constitutional architecture: this document supplies the physical-environmental-disturbance diagnostic foundation, and Document IX.01 carries the constitutional-stress-test analysis the diagnostic enables. It coordinates with Document II.08 on the cross-regime interactions; Part Seven specifies the physical-environmental-regime side. It does not duplicate the cultivation-terrain treatments of Part VI or specify decision rights, which are Part XII's terrain. The analytical work proceeds in seven movements: climate; hydrological regime shift and Indigenous water-sovereignty; fire regime shift and Indigenous fire-stewardship; geological events and comparative disaster-governance; anthropogenic chemical contamination and atmospheric composition shift; cross-regime interactions; and a closing synthesis.

# Part One — Climate

The climate dimension of the physical-environmental-disturbance regime is engaged through the authoritative scientific synthesis, the climate-justice canon, and the broader literature, with the substantive constitutional-stress-test treatment located, by cross-reference, in Document IX.01.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's Sixth Assessment Report — Working Group I on the Physical Science Basis (2021), Working Group II on Impacts, Adaptation, and Vulnerability (2022), Working Group III on Mitigation of Climate Change (2022), and the Synthesis Report (2023) — supplies the authoritative scientific synthesis. The findings, summarized at the breadth a constitutional document requires rather than the depth Document IX.01 carries, are that anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions have already warmed the climate by approximately 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels, that further warming is locked in by emissions already released, that the trajectory under current policies points toward warming well above the 1.5°C and 2°C thresholds the international community has identified as the bounds of relatively manageable change, and that the impacts on water, food, ecosystems, settlements, and human health are accumulating at a pace that is exceeding earlier projections. The IPCC's mitigation pathway analysis specifies that limiting warming requires rapid, deep, and sustained reductions in emissions across every sector of the global economy. The document carries this synthesis as the empirical anchor of the climate dimension; Document IX.01 carries the constitutional-stress-test analysis.

The climate-justice canon is engaged at primary-articulator standing because the climate disturbance does not act on a homogeneous population, and the diagnostic must register what the science alone does not. Robert D. Bullard's *Dumping in Dixie: Race, Class, and Environmental Quality* (1990), the foundational text of the environmental-justice movement, specified the systematic pattern by which environmental hazards — landfills, toxic facilities, polluting industry — were sited in Black and other racialized communities, and named the pattern as environmental racism. The framework Bullard founded has been carried forward across decades. David Pellow's *What Is Critical Environmental Justice?* (2017) supplies the contemporary critical articulation, specifying that environmental injustice operates not as a sequence of individual siting decisions but as a structural feature of the political economy, and that critical environmental justice must engage that structure rather than litigate its episodes. Laura Pulido's *Black, Brown, Yellow, and Left: Radical Activism in Los Angeles* (2006) supplies the multi-racial movement history and her broader climate-and-environmental-racism scholarship specifies how the climate disturbance is refracted through the racial structure of the polity. And Kyle Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi), engaged in Document III.01 and Document VI.01, specifies climate colonialism: the recognition that climate change is, for Indigenous peoples, the continuation of a centuries-long colonial dispossession — the loss of ancestral foodways, sacred sites, species, and territories — rather than a novel arrival in the contemporary moment.

The wider literature carries the engagement into the public register. Naomi Klein's *This Changes Everything: Capitalism vs. the Climate* (2014) traced the structural antagonism between the climate trajectory and the political economy that has generated it, and her *On Fire: The Case for the Green New Deal* (2019) carried the analysis into the contemporary policy register. Mary Annaïse Heglar's essays articulated the emotional labor of carrying climate knowledge and the demand that climate writing reach the populations the technical literature does not address. Kim Stanley Robinson's *The Ministry for the Future* (2020) supplies the imaginative-documentary engagement, working through the institutional and political pathways of an avoided catastrophe in narrative form. The climate dimension is named here at the diagnostic depth this document requires; Document IX.01 carries the constitutional-stress-test analysis that draws the full implications.

# Part Two — Hydrological Regime Shift and Indigenous Water-Sovereignty

The hydrological dimension of the physical-environmental-disturbance regime is the dimension on which Indigenous water-sovereignty traditions stand at primary-articulator standing, and the section engages the science and the tradition together.

Peter Gleick's Water and Conflict corpus, carried through the Pacific Institute archive across recent decades, supplies the diagnostic of how water resources, freshwater availability, drought, and flood are being reorganized under climate change and population pressure, and how water scarcity and water-quality degradation are increasingly bound to conflict, displacement, and instability. Erica Gies's *Water Always Wins: Thriving in an Age of Drought and Deluge* (2022) supplies the contemporary articulation of an alternative water paradigm: the slow-water approach, in which hydrological regulation is conducted by working with water's own dynamics — the floodplain restored, the wetland recovered, the watershed allowed to retain rather than dispatch — rather than against them through the hard infrastructure of dams, levees, and channels that the twentieth century had built and that the climate disturbance is now overwhelming.

The Indigenous water-sovereignty literature is engaged at primary-articulator standing. Winona LaDuke (Anishinaabe), engaged in Documents VI.01, VI.03, and elsewhere, has written across decades on water protection as the substantive practice of Indigenous sovereignty — the wild-rice waters, the lakes and rivers of the Anishinaabe and Ojibwe homelands, the work of Honor the Earth on water-and-pipeline opposition. The Standing Rock #NoDAPL archive, engaged in Documents VI.01 and VI.03 for land and energy sovereignty, is engaged here for what it most centrally articulated: the assertion of Mni Wiconi, water is life, as the constitutional principle of Indigenous water-sovereignty. The Standing Rock movement specified that water is not a commodity, not a utility, but a relative and a being to which obligations are owed, and that decisions affecting water are matters of jurisdiction and consent rather than of technical regulation.

The contemporary legal-personhood-for-rivers tradition carries the same articulation into working law. Anne Salmond's *Tears of Rangi: Experiments Across Worlds* (2017) traced the path to *Te Awa Tupua*, the New Zealand legislation that recognized the Whanganui River as a legal person, with its own legal standing, drawing on the Whanganui *iwi*'s longstanding statement, *Ko au te awa, ko te awa ko au* — I am the river, the river is me. The Atrato River decision of the Colombian Constitutional Court (T-622 of 2016) extended a parallel form of legal personhood to the Atrato River, in a ruling that drew on the constitutional rights of the river's communities and on the Colombian Constitution's pluralist commitments. The case of *Sarayaku Kichwa v. Ecuador* in the Inter-American Court of Human Rights (2012) extended the principle through the international human-rights system, ruling against Ecuador for permitting oil exploration on Sarayaku territory without the community's free, prior, and informed consent. The legal-personhood-for-rivers literature and the Inter-American jurisprudence together specify that the Indigenous water-sovereignty tradition is operating, in working legal systems, as a constitutional articulation that the dominant water-resources framework has not contained — and the document carries that articulation as the primary statement of what water-disturbance governance must answer to.

# Part Three — Fire Regime Shift and Indigenous Fire-Stewardship

The fire dimension of the physical-environmental-disturbance regime is among the dimensions on which the Indigenous primary articulation is most empirically and operationally consequential, because the very pattern of "wildfire" as catastrophic event is in significant part the product of a century of fire-management policy that displaced Indigenous fire stewardship and produced the fuel accumulations the contemporary catastrophic fires are burning through.

Stephen J. Pyne's fire-history corpus supplies the dominant scholarly account. *Fire in America: A Cultural History of Wildland and Rural Fire* (1982) traced the long history of fire on the North American continent, including the Indigenous burning practices that shaped its ecosystems for millennia before European arrival. *Vestal Fire: An Environmental History, Told through Fire, of Europe and Europe's Encounter with the World* (1997) extended the analysis across the wider colonial encounter. *The Pyrocene: How We Created an Age of Fire, and What Happens Next* (2021) supplied Pyne's contemporary synthesis: that humanity has, through its mastery of fire and its global combustion of fossil carbon, generated a planetary fire age, and that the contemporary wildfire crisis must be read within that long arc.

The Indigenous fire-stewardship tradition stands at primary-articulator standing in this section, and it must be engaged at depth. M. Kat Anderson's *Tending the Wild: Native American Knowledge and the Management of California's Natural Resources* (2005) supplied the foundational synthesis: that the landscapes of pre-contact California — the oak woodlands, the prairies, the chaparral — were not wilderness in the European sense but cultivated landscapes, shaped by the deliberate, seasonal, knowledge-bearing burning practices of the California tribes, and that the suppression of those practices by colonial and state fire-exclusion policy was a deliberate disruption of the cultivation through which the landscapes had been sustained. Frank K. Lake (Karuk descent), through his work as a research ecologist and through his sustained collaboration with tribal communities, has carried forward the cultural-burning scholarship and the practice — the work with Karuk, Yurok, Hupa, Maidu, and other northern California tribes to revitalize cultural burning as a practice and to integrate Indigenous fire knowledge into the working management of contemporary fire-prone landscapes. The Indigenous Peoples Burning Network and the Karuk, Yurok, Maidu, and Hupa fire-stewardship traditions are the contemporary institutional and practical carriers of this revitalization, and the document recognizes their work as the operative articulation of fire stewardship at primary-articulator standing.

Victor Steffensen's *Fire Country: How Indigenous Fire Management Could Help Save Australia* (2020) carries the same articulation from the Australian continent, drawing on Steffensen's work with elders and on his apprenticeship with the late Tagalaka elders Dr. Tommy George and George Musgrave. Steffensen specifies that Aboriginal fire stewardship — the cool, small, frequent, country-knowing burns conducted under the discipline of Indigenous fire knowledge — produced fire-adapted landscapes whose contemporary catastrophic fires are in significant part the consequence of that stewardship's interruption, and that the recovery of the practice is not nostalgia but operational necessity. Davianna Pōmaikaʻi McGregor's *Nā Kuaʻāina* (2007), engaged in Document VI.02 for the *ahupuaʻa*, supplies the Hawaiian dimension through the *aloha 'aina* tradition's relation to fire and land tending. The fire dimension of the physical-environmental regime is, more than most, a dimension whose disturbance is in large part the disturbance of the Indigenous stewardship that had cultivated it; the diagnostic specifies this directly.

# Part Four — Geological Events and Comparative Disaster-Governance

The geological dimension of the physical-environmental-disturbance regime — earthquake, volcanic event, tsunami, landslide — is engaged not as a question of geological prediction but as a question of how a polity governs the disturbances geological events produce on its built environment, its communities, and its political economy. The dimension's diagnostic standing in the document is therefore principally on the governance side.

Susan Cutter's social-vulnerability-to-hazards scholarship supplies the foundational analytical apparatus: that the disaster generated by a geological event is not the event itself but the event acting on the social, economic, and built structure of the population it strikes, and that vulnerability — the differential exposure of populations to the event's harm — tracks race, class, age, and the structural position of communities within the polity. The comparative-disaster-governance canon developed by Russell Dynes, Thomas Drabek, and the Disaster Research Center tradition specified the institutional and organizational dynamics of disaster response across decades of empirical study, establishing that disasters do not principally produce the social pathologies they are popularly imagined to produce — looting, panic, the breakdown of social order — but instead reveal the structural pathologies already present in the polity.

Naomi Klein's *The Shock Doctrine: The Rise of Disaster Capitalism* (2007) carries the analysis into the political-economic register. Klein traced, across a series of cases, the pattern by which disasters — natural and otherwise — have been used by political and economic actors as occasions for the imposition of policies that could not have been imposed under normal democratic conditions: privatizations, deregulations, the dismantling of public infrastructure, the displacement of populations. The shock-doctrine analysis specifies that disaster governance is itself a political-economic terrain on which the regime's harm is distributed and amplified. Andy Horowitz's *Katrina: A History, 1915–2015* (2020) supplies the most thoroughgoing contemporary case study, tracing Hurricane Katrina not as a discrete 2005 event but as a long historical disaster — the cumulative consequence of decades of policy decisions about levees, marshland, housing, race, and the political economy of New Orleans — and registering the post-Katrina rebuilding as itself a continuation of the disaster, conducted under exactly the dynamics Klein's analysis specifies. The Haitian 2010 earthquake political-economy archive carries the analysis into the international register, registering how international humanitarian intervention, debt politics, and the reconstruction economy together extended the disaster's effects far beyond the seismic event itself. The Pacific Islander and Caribbean comparative-disaster-governance literature, addressing populations whose vulnerability to geological events is compounded by climate-driven sea-level rise and by colonial-historical political-economic structures, completes the section's account of disasters as governance phenomena rather than only as physical events.

# Part Five — Anthropogenic Chemical Contamination and Atmospheric Composition Shift

The chemical-contamination and atmospheric-composition dimensions of the physical-environmental-disturbance regime register the dispersal of human-produced substances into the physical environment of the polity at planetary scale, and the section engages them through the historical archive and the contemporary literature.

Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962), engaged in Document II.04 for its bearing on the biological regime, anchors this section as the historical articulator of the diagnostic register. Carson's documentation of synthetic pesticide dispersal through air, water, soil, and food, and the cascading damage that followed, established the framework within which the contamination literature has worked since. Sandra Steingraber's *Living Downstream: An Ecologist's Personal Investigation of Cancer and the Environment* (1997), engaged in Document VI.06 for the health implications, is engaged here for its specification of the chemical environment itself: the trajectory by which thousands of synthetic compounds have been released into the planet's air, water, and soils with insufficient knowledge of their long-term effects, and the accumulation of those compounds in bodies and in ecosystems.

The contemporary literature documents the regime's specific present. Rob Bilott's *Exposure: Poisoned Water, Corporate Greed, and One Lawyer's Twenty-Year Battle Against DuPont* (2019) traced the per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances — the PFAS or forever chemicals — from their industrial origins through the litigation that exposed their pervasive contamination of water supplies and human bodies, and the contemporary PFAS-and-litigation archive carries the case forward into the present. Anna Clark's *The Poisoned City: Flint's Water Crisis and the American Urban Tragedy* (2018) supplies the most thorough contemporary documentation of the Flint water crisis, registering the lead contamination of a majority-Black city's drinking water as both a chemical event and a governance failure — a failure whose racial and political-economic structure connects this dimension directly to the environmental-justice tradition of Part One. Steve Lerner's *Sacrifice Zones: The Front Lines of Toxic Chemical Exposure in the United States* (2010) documented the communities — overwhelmingly poor, overwhelmingly racialized — bearing disproportionate exposure to the chemical-industrial complex's emissions, and named them as the sacrifice zones the dominant political economy depends on.

The atmospheric-composition dimension is engaged in two registers. The climate dimension of atmospheric composition — the carbon dioxide and methane accumulation engaged in Part One — is the principal contemporary case, with the IPCC literature carrying the empirical record. The historical case of the ozone-regime crisis and response, documented in Edward A. Parson's *Protecting the Ozone Layer: Science and Strategy* (2003), supplies the constitutional document with an important comparative reference: the chlorofluorocarbon depletion of the stratospheric ozone layer was a planetary atmospheric-composition disturbance that was, through the Montreal Protocol and its subsequent strengthening, substantially addressed by coordinated international action — demonstrating that planetary atmospheric-composition disturbances are tractable when the political conditions, the scientific consensus, and the available alternatives align, and providing the model against which the contemporary climate response is measured and found, by every relevant metric, lacking.

# Part Six — Cross-Regime Interactions

The physical-environmental-disturbance regime is coupled to the other four regimes, and this section specifies the physical-environmental-regime side of each coupling. Document II.08 carries the integrating cross-regime architecture; this document specifies the physical-environmental side at the diagnostic depth that document then integrates.

The physical-environmental-cultural coupling is the coupling through which climate colonialism, environmental racism, and hydro-colonialism are registered. Kyle Whyte's climate-colonialism analysis, engaged in Document III.01 and in Part One above, specifies that for Indigenous peoples the climate disturbance is the continuation of a long colonial dispossession. The environmental-racism mechanisms documented by Bullard, Pellow, and Pulido in Part One specify the racialized distribution of contamination, hazard, and climate vulnerability. Farhana Sultana's scholarship on hydro-colonialism carries the analysis into the water register, specifying that the structures of water access, governance, and infrastructure across the global South carry the colonial and post-colonial political economy into the present, and that water disturbance under climate change is being routed through those structures into a sharply unequal distribution of harm.

The physical-environmental-biological coupling is the coupling Part Four of Document II.04 specified at the climate-driven vector-borne-disease register, the climate-and-biodiversity-loss coupling registered in the IPBES Global Assessment, and the soil-degradation-and-food-web coupling registered in the regenerative-agriculture canon engaged in Document VI.02.

The physical-environmental-political-economic coupling is the coupling through which the climate disturbance is being intensified and managed by the political economy that generated it. Adrienne Buller's *The Value of a Whale: On the Illusions of Green Capitalism* (2022) supplies the contemporary critique of climate-financialization — the framing in which the climate disturbance is to be addressed through the pricing of nature, the financialization of carbon, and the elaboration of green-financial instruments that, on Buller's analysis, principally serve to extend the asset base of existing financial actors while delivering uncertain mitigation outcomes. The wider fossil-fuel political-economy archive — the documentation of how the fossil-fuel industry has shaped, delayed, and absorbed climate policy across half a century — completes the coupling's diagnostic.

The physical-environmental-cognitive coupling is the coupling through which the climate disturbance has been refracted through the cognitive-and-informational regime that Document II.06 diagnoses. Naomi Oreskes and Erik M. Conway's *Merchants of Doubt: How a Handful of Scientists Obscured the Truth on Issues from Tobacco Smoke to Global Warming* (2010) supplied the documentary anchor: a sustained, organized, well-funded campaign to manufacture doubt about climate science, conducted by a small network of scientists and institutions deeply tied to the industries the science indicted, modeled on the tobacco industry's earlier campaign against the cancer literature. The contemporary climate-denial-industrial-complex archive carries the analysis forward, documenting how the disinformation operations have evolved, expanded across platforms, and adapted to each shift in the underlying scientific consensus.

The coordination with Document IX.01 is the section's closing specification. This document supplies the physical-environmental-disturbance diagnostic foundation; Document IX.01 carries climate as the paradigmatic stress test of the constitutional architecture — the case in which the disturbance regime's planetary scale, intergenerational temporal horizon, and cross-jurisdictional incidence test, more thoroughly than any other case, what the constitutional architecture must be capable of absorbing.

# Part Seven — Closing Synthesis

The physical-environmental-disturbance regime specified in this document — climate, hydrological regime shift, fire regime shift, geological events, anthropogenic chemical contamination, atmospheric composition shift, and the cross-regime couplings through which it acts — is among the most consequential of the disturbance regimes the constitutional architecture must absorb. This closing section positions the document within Part II and within the wider arc.

This document is the Part II physical-environmental-regime diagnostic against which the material-substrate documents of Part VI build their cultivation-terrain response. Document VI.01's specification of land relations bears on the watershed, the fire-prone landscape, and the bioregion the disturbance acts on. Document VI.02 on food and soil, Document VI.03 on energy, Document VI.04 on circular material flows, and Document VI.05 on the built environment together specify the cultivation conditions through which the polity's exposure to physical-environmental disturbance is mediated. Document VI.06 on health and Document VI.07 on care, kinship, and social reproduction specify the bodily and relational substrates on which the regime's harm is registered. Document IX.01 carries climate as the constitutional stress test, with this document as its diagnostic foundation. The structural-commitments documents of Part XII — particularly Document XII.09 on foundational decision rights, where the right not to be harmed at biospheric scale is specified, and Document XII.10 on public-check decision rights at the ecosystem and habitat scales — operationalize the architecture this diagnostic specifies the need for. Document XIII.01 integrates the diagnostic into the cultivation-protocol synthesis. The document coordinates with Document III.01 for the constitutional ontology, with Document V.01 for the disturbance-ecology canon, and with Document VII.01 for the multi-eyed-seeing methodology under which the Indigenous water-and-fire-sovereignty traditions have been engaged.

The physical-environmental substrate of the biocultural polity is being destabilized at planetary scale and across every dimension named above, with the trajectory accelerating and the harm distributed along the lines the environmental-justice canon and the Indigenous primary articulators have specified. A constitutional architecture adequate to its biocultural ontology must absorb the regime not as a sequence of crises to be managed individually but as a coupled disturbance landscape to be cultivated through. What this document diagnoses, the cultivation-terrain documents of Part VI, the constitutional climate stress test of Document IX.01, and the structural-commitments architecture of Part XII are written to operationalize.
