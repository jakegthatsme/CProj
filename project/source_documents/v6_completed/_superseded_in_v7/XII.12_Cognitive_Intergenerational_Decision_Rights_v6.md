## Thread C — Intergenerational Criterion as Decision Rule as Bounded Decision Right

The intergenerational criterion as decision rule is specified as a bounded decision right at the temporal-coupling register. The bounding work the present thread conducts is distinguishable from the cultivation work Document XII.04 (Intergenerational Rectification) carries at substantive depth: Document XII.04 articulates what intergenerational rectification substantively looks like as cultivation work across temporal scales; the present thread specifies the criterion's operation as decision rule at the moment of decision across the broader Part-XII architecture — what makes a proposed decision pass the intergenerational threshold rather than fall below it.

The analytical apparatus on intergenerational equity in legal-political architecture operates substantively through several primary articulators. Edith Brown Weiss in *In Fairness to Future Generations: International Law, Common Patrimony, and Intergenerational Equity* (Transnational Publishers / United Nations University, 1989) supplies the foundational analytical articulation of intergenerational equity as legal-architectural commitment with substantive operational specifications. Weiss articulates three principal intergenerational obligations: conservation of options (preserving the diversity of natural and cultural resources available to future generations); conservation of quality (preserving environmental and cultural quality at no worse than the level inherited); and conservation of access (preserving equitable access to the inheritance for all members of the present generation). The three obligations supply analytical apparatus for the criterion's operation as decision rule — a proposed decision passes the intergenerational threshold when it does not foreclose options, does not degrade quality, and does not constrict access across generations. Weiss's analytical work positions the obligations as substantive legal commitments rather than as supplementary moral principles, with implications across international law (the precautionary principle, the polluter-pays principle, the common-heritage-of-mankind principle) and across domestic legal architecture.

Stephen M. Gardiner in *A Perfect Moral Storm: The Ethical Tragedy of Climate Change* (Oxford University Press, 2011) extends the analytical apparatus into the contemporary intergenerational-architectural crisis. Gardiner articulates the "perfect moral storm" as the convergence of three analytical conditions that the contemporary intergenerational architecture has substantially failed to engage: the global storm (asymmetric distribution of climate impact across geographic configurations); the intergenerational storm (asymmetric distribution of climate impact across generations); and the theoretical storm (inadequacy of the contemporary moral-and-political-theoretical apparatus to engage the architectural conditions). The architectural implication for the intergenerational criterion as decision rule is that the criterion must engage the asymmetries at substantive analytical depth — the criterion is not a generalized future-orientation but a specific architectural commitment that engages the asymmetric distributions the analytical work identifies.

Roman Krznaric in *The Good Ancestor: A Radical Prism for Long-Term Thinking* (The Experiment, 2020) supplies the contemporary scholarly engagement on long-term thinking as architectural-civilizational commitment, articulating six principles of long-term thinking (deep-time humility, legacy mindset, intergenerational justice, cathedral thinking, holistic forecasting, transcendent goal). The Long Time Project and the broader long-termist scholarly infrastructure supply contemporary operational engagement, though the analytical work is engaged critically — the long-termist tradition's substantive operational record has been mixed, with substantial debate within the tradition about how the criterion operates against immediate-justice obligations to currently-existing populations whose access has been substantially constrained.

The Haudenosaunee seventh-generation criterion operates at primary-articulator standing as the most-developed primary articulation of intergenerational criterion as decision rule in continuous practice across centuries. Oren Lyons (Onondaga) and colleagues, eds., *Exiled in the Land of the Free: Democracy, Indian Nations, and the U.S. Constitution* (Clear Light, 1992) and John Mohawk's (Seneca) *Utopian Legacies: A History of Conquest and Oppression in the Western World* (Clear Light, 2000) supply the primary-articulator analytical engagement. The seventh-generation criterion specifies that decisions are evaluated against their consequences for the seventh generation following the decision — a multi-century forward horizon (approximately 175 to 200 years) that exceeds any temporal horizon the contemporary American electoral-political architecture systematically engages. The criterion operates as decision rule at the moment of decision rather than as cultivation framing applied after the fact: the Haudenosaunee Grand Council's deliberative architecture specifies that proposed decisions are evaluated against the seventh-generation consequence at the moment of deliberation, with the criterion operating as substantive analytical filter on which decisions can pass the deliberative threshold.

The Commissioner's annual reports and Future Generations Reports (the 2020 *Future Generations Report* supplies substantial analytical engagement) articulate the operational record on how the criterion has operated as decision rule across Welsh public-body decision-making. The architectural implication is that the criterion can operate as decision rule in contemporary constitutional architecture, with the Welsh record supplying empirical engagement on what operates and what fails.

## Thread D — Multi-Lens Checks-and-Balances Feedback Architecture as Bounded Decision Right

The multi-lens checks-and-balances feedback architecture is specified as a bounded decision right requiring that checks operate not only along the classic legislative-executive-judicial axis but along multiple analytical lenses. The bounding work the present thread conducts is the formal-architectural specification of the multi-lens commitment as bounded right rather than as supplementary analytical apparatus attached to the three-branches default.

The classic American constitutional architecture organized checks-and-balances along the three-branches axis through the architectural commitments articulated principally in *The Federalist* No. 51 (James Madison, February 8, 1788), under which the three branches operate as institutional-architectural checks on one another through the mechanism Madison specifies as "ambition must be made to counteract ambition." The Madisonian architecture has operated as the contemporary American constitutional baseline across the subsequent two-and-a-third centuries. The contemporary scholarly literature on the limits of the three-branches architecture has been substantial: Bruce Ackerman in *The Decline and Fall of the American Republic* (Harvard University Press, 2010) articulates the analytical apparatus on how the contemporary American constitutional architecture has substantially eroded the three-branches checks under conditions the original architecture did not anticipate — the rise of the modern administrative state, the expansion of executive power across the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the concentration of political-economic power in extra-governmental institutions. Jack M. Balkin in *Living Originalism* (Harvard University Press, 2011) and his broader corpus on constitutional dysfunction supplies the analytical work on the architectural-interpretive apparatus through which the three-branches commitments operate (or fail to operate) under contemporary conditions. Sanford Levinson's *Our Undemocratic Constitution* (Oxford University Press, 2006, already engaged in Document XII.09) extends the analytical work.

The analytical apparatus on multi-lens analytical engagement supplies the substantive content of the lens-architectural commitment. Sandra Harding in *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge? Thinking from Women's Lives* (Cornell University Press, 1991) articulates the foundational analytical apparatus on standpoint epistemology — the analytical move that knowledge production is conditioned by the standpoint from which it is produced, with the architectural implication that adequate engagement with any analytical question requires engagement from multiple standpoints rather than from a single privileged standpoint. Harding's analytical work positions standpoint epistemology as substantive analytical commitment rather than as supplementary respect-for-diversity gesture, with implications across the broader analytical-architectural configuration. Donna Haraway's "Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective" (*Feminist Studies* 14, no. 3, 1988, 575–599) supplies the foundational analytical articulation on partial perspectives — the analytical move that all knowledge production is partial-perspectival, with the architectural implication that adequate analytical engagement requires coordination among partial perspectives rather than privileging any single perspective as comprehensive.

The architectural implication for the multi-lens commitment is that the lenses cannot operate independently but must operate in intersectional coupling, with the analytical work conducted at the intersections rather than at the single-axis-lens register.
