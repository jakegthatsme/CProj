# II.06 — The Cognitive-and-Informational-Disturbance Diagnostic

## Part II — Disturbance-Regime Diagnostics and the Implementation Gap

*Fifth-pass designation: Document U. This document is the sixth document of Part II, specifying the cognitive-and-informational-disturbance regime as one of the five regimes the constitutional architecture must absorb, and is a designated carrier of both the sixth-pass communications-and-media-and-press positive thread and the sixth-pass technology-and-AI positive thread.*

The cognitive-and-informational-disturbance regime engaged in this document is the regime acting on the cognitive substrate of the biocultural polity — the attention, the perception, the reasoning, and the shared informational environment through which the polity's deliberation and decision are conducted. It is the regime whose diagnosis the contemporary moment most urgently requires, because the cognitive substrate is the substrate through which every other diagnosis is registered: a polity whose cognitive environment is destabilized cannot reliably perceive the climate regime, the biological regime, the political-economic regime, or itself, and the disturbance of cognition therefore compounds every other disturbance.

The document carries the disability-justice, neurodivergent, and Eastern primary articulators on cognitive substrate at primary-articulator standing, and as a sixth-pass thread carrier it adds two positive threads alongside the negative-side diagnostic: the communications-and-media-and-press positive thread (Thread H), specifying what a healthy communicative substrate looks like; and the technology-and-AI positive thread (Thread I), specifying what good technology architecture looks like at the cognitive-and-informational register, with Document VI.04 carrying the material-flow register.

The sixth-pass project rests on the load-bearing triad articulated in Document III.01 and not re-argued here. The document assumes the constitutional ontology and nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, the systems-science substrate of Document V.01, the biological-evolutionary critique of Document II.01, and the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01. It does not duplicate the surveillance-capitalism political-economy analysis that belongs to Document II.07, the cross-regime interactions architecture of Document II.08, the neurodivergent-polis cultivation conditions of Document XII.05, the e-democracy and public-check architecture of Document XII.03, the cognitive-and-intergenerational rights of Document XII.12, or the material-substrate-of-technology analysis of Document VI.04. It coordinates with each and specifies the cognitive-and-informational-disturbance side. The analytical work proceeds in ten threads: opening framing; disinformation cascades; attention-capture architectures; AI-driven epistemic disruption; cognitive-load shocks at scale; generational cognitive-substrate shifts and the disability-justice and neurodivergent primary articulators; cross-regime interactions; communications, media, and press as positive cultivation; technology and AI as positive cultivation at the cognitive-and-informational register; and a closing synthesis.

# Part One — Disinformation Cascades

The disinformation dimension of the regime is engaged through the contemporary scholarly and documentary archive that the past decade has produced at sustained depth.

Yochai Benkler, Robert Faris, and Hal Roberts's *Network Propaganda: Manipulation, Disinformation, and Radicalization in American Politics* (2018) supplies the diagnostic's foundational empirical finding: the American media ecosystem is asymmetric — the right-wing portion is structurally insulated from cross-checking by sources outside it, producing a self-reinforcing propaganda loop, while the broader media ecosystem, with its many faults, remains in significant respects internally cross-checking. The asymmetry, on Benkler and colleagues' analysis, is a structural fact of the media ecosystem rather than a contingent feature of any single outlet, and it explains why misinformation traveling within the asymmetric portion of the ecosystem propagates and accumulates rather than being corrected by exposure to evidence. The Election Integrity Partnership and Stanford Internet Observatory reports across the 2020 and 2022 election cycles, and the parallel work of the Oxford Internet Institute through Samantha Bradshaw and Philip Howard, supply the documentary archive of how organized influence operations — domestic and foreign, partisan and commercial — have operated across platforms, identifying specific operations and their reach. Renée DiResta's writings on operational influence supply the analytical apparatus for distinguishing organic from coordinated information operations. The Mueller Report and the subsequent scholarship on Russian information operations in the 2016 United States election document the foreign-state instance of the regime at the breadth the historical record establishes. And Anya Schiffrin's edited volume *Media Capture: How Money, Digital Platforms, and Governments Control the News* (2021) supplies the comparative analysis of how media systems across the world are captured — by oligarchs, by governments, by platform monopolies — and so connects the disinformation regime to the political-economic regime Document II.07 engages.

# Part Two — Attention-Capture Architectures

The attention-capture dimension is engaged through the literature that has named the contemporary digital environment as an attention economy structured to extract attention from users for the benefit of platforms and advertisers.

Tim Wu's *The Attention Merchants: The Epic Scramble to Get Inside Our Heads* (2016) traced the long history of the attention economy — from the nineteenth-century penny press through twentieth-century broadcasting to the contemporary platform economy — and specified that the contemporary digital environment is the most pervasive and most precisely targeted attention-extraction system the history of the form has produced. The Center for Humane Technology and the work of Tristan Harris supply the contemporary critique from the inside of the industry the critique is directed at — Harris having designed for the attention-extraction architectures he now identifies as systematically harmful — and have specified the design patterns through which the architectures operate. James Williams's *Stand Out of Our Light: Freedom and Resistance in the Attention Economy* (2018) supplies the philosophical analysis: that the attention-capture architectures threaten not merely the user's productivity or focus but the conditions of autonomy themselves, since attention is the medium through which a person directs their own life. Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) supplies the political-economic analysis at greater scale, specifying that the attention-capture architectures are the user-facing surface of a deeper extraction — the extraction of behavioral data as the substrate of a new logic of capital accumulation — and that the disturbance is therefore not only of the user's attention but of the human-experience-rendering capacity itself. Natasha Dow Schüll's *Addiction by Design: Machine Gambling in Las Vegas* (2012) supplies the analytical precursor: her ethnographic study of how machine-gambling architectures are deliberately designed to capture attention into what gamblers call the zone — a state of dissociated continuous engagement — anticipated the design patterns the digital platforms would adopt, and her analysis of the relationship between user and machine in that zone supplies the analytical model for the contemporary platform engagement.

# Part Three — AI-Driven Epistemic Disruption

The artificial-intelligence dimension of the regime is engaged through the contemporary critical-AI literature, with the foundational critique held at primary-articulator standing.

Emily M. Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major, and Margaret Mitchell's "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?" (FAccT 2021) supplies the foundational critique of the contemporary large-language-model architecture: that the models are statistical patterns generated from massive training corpora rather than systems that understand the language they produce, that they encode and amplify the biases of their training data, that their environmental and labor costs are substantial, and that their deployment without adequate caution risks systematic harm. The paper's publication and the subsequent termination of two of its authors at Google made it the founding text of the contemporary AI-ethics literature and a primary articulator of what the field's standing critique is. Laura Weidinger and colleagues' DeepMind ethical-and-social-risks corpus extended the analysis through systematic taxonomies of language-model risks. Abeba Birhane's work on relational ethics and AI specifies that the dominant individualist-rationalist frame within which AI ethics has been conducted misses the relational, contextual character of the harms AI systems produce and the relational ethics through which a just AI governance must be conducted. The algorithmic-amplification literature, including the work of Eytan Bakshy on Facebook's algorithmic environment and Zeynep Tufekci's *Twitter and Tear Gas: The Power and Fragility of Networked Protest* (2017), specifies how algorithmic curation shapes the informational environment in which contemporary publics form their views. The AI-governance corpus, including Stuart Russell's *Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control* (2019) and the work of Allan Dafoe and the GovAI tradition, supplies the analytical apparatus for the longer-term safety and alignment questions. Ruha Benjamin's *Race After Technology: Abolitionist Tools for the New Jim Code* (2019) specifies how technological systems encode and extend racial hierarchies behind a veneer of neutrality. Safiya Umoja Noble's *Algorithms of Oppression: How Search Engines Reinforce Racism* (2018), engaged in Document II.02 for the cultural-disturbance regime, is engaged here for its specific bearing on search and platform algorithms. The comparative AI-regulation literature — the European Union AI Act, the contemporary United States AI-governance archive, and the developing international landscape — supplies the policy register. And Karen Hao's *Empire of AI* (2025) supplies the contemporary documentary critique of the AI-industrial architecture as it has consolidated in the early 2020s, registering the concentration of compute, data, and labor through which the AI industry is built.

# Part Four — Cognitive-Load Shocks at Scale

The cognitive-load dimension of the regime concerns the structural cognitive demand that the contemporary information environment places on persons and on populations.

Iris Marion Young's *Responsibility for Justice* (2011) supplies the political-philosophical anchor: her concept of structural responsibility specifies that individuals bear a kind of responsibility for the structural injustice of the systems they participate in that differs from the liability model dominant in much moral theory, and that the cognitive demand of holding this structural responsibility — under conditions of pervasive informational overload — is itself a question political theory must engage. The decision-science literature on cognitive fatigue and ego-depletion, founded in Roy Baumeister and Kathleen Vohs's work and extending across decades of empirical research, articulated the finding that cognitive resources are depletable and that decision quality degrades under sustained cognitive load. The document engages this literature with the replication-crisis caveats that the field's own self-correcting work has produced: the multi-lab replication study by Hagger and colleagues failed to replicate the central ego-depletion effect at the magnitude the earlier literature had reported, and the wider replication crisis in social psychology has required the literature to revise its confidence in many specific findings. The cognitive-load diagnostic must therefore be drawn from the literature's robust findings rather than from its contested ones: that sustained, high-volume, fast-paced informational demand imposes real cognitive cost is well-supported; the precise mechanism remains under revision. The contemporary attention-and-mental-health archive — including the United States Surgeon General's advisories on youth mental health and social media — registers the population-scale outcome and connects the cognitive-load dimension to Part Five's generational analysis.

# Part Five — Generational Cognitive-Substrate Shifts and the Disability-Justice and Neurodivergent Primary Articulators

This part engages two distinct bodies of analysis bearing on the cognitive substrate, holding them in their distinct registers: the literature on generational cognitive-substrate shifts under the contemporary informational environment, engaged critically; and the disability-justice and neurodivergent canon at primary-articulator standing, which articulates cognitive variation on terms that the generational literature has not always reached.

The generational-cognitive-substrate-shift literature has been organized around the question of whether the contemporary digital environment is producing a generational shift in how cognition is structured, with what consequences for mental health and social capacity. Marc Prensky's framing of digital natives, articulated in the early 2000s, set the question's terms; the subsequent critical literature has substantially revised the simple-generational-shift hypothesis Prensky proposed. Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness* (2024) supplies the most influential contemporary articulation: Haidt argues that the rise of smartphone-based social media use among adolescents from roughly 2010 forward has produced a generational mental-health crisis, registered in dramatic increases in adolescent depression, anxiety, and suicide. The document engages Haidt's argument substantively — registering the increase in adolescent mental-health distress as a documented phenomenon — and it engages, with equal substantive depth, the methodological critique advanced by Andrew Przybylski, Amy Orben, and colleagues. The critique specifies that the empirical evidence linking specific patterns of social-media use to mental-health outcomes is, when examined with the rigor the question requires, far weaker than the strong causal claim of Haidt's analysis can be drawn from; that the effect sizes in the broader literature are small and confounded; that the generational mental-health trends have multiple plausible structural causes; and that policy built on the strong causal claim risks misdirecting attention from the deeper drivers. The document does not flatten this dispute. The cognitive-and-informational regime is being disturbed; the precise role of smartphone social media within that disturbance is, on the available evidence, contested at the magnitude Haidt's analysis requires, and a constitutional architecture must be built to absorb the disturbance under both readings. Walter Ong's *Orality and Literacy: The Technologizing of the Word* (1982) supplies the deeper comparative analysis: that cognitive substrates have shifted across the historical transitions from oral to literate to print culture, that each transition has carried both gains and losses, and that the contemporary digital transition is best understood within that long perspective rather than as an unprecedented event.

The disability-justice and neurodivergent canon engaged in this part stands at primary-articulator standing on cognitive substrate, and it is engaged in its own register. Mia Mingus's writing on access intimacy, and the broader corpus on care webs, engaged in Document VI.07 for the relational dimension, specifies the cognitive substrate from within the disability-justice tradition. Patty Berne and the Sins Invalid corpus, drawn on in Documents VI.06 and VI.07, carry the foundational disability-justice articulation including its specifications about cognitive variation and the cognitive demands the dominant social order places on bodies and minds it has not been built for. Eli Clare's *Brilliant Imperfection: Grappling with Cure* (2017), engaged in Document VI.06 for the health register, is engaged here for its bearing on cognition: the refusal of the cure imperative as a measure of the cognitive person, the insistence that cognitive variation is part of the human range rather than a deficiency to be corrected.

The Mad-studies tradition, carried through Brendan Stone's work and the *Asylum Magazine* corpus, articulates the survivor-and-service-user perspective on the psychiatric system and on the framing of cognitive variation as mental illness. Sami Schalk's *Bodyminds Reimagined: (Dis)ability, Race, and Gender in Black Women's Speculative Fiction* (2018) supplies the analytical concept of the bodymind — the recognition that mind and body are not separable and that disability registers across their integrated formation. Margaret Price's *Mad at School: Rhetorics of Mental Disability and Academic Life* (2011) specifies the academic and educational structures that exclude minds the structures have not been built for. The neurodivergent-constitutional-articulator tradition includes Steve Silberman's *NeuroTribes: The Legacy of Autism and the Future of Neurodiversity* (2015), the documentary historical articulation of the neurodiversity perspective; Devon Price's *Unmasking Autism: Discovering the New Faces of Neurodivergence* (2022), on autistic personhood and the cost of masking; Robert Chapman's philosophical work on neurodivergent personhood and the politics of psychiatric pathologization; and Damian Milton's "On the Ontological Status of Autism: The 'Double Empathy Problem'" (2012), the foundational articulation that the breakdowns in communication and understanding between autistic and non-autistic people are mutual rather than residing in autistic deficit. China Mills's *Decolonizing Global Mental Health: The Psychiatrization of the Majority World* (2014) carries the analysis into the international register, specifying how the global mental-health apparatus has exported a particular psychiatric framing to the majority world in ways that suppress local conceptualizations of distress and impose pharmaceutical interventions on populations the framing has constructed as in need of them. Together this canon specifies what neither the generational-shift literature nor the dominant cognitive-science literature reaches: that cognitive variation is a structural feature of human populations rather than a deviation from a norm, that the disturbance of cognition is registered differently by minds the dominant cognitive architecture has not been built for, and that cognitive-cultivation conditions must answer to the full range of bodyminds rather than to the cognitively-normative subset.

The Eastern primary articulators on cognitive substrate are engaged here as a distinct articulation. The Buddhist *bhavanga* and Abhidhamma consciousness analysis specifies cognition as a continuously arising flow of momentary events rather than as the persistent operation of a substantial self; the Madhyamaka two-truths cognitive analysis, engaged through Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* and Jay Garfield's translations and commentary in *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way* and elsewhere, specifies cognition as proceeding within the conventional truth of experience while ultimately empty of the inherent self that the conventional grammar of cognition implies; Daoist contemplative traditions specify cognitive cultivation through stillness, *wu wei*, and the alignment of mind with the natural unfolding of *ziran*; the Andean *kawsay* tradition specifies cognition within the relational cosmos; and Pacific Islander relational-cognition traditions specify cognition through the *Vā* and through the kin and place relations that constitute the cognizing person. These traditions are engaged at primary-articulator standing as the longest-elaborated traditions of cognitive cultivation the human record carries, and the cognitive-cultivation conditions of Document XII.05's neurodivergent polis are written on their substrate.

# Part Six — Cross-Regime Interactions

The cognitive-and-informational-disturbance regime is coupled to the other four regimes. The cognitive-cultural coupling is the media-axis-and-cognitive-substrate coupling, with surveillance capitalism operating as concentrated cultural-and-cognitive disturbance — Zuboff's analysis of Part Two specifies that the attention-capture architectures are themselves cultural-disturbance instruments, producing and propagating the cultural disturbance Document II.02 diagnoses. The cognitive-political-economic coupling registers the algorithmic labor-market discrimination, the AI-driven concentration of capital, and the surveillance-capitalism analysis whose substantive political-economic treatment is carried in Document II.07. The cognitive-physical-environmental coupling is the coupling Document II.05's Part Six already specified — the climate-disinformation operations through Oreskes and Conway's *Merchants of Doubt* and the contemporary climate-denial-industrial-complex archive. The cognitive-biological coupling is the coupling Document II.04's Part Six specified — the pandemic-and-vaccine-disinformation cascades through which the COVID-19 response was degraded and the biological-regime's harm was amplified. Document II.08 integrates the cross-regime interactions architecture; this section specifies the cognitive-regime side at the diagnostic depth that document then integrates.

# Part Seven — Communications, Media, and Press as Positive Cultivation

This thread carries the sixth-pass communications-and-media-and-press positive thread. Threads One through Three diagnosed the negative-side dynamics of the cognitive-and-informational regime; this thread specifies the positive vision — the press, media, and communicative architecture under which a healthy cognitive substrate is cultivated — and it is held distinct from the negative diagnostic and from Document XII.03, which carries the e-democracy and structural-public-check architecture this thread points toward.

The foundational analysis of the public-communicative sphere is engaged through Jürgen Habermas's *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere: An Inquiry into a Category of Bourgeois Society* (1962, English 1989). Habermas's historical analysis of the emergence and decline of the bourgeois public sphere — the eighteenth-century coffeehouses, salons, journals, and newspapers within which a critical public deliberation had operated — and his account of how that public sphere was subsequently colonized by commercial media and administrative power, supplied the modern political theory of communication its principal text and its standing analytical framework. Nancy Fraser's "Rethinking the Public Sphere: A Contribution to the Critique of Actually Existing Democracy" (1990) supplied the necessary corrective: the Habermasian public sphere had been, in its actual historical form, exclusive — of women, of working classes, of racialized populations — and the proper analytical concept is therefore not a single public sphere but the plural and contestatory landscape of counterpublics that have always operated alongside and against the dominant one. Fraser's reframing is the analytical apparatus through which the positive media architecture this thread specifies must be assessed: a healthy communicative substrate is one in which plural publics and counterpublics each have the material conditions to constitute and sustain themselves.

Yochai Benkler's *The Wealth of Networks: How Social Production Transforms Markets and Freedom* (2006) supplied the optimistic analysis of how networked communication could enable distributed, non-market, peer-produced communicative work to flourish — an analysis the contemporary disturbance has substantially complicated but whose core specification of distributed communicative production remains a positive resource. James Curran's *Media and Democracy* (2011) supplied the comparative analysis of media systems and democratic outcomes, specifying that the structure of a polity's media system — public-service, market, mixed — substantially shapes the quality of its democratic deliberation. Robert W. McChesney's *Rich Media, Poor Democracy: Communication Politics in Dubious Times* (1999) supplied the long critique of how the commercial concentration of American media has impoverished its democratic function, and Victor Pickard's *Democracy Without Journalism? Confronting the Misinformation Society* (2019) supplied the contemporary case for the structural reform of journalism — for public funding, for nonprofit and public-service models, for the recognition that journalism is a public good that the market has predictably failed to sustain.

The community-media and alternative-media traditions are engaged in this thread because they specify the operative positive media architectures the dominant commercial system does not produce. Clemencia Rodríguez's *Fissures in the Mediascape: An International Study of Citizens' Media* (2001) supplied the foundational analysis of community media — the locally produced, locally governed media through which marginalized publics have built their own communicative infrastructure across the world — and specified the operative practice of citizens' media as itself a constitutive act of citizenship. The press-freedom literature carried by the Committee to Protect Journalists, Reporters Without Borders, and PEN America documents the conditions under which press freedom is sustained or destroyed across the world, and the alternative-media tradition of *Democracy Now!* and *The Real News Network* supplies the contemporary working examples of independent, mission-driven journalism. The Indigenous-media tradition is engaged at primary-articulator standing: the historical record of the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first newspaper published by Native Americans (1828), and the contemporary work of the Aboriginal Peoples Television Network (APTN) and the wider Indigenous-media network specify Indigenous communicative sovereignty as a constitutional matter. The Black-press tradition through *The Crisis*, the NAACP-affiliated journal founded by W. E. B. Du Bois in 1910, and through *Ebony* and the broader Black-press archive supplies the parallel documentation of a counterpublic press that has carried Black communicative life across the long history of the dominant press's failures to do so.

The positive cultivation specification follows. A healthy communicative substrate is one in which press freedom is protected, public-service media are resourced, community and counterpublic media are sustained at the scales they require, and the Indigenous and Black-press traditions stand at the standing the project's primary-articulator commitment requires. The architecture for this cultivation, including its decision-rights and provisioning specifications, is carried in Document XII.03; this thread specifies the diagnostic foundation and the positive vision against which the diagnostic of Threads B–D is measured.

# Part Eight — Technology and AI as Positive Cultivation, at the Cognitive-and-Informational Register

This thread carries the sixth-pass technology-and-AI positive thread at its cognitive-and-informational register. Document VI.04 carries the material-flow register of this thread; the two are coordinated and distinct, and together they specify what good technology architecture looks like under the cultivation framing. This thread specifies the cognitive-and-informational side.

The foundational Western technology-criticism canon supplies the analytical frame. Lewis Mumford's *Technics and Civilization* (1934) specified that technics and culture are mutually constituted, and his later *The Myth of the Machine* (1967) drew the distinction between democratic and authoritarian technics — between the small-scale, distributed, comprehensible tools that augment a community's own capacities and the megamachine that subordinates them. Ivan Illich's *Tools for Conviviality* (1973), engaged in Document VI.04 for the material register, is engaged here for the cognitive register: the convivial tool is accessible, comprehensible, repairable, and augmenting; the radical-monopoly tool, which has crossed Illich's second watershed, displaces and replaces the capacities it was meant to serve and renders its users dependent. Langdon Winner's *The Whale and the Reactor: A Search for Limits in an Age of High Technology* (1986) specified that technical artifacts carry politics — that the choices embedded in a technology's design embed political commitments that the technology then enforces — and named the analysis of those embedded politics as a central task of political theory. E. F. Schumacher's *Small Is Beautiful: A Study of Economics as if People Mattered* (1973) supplied the appropriate-technology tradition the cognitive register also draws on.

The contemporary cognitive-technology literature specifies what the contemporary digital environment has done to the cognitive substrate. Sherry Turkle's *Alone Together: Why We Expect More from Technology and Less from Each Other* (2011) and *Reclaiming Conversation: The Power of Talk in a Digital Age* (2015) supplied the most sustained contemporary analysis of how the contemporary digital architectures have reshaped the cognitive and conversational substrates of everyday life, and how the recovery of unmediated conversation is itself a positive cultivation practice the technology-and-AI register requires. The AI-safety and alignment literature, engaged in Brian Christian's *The Alignment Problem: Machine Learning and Human Values* (2020) and in the AI Now Institute's corpus, supplies the contemporary articulation of what good AI architecture would have to do — how AI systems would have to be designed to operate within human values rather than across them. The platform-cooperativism literature, carried by Trebor Scholz's *Uberworked and Underpaid: How Workers Are Disrupting the Digital Economy* (2016), supplies the operative alternative to the platform-corporation model — platforms owned and governed by the workers and users they serve — and is the contemporary articulation of how a convivial digital architecture could be institutionally built.

The Indigenous-AI articulation stands at primary-articulator standing within this thread, and as in Document VI.04 the document must not let the Western technology-criticism canon absorb it. Jason Edward Lewis and colleagues' *Indigenous Protocol and Artificial Intelligence Position Paper* (2020), and Suzanne Kite (Oglala Lakota)'s articulation of Indigenous protocol in technology and AI design, specify that the cognitive-and-informational register of technology is, like the material-flow register, a question of relationship and protocol: of how a new entity is brought into relation with a community, what obligations it carries, and under what protocols of consent it is made and used. Kite's articulation specifies that the AI system or technological artifact is a being brought into a web of relations, accountable under the same protocols by which other beings are brought into relation, and that a technology made and used without regard to those protocols is improperly made and used regardless of its function. The Indigenous-AI tradition is the primary articulator of what good cognitive-and-informational technology architecture looks like under the cultivation framing; the Western technology-criticism canon is engaged as partial recovery.

The positive cultivation specification follows. Good technology and AI architecture, at the cognitive-and-informational register, is convivial in Illich's sense — comprehensible, repairable, augmenting rather than substituting — and operated under the Indigenous protocol the position paper specifies. It is cooperatively owned where the technology is platform-mediated, transparent in its alignment commitments where it is AI-mediated, and built to support rather than to extract the cognitive substrate of its users. The cultivation framing carries this specification forward as the conditions Document XII.03, Document XII.05, and Document XII.12 are to operationalize at the constitutional scale.

# Part Nine — Closing Synthesis

This document is the Part II cognitive-and-informational-regime diagnostic against which the structural-commitments documents of Part XII operate. Document XII.05 on the neurodivergent polis carries the cognitive-cultivation conditions that the disability-justice and neurodivergent canon of Part Five specified. Document XII.12 on cognitive-and-intergenerational decision rights carries the foundational rights bearing on cognition and intergenerational social reproduction. Document XII.03 on e-democracy and structural public check carries the operative architecture of the positive media-and-press thread of Part Seven. Document VI.04 carries the material-flow register of the technology-and-AI thread that Part Eight specified at the cognitive-and-informational register. Document II.08 integrates the cross-regime interactions architecture this document's Part Six specified the cognitive-regime side of. Documents III.01, V.01, and VII.01 supply the ontology, the systems-science substrate, and the methodological discipline under which the document has been conducted.

The cognitive-and-informational substrate of the biocultural polity is being disturbed at planetary scale, through the dynamics of disinformation cascade, attention capture, AI-driven epistemic disruption, cognitive-load shock, and generational substrate shift that this document has diagnosed. The positive cultivation of the substrate — through press freedom, public-service and community media, convivial technology, and the Indigenous protocols specified at primary-articulator standing — is the work the constitutional architecture of Part XII is being built to support. The disability-justice, neurodivergent, and Eastern primary articulators carry the cognitive-cultivation conditions the architecture must answer to. What this document specifies, the structural-commitments documents of Part XII and the cultivation protocol of Part XIII are written to operationalize.
