## Opening: The Architecture Is Not a Building

The fifth-pass project framed its positive constitutional work as renovation: the captured eighteenth-century architecture is to be replaced by a new one, synthesized from the multi-traditional canon at primary-articulator standing. The sixth pass moves beyond that framing not by abandoning the renovation but by reconceiving what the renovation is. The architecture is not a building. It is not a structure specified in advance, imposed at founding, and amended only through formal constitutional process. It is a cultivation protocol — a specification of the conditions under which a biocultural polity grows in response to the disturbance landscape it inhabits, with collective deliberative discipline as the cultivation work, primary-articulator standing for Indigenous and Eastern traditions as the methodological-and-substantive commitment, and the nested-scales reference frame `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6` articulates as the spatial-and-temporal grammar. The renovation, on this reading, is not the substitution of one architectural specification for another but the substitution of an architectural specification for a cultivation protocol. What the polity *is* changes: from designed structure to cultivated formation. What the constitutional document *does* changes: from blueprint to cultivation specification. What the constitutional discipline *requires* changes: from periodic interpretation at one institutional scale to ongoing cross-scale cultivation work across the architecture's full nested-scales reference frame.

The integrative work of this document is to specify that protocol. The protocol has eleven principal elements drawn from across the sixth-pass arc; the elements operate not as a list of separable commitments but as one coordinated cultivation architecture, coupled across the nested scales `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6` specifies, distributed across the five disturbance regimes the Part II diagnostic documents specify, and instantiating the load-bearing triad of biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, and disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation. The three integrative moves that hold the eleven elements together — cross-scale coupling, regime distribution, triad instantiation — are the document's analytical apex; they articulate the architecture's character as one cultivation protocol rather than as eleven coordinated policies. The document closes by engaging the renovation-versus-decolonial discipline at the level of the constitutional proposal itself, naming what the protocol does and does not authorize itself to claim under the multi-traditional canonical commitment, and handing the integrative reading to `XIV.01_Capstone_Synthesis_v6` for the arc-wide multi-eyed-seeing capstone work.

## Thread A — Cultivation as Substantive Analytical Move, Not as Metaphor

The cultivation framing functions in this document not as decorative analogy from agriculture or ecology applied to constitutional theory but as substantive analytical move at the level of constitutional architecture. The move is most clearly seen by setting cultivation against three contrast cases the framing distinguishes itself against, each of which represents a constitutional-architectural commitment the sixth-pass project does not adopt.

The first contrast is design-imposition: the eighteenth-century framing under which the U.S. founders worked, in which the polity's institutional architecture is specified in advance, imposed at founding, and amended only through formal constitutional process. The framing is articulated paradigmatically in *The Federalist*, particularly in Madison's specification of separation of powers and bicameralism in *Federalist* No. 51 (1788) and in the Constitution's Article V amendment procedure. Its anthropological-and-ontological substrate — what Charles Mills, in *The Racial Contract* (Cornell, 1997), and Carole Pateman, in *The Sexual Contract* (Stanford, 1988), read as the social-contractarian inheritance — assumes that the polity is constituted by the founding act and reproduces itself thereafter through the institutional mechanisms the founding specifies. `II.01_Biological_Evolutionary_Anthropological_Critique_v6` reads this assumption against the cooperative-cognitive-primate evidence Michael Tomasello develops in *A Natural History of Human Morality* (Harvard, 2016) and the Lockean possessive-individualism inheritance Crawford Brough Macpherson critiqued in *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism* (Clarendon, 1962); the founders' anthropology, read against the evidence of how cooperative cognition actually operates, is shown to be inadequate to its own subject matter. `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6` carries the substantive ontological alternative: the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested scales as a biocultural formation, not a designed institutional fixture. The design-imposition framing is, under that ontology, an analytical category mistake — it treats the polity as if it were the kind of thing that could be specified in advance, when the cooperative-cognitive-primate evidence and the disturbance-ecology, CAS, and biocultural-diversity literatures show it is not.

The second contrast is design-renovation: the framing under which the fifth-pass project itself operated until the sixth-pass commitment, in which the renovation work consisted in proposing a new institutional architecture as replacement for the captured eighteenth-century one. The design-renovation framing inherits from design-imposition the assumption that what a polity needs at the level of constitutional architecture is a specification — a blueprint, however differently grounded, however more carefully attuned to the multi-traditional canon, however more genuinely committed to the disturbance landscape the polity inhabits. The sixth-pass commitment moves beyond the design-renovation framing for three reasons. First, the appropriation critique the decolonial canon raises against synthesis-as-architecture (engaged at depth in Thread B) bears on any single-document constitutional architecture drawing on Indigenous, Eastern, African, Andean, Pacific Islander, Maori, Black-radical, and other traditions, however carefully it preserves each tradition's analytical register; design-renovation, even at the project's best calibration of multi-traditional engagement, performs the appropriation the critique identifies. Second, the analytical limits of any single architectural synthesis as response to multi-traditional inheritance — limits the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6` specifies, drawing on Mi'kmaq Elder Albert Marshall's articulation of two-eyed seeing and elaborated for the multi-traditional canon across the project's arc — show that no single synthesis can carry what the canonical plurality requires; design-renovation, by virtue of proposing one architecture, exceeds what the multi-traditional canon authorizes. Third, the recognition that the cultivation framing the Indigenous and Eastern traditions have carried for centuries — articulated paradigmatically in the Haudenosaunee Great Law's seventh-generation criterion, in the Daoist *wu wei* and *ziran* tradition's specification of non-coercive cultivation as governance method, in the Andean Pachamama-and-Buen-Vivir constitutional incorporations Eduardo Gudynas and Catherine Walsh have analyzed, in the Maori marae-based deliberation tradition Margaret Mutu and Moana Jackson have specified — is the substantive analytical move the project's renovation work requires, not merely the language in which it is presented. Design-renovation treats cultivation as how the architecture is described; the sixth-pass commitment treats cultivation as what the architecture *is*.

The third contrast is technocratic optimization: the framing under which the eco-modernist tradition and the broader ecological-management literature operate, in which expert managers optimize ecosystem-and-social-system parameters under scientific guidance. The framing is articulated paradigmatically in Ted Nordhaus and Michael Shellenberger's *Break Through: From the Death of Environmentalism to the Politics of Possibility* (Houghton Mifflin, 2007) and in the broader Breakthrough Institute corpus on ecomodernism and intensification; it is articulated in the technocratic ecosystem-services literature that translates ecological function into market-priced parameters expert managers can optimize. `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v6`'s Thread K specifies in detail that eco-mimetic policy-making is not technocratic optimization: the operational vocabulary the document draws from adaptive-cycle theory, biomimicry, pattern language, and regenerative design is collective deliberative cultivation discipline grounded in Indigenous and Eastern primary articulators with Western canonical recovery as supporting substrate, not expert-managerial optimization. The cultivation framing at the level of constitutional architecture extends the same distinction: cultivation is what the architecture does at every scale of operation, not what experts do at one scale of administrative implementation. The constitutional architecture is cultivation work conducted by the polity, under the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline and the multi-lens checks-and-balances feedback architecture, with primary-articulator standing for non-Western traditions throughout; it is not expert optimization conducted on the polity, under technocratic guidance.

The cultivation framing, set against these three contrasts, names what the constitutional architecture is at the level of constitutional ontology, method, and orientation. At the level of ontology (drawing on `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6`), the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested scales as a biocultural formation, not a designed institutional fixture. At the level of method (drawing on `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v6`), the cultivation work proceeds through legislative-cycle-mimicking-adaptive-cycle architecture, scope-scale-feedback-and-sunset-or-adaptation-conditioned legal drafting, and ongoing cross-scale ecological-monitoring-style constitutional-and-statutory review, with collective deliberative discipline as the operating vocabulary and the multi-traditional canon at co-equal standing. At the level of orientation (drawing on the disturbance-regime diagnostics `II.02_Cultural_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v6`, `II.04_Biological_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v6`, `II.05_Physical_Environmental_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v6`, `II.06_Cognitive_Informational_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v6`, `II.07_Political_Economic_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v6`, and the cross-regime-interactions analysis `II.08_Disturbance_Regime_Interactions_v6` carries), the architecture is cultivated to absorb compound disturbance across the regime landscape rather than to operate under steady-state assumptions, with the temporal horizon extending across the intergenerational scale the seventh-generation criterion specifies.

Cultivation, on this articulation, is not the application of agricultural or ecological metaphor to constitutional theory. It is the substantive analytical move that constitutes constitutional theory under the project's renovation commitment. Every subsequent thread of this document executes the cultivation framing in its substantive analytical work: Threads C through M articulate the eleven protocol elements as cultivation conditions the architecture maintains, not as institutional specifications the architecture imposes; Threads N, O, and P articulate how the elements couple across scales, distribute across regimes, and instantiate the triad as integrated cultivation work; Threads Q and R close by engaging the cultivation-as-cover-for-synthesis risk at the level of constitutional proposal and by handing the integrative cultivation reading to XIV.01 for the arc-wide capstone synthesis.

## Thread B — Cultivation as Legitimate Response to the Renovation-versus-Decolonial Question

The renovation-versus-decolonial question, engaged at the level of method in `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6`'s Thread M and at the level of methodological discipline in `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6`, returns at this document as a question about the constitutional proposal itself. The question, in its strongest decolonial articulation, is whether the project's positive constitutional proposal — articulating a cultivation protocol that draws on Indigenous, Eastern, African, Andean, Pacific Islander, Maori, Black-radical, and other traditions at primary-articulator standing — escapes the decolonial critique of synthesis-as-appropriation. The critique, in its strongest version, holds that any single-document constitutional synthesis drawing on multiple traditions, however carefully it preserves each tradition's analytical register, performs an act of appropriation by virtue of synthesizing them into one architectural register.

The literature carrying this critique is substantial and the project takes it seriously. Walter D. Mignolo, in *The Darker Side of Western Modernity: Global Futures, Decolonial Options* (Duke, 2011), articulates the appropriation question through the framework of coloniality: the matrix of power Aníbal Quijano specified in "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America" (*Nepantla* 1, no. 3, 2000) and elaborated across the Modernity/Coloniality research program does not merely structure the relations between colonizer and colonized in the historical sense; it structures the categories under which knowledge is produced and integrated, including the categories under which a renovation project drawing on multiple traditions performs its integrative work. Linda Tuhiwai Smith (Ngāti Awa, Ngāti Porou), in *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (Zed, 1999; 2nd ed. 2012), articulates the appropriation question through the framework of methodology: the very methodological categories under which Western research engages Indigenous knowledge — categories of synthesis, comparison, integration — carry the coloniality the research seeks to transcend. Boaventura de Sousa Santos, in *Epistemologies of the South: Justice against Epistemicide* (Paradigm, 2014; Routledge, 2016), articulates the critique through the framework of epistemicide: the cognitive injustice the modern Western canonical apparatus perpetrates against subaltern epistemologies operates not only in the exclusion of those epistemologies but in the inclusion of them under the categories of the dominant epistemology. Across these articulations, the appropriation critique bears not only on hostile or careless engagement with non-Western traditions but on any engagement that synthesizes them into a single register, however carefully the synthesis preserves the source-tradition's voice.

The cultivation framing answers this critique differently than design-renovation would. Design-renovation proposes a new architecture synthesized from multiple traditions; the critique reads the proposal as appropriation by virtue of the synthesis-into-architecture, regardless of how carefully the source-traditions are preserved in the synthesis. Cultivation proposes maintaining conditions under which each tradition continues to articulate and elaborate within its own analytical apparatus, with the constitutional polity as field-condition cultivated to support the plurality rather than as architecture synthesized from it. The distinction is not rhetorical. Design-renovation specifies a single integrated architecture that incorporates the traditions; cultivation specifies the conditions under which the traditions continue to operate as plural cultivation work within the polity. The first incorporates the plurality into one architectural specification; the second maintains the plurality as ongoing constitutional method.

The distinction matters operationally. Under design-renovation, the question of how each tradition's analytical contribution fits into the architectural specification is answered by the project's synthesis — the architecture says where the Haudenosaunee Great Law applies and where the Madisonian separation of powers applies and where the Andean Buen Vivir framework applies, and the answer is fixed at founding (or at amendment, which inherits the founding's architectural-specification framing). Under cultivation, the question is not answered by the project's synthesis but is maintained as live constitutional question, with the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6` specifies as the ongoing method by which the traditions continue to articulate their contributions in their own analytical registers, and with the cross-scale feedback architecture as the means by which the contributions propagate across the architecture's full nested-scales reference frame.

The cultivation framing's response to the appropriation critique is, however, not unproblematic. The decolonial canon's strongest version of the critique anticipates cultivation-framing-as-cover-for-synthesis — a risk Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi Nation), in *Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants* (Milkweed, 2013), and Kyle Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi Nation), in "Settler Colonialism, Ecology, and Environmental Injustice" (*Environment and Society* 9, no. 1, 2018) and in the broader corpus on settler-Indigenous relations, have named explicitly. Cultivation can become the rhetorical cover under which synthesis-as-appropriation proceeds under softer name; the discipline against this risk is concrete, not rhetorical.

The discipline operates at multiple levels of the protocol. At the level of primary-articulator standing, the protocol preserves Indigenous, Eastern, African, Andean, Pacific Islander, Maori, Black-radical, and other traditions' analytical apparatus in their own registers throughout the arc — `I.02_Founding_Texts_and_1787_Implementation_v6` engages the Kurukan Fuga Charter (Mali Empire, ca. 1235), the Constitution of Medina (622 CE), the Kaianerekowa (the Great Law of Peace of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy), the Cherokee Constitution of 1827, the Uluru Statement from the Heart (2017), and *Matike Mai Aotearoa* (2016) as foundational texts at co-equal standing with the U.S. Declaration of Independence and the 1787 Constitution; `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6` articulates the biocultural-governance ontology with Vine Deloria Jr. (Standing Rock Sioux), Robin Wall Kimmerer, Daniel Wildcat (Yuchi/Muscogee), Tyson Yunkaporta (Apalech clan), and Linda Tuhiwai Smith as primary articulators; `V.01_CAS_Emergence_Adaptive_Governance_v6` articulates adaptive governance with Indigenous resilience traditions as primary articulators alongside the Resilience Alliance corpus; `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v6` articulates eco-mimetic policy-making with the Haudenosaunee Great Law, the Andean Pachamama-and-Buen-Vivir constitutional incorporations, the Maori marae-based deliberation tradition, the Daoist *wu wei* and *ziran* tradition, the Buddhist dependent-origination tradition, the Confucian remonstrance and *li* tradition, and the Islamic *shura* and *waqf* traditions as primary articulators of the operational method. Across these documents and across the rest of the arc, the engagement is in each tradition's own analytical apparatus, not in the project's synthesis vocabulary.

At the level of cross-citation, the protocol cites the upstream documents for the substantive content each carries rather than synthesizing the content into single-vocabulary form here. Threads C through M of this document do not re-articulate the substantive analytical work the upstream documents perform; they specify how each protocol element operates under the cultivation-protocol framing and cite the upstream document for the analytical content. This is the move-don't-regenerate discipline `usage-efficiency` specifies, but it is also the substantive discipline against appropriation: the upstream documents carry the engagement in each tradition's own register, and the integration here does not collapse those registers into a single synthesis vocabulary.

At the level of substantive engagement with the appropriation critique, Thread Q below engages the critique at the level of the constitutional proposal itself. The protocol does not authorize itself to claim that the critique has been answered, that cultivation framing is unproblematic, or that the project speaks for the traditions it engages. It names what the protocol does and does not authorize itself to claim, and it acknowledges that the cultivation discipline includes the capacity for revision from sources the project does not author. The renovation-versus-decolonial discipline operates not only at the level of method (engaged in `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6` and `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6`) and at the level of the constitutional proposal (engaged in Thread Q below) but at the level of the protocol's character as ongoing cultivation discipline rather than fixed-at-founding architectural specification.

At the level of institutional architecture, the protocol instantiates the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6` specifies — drawing on Albert Marshall's two-eyed-seeing articulation and elaborated through the multi-traditional canonical commitment — as ongoing constitutional method rather than as one-time-at-founding architectural feature. The plurality is maintained through the architecture's operational discipline, not collapsed into the architecture's structural specification.

The cultivation framing's response to the appropriation critique is, on this articulation, a substantive answer that is itself subject to the critique's ongoing pressure. The protocol does not claim to have resolved the critique; it claims to have advanced a constitutional proposal under the cultivation framing that distinguishes itself from the design-renovation framing the critique most sharply identifies, while remaining subject to the critique's ongoing discipline. The residual analytical commitment the project takes on is to maintain the discipline against cultivation-as-cover-for-synthesis through the concrete operational commitments specified above, and to accept that the discipline's adequacy is itself a question the traditions whose primary articulators the project engages are entitled to answer.

## Thread C — Foundational Normative Texts as Governing Criterion

The first of the eleven protocol elements specifies foundational normative texts as governing criterion of the cultivation protocol. The element draws on `I.02_Founding_Texts_and_1787_Implementation_v6` for the foundational-texts substrate, on `I.01_Declaration_as_Foundation_v6` for the multi-traditional canonical commitment as constitutional method, on `I.03_Federalist_AntiFederalist_Records_v6` and `I.04_Founders_in_Hindsight_v6` for the analytical record of one historical implementation read against the foundational-texts substrate, and on `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6` for the methodological discipline under which the texts are engaged.

The substantive content the upstream documents carry is not re-articulated here. `I.02_Founding_Texts_and_1787_Implementation_v6` engages the foundational-texts substrate at depth: the Declaration of Independence (1776) and the Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776) as the U.S. founding texts read against their own normative commitments; the 1787 Constitution and the Reconstruction Amendments (1865, 1868, 1870) as one partial implementation of the founding commitments; the Kurukan Fuga Charter (Mali Empire, ca. 1235) and the Constitution of Medina (622 CE) as African foundational texts at co-equal standing; the Kaianerekowa (the Great Law of Peace of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy) and the Cherokee Constitution of 1827 as Indigenous foundational texts at co-equal standing; the Uluru Statement from the Heart (2017) and *Matike Mai Aotearoa* (Independent Working Group on Constitutional Transformation, 2016) as contemporary Indigenous foundational texts at co-equal standing; the Constitution of Ecuador (2008) as the constitutional incorporation of Pachamama and Buen Vivir; and the broader multi-traditional canonical inheritance. The substantive engagement with each text — its analytical commitments, its normative claims, its constitutional-architectural implications — is conducted in `I.02_Founding_Texts_and_1787_Implementation_v6`, not here.

What this thread specifies is how the foundational normative texts operate as governing criterion under the cultivation protocol. The protocol's commitments are five. First, all canonical traditions' foundational texts stand at co-equal canonical weight; no foundational text is privileged over another by virtue of its tradition's place in the historical succession of imperial-and-colonial power. The U.S. Declaration of Independence is read alongside the Kurukan Fuga Charter and the Kaianerekowa at the same canonical register, not as primary text supplemented by comparative materials.

Second, the texts are read as cultivation specifications rather than as institutional blueprints. The Declaration's commitment to "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness" is read not as a list of institutional rights to be enforced under the originalist framing Antonin Scalia articulated and the Federalist Society inheritance has carried but as a cultivation specification carrying normative commitments under which the polity is to grow — the commitments to be elaborated and instantiated through the architecture's ongoing cultivation work, not enforced through originalist interpretation at one institutional scale. The Kaianerekowa's seventh-generation criterion and its specification of the Council's deliberative method are read not as historical-anthropological materials to be admired but not adopted but as cultivation specifications carrying normative commitments at the same register as the Declaration's commitments. The Andean *sumak kawsay* (Buen Vivir) commitment incorporated in the Constitution of Ecuador is read as cultivation specification carrying the same constitutional standing as the Bill of Rights' commitments.

Third, the texts are engaged through the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6` specifies. The discipline holds that each tradition's foundational texts are engaged in that tradition's own analytical apparatus, not collapsed into a single hermeneutic vocabulary; the multi-eyed-seeing methodology coordinates the engagements across traditions through the cultivation discipline rather than synthesizing them into one interpretive framework.

Fourth, the texts are subject to the renovation-versus-decolonial discipline of Thread B in their application. The cultivation protocol does not authorize itself to claim a single integrated reading of the multi-traditional foundational-texts substrate; the application of the texts as governing criterion proceeds under the discipline against synthesis-into-architecture, with the plurality of foundational commitments maintained as ongoing constitutional question.

Fifth, the texts operate as governing criterion across the architecture's full nested-scales reference frame. The Declaration's commitments do not apply only at the U.S. federal scale; the Kaianerekowa's commitments do not apply only at Haudenosaunee Confederacy scale; the Kurukan Fuga Charter's commitments do not apply only at Mali Empire historical scale. Each text's commitments propagate across the architecture's scales as cultivation specifications that the architecture's cross-scale feedback work elaborates and instantiates at each scale of operation.

The cultivation protocol's foundational-normative-texts element is, on this articulation, neither originalist nor lapsed-historical. It treats the foundational texts of all canonical traditions as living cultivation specifications operating across the architecture's full nested-scales reference frame, engaged through the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline, and subject to the renovation-versus-decolonial discipline in their application. The substantive content of each text and the analytical engagement of each is carried in `I.02_Founding_Texts_and_1787_Implementation_v6` and the rest of Part I; what this element specifies is the architecture's operational standing toward that substrate.

## Thread D — Indigenous Co-Governance and FPIC as Constitutional Commitment

The second protocol element specifies Indigenous co-governance and Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) as constitutional commitment of the cultivation protocol. The element draws on `I.02_Founding_Texts_and_1787_Implementation_v6` for the Indigenous foundational-texts substrate, on `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6` for the multi-eyed-seeing methodological commitment, on `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6` for the biocultural-governance ontology with Indigenous primary articulators, and on `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v6` for the Indigenous primary articulations of eco-mimetic legal architecture — particularly the Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River) legal-personhood architecture established under the Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017 of New Zealand and the Andean Pachamama-and-Buen-Vivir constitutional incorporations in the Constitution of Ecuador (2008) and the Constitution of Bolivia (2009).

The substantive content the upstream documents carry is not re-articulated here. The protocol's commitments under this element are five.

First, Indigenous co-governance operates as ongoing institutional architecture at scales matched to Indigenous nations' territorial and jurisdictional standing, not as advisory consultation supplementary to settler-state governance. The substantive articulation of what co-governance means at constitutional scale draws on the *Matike Mai Aotearoa* report's specification of co-governance architectures for Aotearoa New Zealand — six models the Independent Working Group on Constitutional Transformation specified, ranging from continued Crown sovereignty with Maori consultation to full Maori sovereignty over Maori matters with relational governance protocols between Maori and Crown. The cultivation protocol does not adopt one *Matike Mai* model as architectural specification; it adopts the *Matike Mai* methodology — co-governance as constitutional matter to be specified through ongoing deliberation between Indigenous nations and settler states at appropriate scales, with the substantive architecture cultivated rather than imposed.

Second, Free, Prior, and Informed Consent operates as constitutional decision rule for any matter touching Indigenous lands, resources, knowledges, or sovereignty. The FPIC framework draws on the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, adopted by General Assembly Resolution 61/295 on 13 September 2007), particularly Articles 10, 11, 19, 28, 29, and 32, which specify FPIC commitments across the range of matters touching Indigenous interests. FPIC operates under the protocol not as advisory consultation that the settler state can override after consultation but as decision rule with dispositional force at constitutional scale. The substantive articulation of what FPIC requires in particular cases — what counts as informed consent, what counts as the Indigenous polity authorized to give or withhold consent, what counts as the matter sufficiently consequential to trigger the FPIC requirement — is cultivated through the architecture's ongoing co-governance work rather than fixed by single-text definition.

Third, cross-jurisdictional protocols between Indigenous nations and the U.S. federal system (and equivalent protocols in other settler-state jurisdictions) operate as constitutional rather than as administrative architecture. The protocols draw on the *Matike Mai* architectural proposals for relational governance between Maori and Crown; on the Whanganui implementation record under the Te Awa Tupua Act 2017, which establishes Te Awa Tupua as a legal person with the standing of a person under New Zealand law and specifies guardianship through Te Pou Tupua (a body composed of two persons, one nominated by the Whanganui iwi and one by the Crown); on the Australian Native Title Act 1993 (Cth) record, read against its substantial limitations as engaged by the Indigenous-rights canon; on the Canadian Crown-Indigenous co-management arrangements; and on the U.S. record under the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 (Public Law 93-638) as one partial precedent. The protocol does not adopt one model as architectural specification; it adopts the operational principle that cross-jurisdictional governance between Indigenous nations and settler states is constitutional matter cultivated through ongoing deliberation, with the substantive architecture cultivated rather than fixed.

Fourth, the legal-personhood architectures of `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v6`'s Threads C and I — the Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui) architecture, the Atrato River case (Decision T-622 of 2016 of the Constitutional Court of Colombia), the Pacha Mama constitutional incorporation in the Ecuadorian Constitution Articles 71-74, the broader rights-of-nature literature Cormac Cullinan elaborated in *Wild Law: A Manifesto for Earth Justice* (Green Books, 2002) and the Earth Law Center has developed — operate as instances of FPIC-as-constitutional-architecture in operation. The legal-personhood architectures instantiate FPIC at the scale of ecosystems-as-persons; they are not separate constitutional commitments from FPIC but are FPIC operating at ecosystem scale with Indigenous co-governance as the institutional architecture.

Fifth, Indigenous co-governance and FPIC operate as constitutional commitments not as terminus of the protocol's engagement with Indigenous traditions but as constitutional architecture that maintains the conditions under which Indigenous nations continue to articulate constitutional theory in their own analytical registers. The Kaianerekowa's specification of council deliberation, the Cherokee Constitution of 1827's specification of national institutional architecture, the Diné (Navajo) Fundamental Law's specification of Diné governance under the *Diné Bi Beenahaz'áanii*, the *Matike Mai Aotearoa* report's specification of co-governance architectures, the Andean *sumak kawsay* and *suma qamaña* commitments incorporated in the Ecuadorian and Bolivian Constitutions, the Mexican *Plan de Ayala* (1911) commitments to Indigenous land restitution under the Zapatista articulation — these and the broader Indigenous constitutional canon continue to articulate constitutional theory within the cultivation protocol's architecture, not as comparative supplement to the protocol's specifications. The constitutional commitment to Indigenous co-governance and FPIC is the cultivation condition under which that ongoing Indigenous constitutional articulation operates within the polity, not the substitute for it.

## Thread E — Intergenerational Rectification as Cultivation Work

The third protocol element specifies intergenerational rectification as cultivation work of the protocol. The element draws principally on `XII.04_Intergenerational_Rectification_v6` for the intergenerational-rectification commitment under the sixth-pass framing, with the Haudenosaunee seventh-generation criterion as primary articulation and the Western canonical recovery (Edith Brown Weiss, Stephen Gardiner, the Long Now Foundation under Stewart Brand and Danny Hillis, Rob Nixon) as supporting substrate. The element also draws on `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v6`'s Thread H (the Welsh Future Generations Commissioner architecture as one operational instantiation) and on `II.08_Disturbance_Regime_Interactions_v6`'s intergenerational-feedback conditions specification.

## Thread Q — The Renovation-versus-Decolonial Discipline at the Level of Constitutional Proposal
