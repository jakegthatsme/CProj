# VI.07 — Care, Kinship, and Social Reproduction as Cultivation

## Part VI — The Material Substrate

*New sixth-pass document. Working designation in the planning record: Document AA / "Care." This document is the seventh and closing document of Part VI, the relational-substrate document, following `VI.06_Health_as_Cultivation_v6.md`.*

Care is the seventh of Part VI's material-substrate cultivation terrains, and it closes both the Part and a coverage gap. The fifth-pass arc specified the polity's relation to land, food and soil, energy, materials, the built environment, and the health of the body — and then stopped, at the threshold of the work through which every one of those substrates is converted into an actual sustained human life: the work of care. The omission was not idiosyncratic. It was the omission the arc had inherited from the liberal political theory it was written against: the theory that treats the household, the raising of children, the tending of the ill and the old, the daily reproduction of persons, as a private and pre-political matter, antecedent to the polity and outside the constitutional frame — and that builds its account of the polity around a citizen who arrives at the public sphere already grown, already fed, already well, as though no labor had produced him. This document closes that gap. It specifies care work, kinship, and social reproduction as cultivation terrain — the relational substrate through which the polity is reproduced across generations — and as constitutional terrain that the constitutional architecture may not pass over.

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad — biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation — articulated in full in Document III.01 and not re-argued here. For care the triad bears with particular directness. Biocultural governance is foregrounded: care work, kinship, and social reproduction are the relational substrate of the biocultural formation — the work and the relations through which the polity, understood as a complex adaptive system, is actually reproduced across generations. Disturbance-adapted implementation bears because social reproduction is the intergenerational work the whole architecture depends upon, and because the contemporary crisis of care is itself a disturbance the architecture must absorb. Eco-mimetic policy-making bears on the closing specification of the care architecture the constitutional order must enable. The document foregrounds no single disturbance regime; it treats the political-economic regime where care is marketized and the cultural regime where care is gendered and racialized.

The document assumes the constitutional ontology and nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, in which kinship is engaged as a biocultural formation, and the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01. It rests alongside Document VI.06, the bodily-substrate document, with which it shares the bodily-and-relational substrate frame, and it cites Document VI.06 rather than re-deriving the health substance. It does not duplicate the neurodivergent-polis analysis of Document XII.05; it engages the disability politics of care-giving and care-receiving and cross-references that document. It does not specify the bounded decision rights or the cultivation protocol; those are the work of Part XII and Part XIII, which the document points forward to. The analytical work proceeds in four phases across nine threads: the establishment of care as cultivation terrain; the marketization-of-care diagnostic; the engagement of six primary-articulator traditions — the feminist political theory of care, social-reproduction theory, the Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition, the Black-feminist and women-of-color care tradition, the queer-kinship tradition, and the disability-justice critique of care-as-burden — each in its own register and none collapsed into another; and the closing cultivation specification.

# Part One — Care, Kinship, and Social Reproduction as Cultivation Terrain

## Thread A — The Relational Substrate of the Polity

Every polity is reproduced, generation by generation, by work. Persons are born and must be birthed; they are helpless for years and must be raised; they fall ill and must be tended; they age and must be cared for; and across the whole of every life the daily round of feeding, cleaning, comforting, teaching, and sustaining must be performed, by someone, or the life does not continue. This work — care work, in the broad sense, together with the kinship relations within which it is conducted and the wider process of social reproduction it constitutes — is the relational substrate of the polity. It is the substrate on which every other substrate of Part VI is finally converted into a living person: the land grows food, but a person must cook it and feed it to a child; the built environment provides shelter, but a household must make it a home; health, as Document VI.06 specified, is cultivated within conditions, and care work is the daily labor through which the conditions are tended. Without this work, continuously performed, the polity simply ceases — not metaphorically but literally, within a single generation.

The dominant tradition of political theory has not denied this work. It has done something more consequential: it has placed the work outside the frame. Liberal political theory, in its classic form, builds its account of the polity around the citizen — the rights-bearing, contracting, deliberating individual who appears in the public sphere as a finished participant — and it locates the production of that individual in a private and pre-political domain, the household, which the theory treats as antecedent to the polity and therefore as not the polity's constitutional concern. The citizen arrives already grown; the labor and the relations that grew him are, by the theory's architecture, invisible. The cultivation framing of this project rejects the placement, and the rejection is the analytical move this thread makes. The reproduction of the polity across generations is not antecedent to the polity; it is the polity's continuance, the literal condition of there being a polity at all in the next generation, and a constitutional architecture that treats it as a private matter outside the frame has omitted the substrate on which the architecture itself depends. Care, kinship, and social reproduction are constitutional terrain.

So understood, care operates across the nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, and three of its scales are load-bearing for this document. At the household scale, care is the daily round of reproductive labor conducted within a domestic unit — and the document will not assume, as Thread G insists, that the unit takes one normative form. At the kin-network scale, care is conducted across the wider web of kin and chosen kin, the network of relations within which households are embedded and across which care, resources, and persons themselves move. At the community scale, care is conducted through the institutions and relations of a wider community — the othermothering networks that Thread F specifies, the care webs that Thread H specifies, the community-scale institutions through which a polity tends those its households and kin networks cannot tend alone. A care architecture adequate to the relational substrate must address all three scales; this document specifies care as cultivation terrain across each. The threads that follow engage, in their own registers, the traditions that have specified what care is and what its cultivation requires.

# Part Two — The Marketization-of-Care Diagnostic

## Thread B — Care as a Commodifiable Service

The framing the cultivation account rejects is the marketization-of-care framing: the understanding of care as a service — a discrete, specifiable, commodifiable, outsourceable, and priceable service — that can be purchased on a market like any other. The framing is, in the precise sense this project's diagnostics have used the term, a design-imposition: it imposes on the relational substrate of the polity the model of a market transaction, and it builds, on that model, the contemporary organization of care.

The marketization framing's signature is a contradiction it cannot resolve and a crisis the contradiction produces. The contradiction is this: the framing simultaneously demands that care work be done — the children must be raised, the ill and old must be tended, the demand for care is absolute and inelastic — and refuses to value or resource the work at anything like its actual worth. Care work, where it is unpaid, is rendered invisible as a natural emanation of love or duty rather than recognized as labor; where it is paid, it is among the lowest-waged, least-secure, least-respected work in the economy, and it is performed disproportionately by women, and within that, disproportionately by racialized and migrant women, onto whom the care needs of wealthier households and a withdrawing public sector are offloaded. The contemporary crisis of care is the sharpening of this contradiction: a social order that requires more care than ever — as populations age, as households hold fewer adults, as public provision withdraws — while resourcing it less, and while placing on the relations care depends on a strain the marketization framing both produces and conceals. The framing conceals the strain because, having defined care as a service, it can register only what appears on a market — the priced transaction — and is structurally blind to the unpaid work, the depleted caregiver, the eroded kin network, and the relation worn thin, none of which the price captures.

The diagnostic's analytical move is to ask what survives of organized and paid care under the cultivation framing and what does not. The move is not the rejection of paid care, and the document is precise about this. What survives, and survives as a genuine good, is resourced, skilled, supported care work: the paid care worker properly waged, trained, secure, and respected; the care institution — the nursery, the clinic, the residence — properly resourced; the recognition that some care is rightly organized, specialized, and collective, and that to romanticize all care as unpaid family labor would be to consign caregivers, overwhelmingly women, to unpaid servitude. A cultivation framing affirms the goods of well-resourced organized care, and a constitutional architecture must secure them. What does not survive is the framing: the construction of care as a service abstracted from the relation it is conducted within; the commodity logic that prices the transaction and is blind to everything the price omits; and the devaluation that the marketization framing both produces — by treating care as low-skilled, substitutable, and cheap — and conceals, by registering only the market and not the depleted relational substrate beneath it. The marketization-of-care framing is the design-imposition under diagnosis; the six traditions that follow specify the relational substrate it abstracts away.

# Part Three — The Feminist Political Theory of Care

## Thread C — Dependency Is Universal and Care Is Political

The feminist political theory of care engaged in this thread stands at primary-articulator standing. It is the tradition that, more directly than any other, named the omission this document's Thread A specified — the placement of care outside the political frame — and demanded that care be recognized as a political category.

Joan Tronto's *Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care* (1993) supplies the tradition's foundational political claim. Tronto defines care broadly, as everything done to maintain, continue, and repair the world so that beings can live in it as well as possible, and her central analytical move concerns the moral boundaries her title names: the boundaries by which care has been walled off from politics, assigned to the private sphere, and allocated to women and to the less powerful, so that those with power are excused from it. *Caring Democracy: Markets, Equality, and Justice* (2013) carries the claim to its constitutional form: Tronto specifies that the allocation of caring responsibilities is itself a question of democratic justice, names the pattern by which the powerful are released from care as privileged irresponsibility, and argues that a genuine democracy is one that takes the just distribution of caring responsibilities as a central political question rather than a private accident. Care, in Tronto's specification, is not a private virtue; it is a political category, and its distribution is a constitutional matter.

Virginia Held's *The Ethics of Care: Personal, Political, and Global* (2006) supplies the tradition's moral-philosophical claim. Held specifies care ethics as a distinct moral framework — not a subordinate supplement to the dominant moral theories of the justice tradition, the Kantian and the utilitarian, but a rival and a corrective to them. Where those theories begin from the abstract, independent, rational agent and reason about the principles that should govern relations among such agents, care ethics begins from the relationship — from the recognition that persons are constituted in and through relations of care and dependency, and that a moral theory built on the fiction of the independent agent has begun from a falsehood. Eva Feder Kittay's *Love's Labor: Essays on Women, Equality, and Dependency* (1999) presses that point to its sharpest edge. Kittay specifies that dependency is not an exceptional condition affecting some unfortunate persons but the universal human condition: every person was, as an infant, wholly dependent, and every person will likely be dependent again in illness and in age, and many are dependent throughout. The liberal fiction of the independent citizen — the citizen imagined, in Kittay's reading of the dominant theory, as a fully cooperating member of society across a complete life — erases this universal fact, and in erasing it erases also the dependency worker, the one whose labor sustains the dependent, and who is herself rendered vulnerable by the work. Kittay's principle of doulia — a public, collective responsibility to support those who care, so that the caregiver is herself cared for — names what follows. And Sara Ruddick's *Maternal Thinking: Toward a Politics of Peace* (1989) specifies that caring work is not the absence of thought but the practice of a distinctive rationality: maternal practice, which Ruddick is careful to specify can be conducted by anyone of any gender, generates its own discipline of attention, judgment, and reasoning, oriented to the preservation and growth of vulnerable life.

For the cultivation framing, the feminist political theory of care establishes three things the constitutional architecture must carry. Dependency is universal: the architecture must be built for the actual human being, who is dependent at the beginning and the end of life and intermittently throughout, not for the fictional independent citizen. Care is political: its distribution is a question of justice, and the pattern of privileged irresponsibility is a constitutional failure. And an architecture built on the independent-citizen fiction has, by that founding move, already failed the relational substrate — has built itself on a person who does not exist, sustained by labor it declines to see.

# Part Four — Social-Reproduction Theory

## Thread D — The Substrate the Political Economy Refuses to Sustain

Social-reproduction theory, engaged in this thread, shares with the feminist political theory of care the recognition that care work is systematically devalued, but it operates in a distinct register and reaches a distinct claim: it is a materialist analysis of how the political economy itself depends on, exploits, and refuses to sustain the work of social reproduction. The two traditions are not collapsed; the care-ethics tradition of Thread C is a political and moral theory of care as a category, and social-reproduction theory is a political-economic theory of reproductive labor's place in the structure of capitalism.

Silvia Federici's *Caliban and the Witch: Women, the Body and Primitive Accumulation* (2004) supplies the tradition's historical analysis. Federici specifies that the gendered division of labor — the assignment of reproductive work to women and its devaluation as non-labor — is not a natural inheritance but a historical production, and she locates a central moment of that production in the witch hunts of early-modern Europe, which she reads as integral to the primitive accumulation that founded capitalism: a campaign of terror that disciplined women's bodies, expropriated their reproductive autonomy, and forced reproductive labor into an unwaged condition that the emerging capitalist order required. Her earlier *Wages Against Housework* (1975), a foundational text of the Wages for Housework campaign, specified the political demand that follows: that reproductive labor be recognized as labor — the demand for wages functioning less as a request for payment than as a refusal of the mystification by which housework is rendered invisible as a labor of love rather than seen as the work that it is.

Nancy Fraser's work supplies the tradition's analysis of the present. Across *Fortunes of Feminism* (2013) and *The Old Is Dying and the New Cannot Be Born* (2019), and in her account of the crisis of care, Fraser specifies a structural contradiction at the heart of the capitalist political economy: the economy depends absolutely on social reproduction — on the work of birthing, raising, caring for, and sustaining the people who are its workers and its citizens — and yet it does not pay for that work, does not replenish the social-reproductive capacities it consumes, and tends, in its drive to expand, to destabilize the very reproductive substrate it requires. The crisis of care of the present moment is, in Fraser's analysis, this contradiction sharpened by a particular phase of capitalism — one that has drawn adults of every gender into waged work while withdrawing public provision, so that the time and the capacity for social reproduction are squeezed from both sides at once. Evelyn Nakano Glenn's *Forced to Care: Coercion and Caregiving in America* (2010) specifies the racialized dimension the analysis requires: care labor in the United States has a long history of coercion — through slavery, through the legal and social compulsion of women, and through the racialized division by which care work has been pushed onto women of color and immigrant women as a matter of imposed status obligation rather than free choice. And Arlie Russell Hochschild's *The Second Shift* (1989) and *The Time Bind* (1997) supply the analysis at the scale of the household and the day: the second shift of domestic and caring labor that working women perform after the waged workday, the stalled revolution that left that shift unequally distributed, and the time bind in which waged work colonizes the hours and the energies that social reproduction requires.

For the cultivation framing, social-reproduction theory establishes that care is the substrate the political economy depends on and structurally refuses to sustain — that the devaluation of care is not an oversight to be corrected by better attitudes but a structural feature of the political economy, and that a constitutional architecture which leaves that political economy's relation to social reproduction unaddressed has left the crisis of care to deepen by the system's own logic.

# Part Five — Indigenous Kinship as Constitutive

## Thread E — Kinship as the Structure of Political Life

The Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition engaged in this thread stands at primary-articulator standing, and it is, with the feminist political theory of care, one of the document's two load-bearing traditions — held, as the plan requires, at co-equal primary-articulator standing with that tradition, neither subordinated to the other and neither synthesized into the other's register. The distinction is the thread's central discipline, and it must be stated at the outset. The feminist political theory of care, engaged in Thread C, operates within a frame in which there is a public sphere and a private sphere, and its claim is that care has been wrongly assigned to the private and must be brought into the political. The Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition makes a different claim. It does not argue that kinship should be brought into the political; it specifies that kinship is the political — that for Indigenous polities kinship was never a private domain at all, but the very structure of political life, the architecture of nationhood itself. The tradition is engaged here under the multi-eyed-seeing discipline of Document VII.01, each scholar in their own register, and it must not be absorbed, as the plan's second What-to-Avoid insists, into the feminist care frame as a set of "Indigenous family values" appended to a Western theory.

Kim TallBear (Sisseton Wahpeton Oyate) articulates one strand. *Native American DNA: Tribal Belonging and the False Promise of Genetic Science* (2013) specifies that Indigenous belonging is not a matter of blood or genetic ancestry — that the genetic definition of Native identity is itself a settler imposition — but a matter of kinship, of political relation, of being in sustained relation with a people. Her essay "Making Love and Relations Beyond Settler Sex and Family" (2018) carries the analysis into a direct critique of what TallBear names settler sexuality and the imposition of the nuclear family: the colonial project, she specifies, worked partly through the suppression of expansive Indigenous kinship forms and the imposition of the monogamous, biological, property-transmitting nuclear household, and Indigenous resurgence accordingly includes the rebuilding of kinship as a made and extended relation — relations of care and obligation that exceed the biological and the marital, and that extend, in many Indigenous traditions, beyond the human, to the other-than-human relatives that Document III.01's engagement with Indigenous ontology specified. Audra Simpson (Mohawk), in *Mohawk Interruptus: Political Life Across the Borders of Settler States* (2014), specifies kinship's directly political function: in her study of Kahnawà:ke, Simpson shows that Haudenosaunee kinship is the substance of political membership — that the determination of who belongs to the nation is constituted through kinship and clan, and that this kinship-constituted membership operates as an act of sovereignty, a refusal of the settler state's authority to determine who is and is not a member. Vine Deloria Jr. (Standing Rock Sioux), in *Custer Died for Your Sins: An Indian Manifesto* (1969) and *God Is Red: A Native View of Religion* (1973), specifies kinship as the organizing principle of Indigenous social and political life as such — the extended kin unit, not the individual and not the abstract citizen, as the basic unit of the polity, and relation, rather than contract, as the bond from which political life is built.

What the Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition articulates that the feminist care tradition does not can now be stated as the thread's finding, and the document lets the difference stand rather than resolving it. The feminist care tradition specifies that care has been wrongly privatized and must be recognized as political; it works to move care across a public-private boundary it takes as given. The Indigenous tradition specifies that kinship constitutes the polity — that there is no prior public-private boundary to move care across, because kinship is itself the structure of political life, the architecture of nationhood, and a domain that extends beyond the human. These are distinct claims, and at points they are in tension: the feminist tradition's project of de-privatizing care presupposes the very public-private architecture the Indigenous tradition says was never the structure of Indigenous polities and was, in its imposition, an instrument of colonization. The cultivation framing carries both, at co-equal standing, and the constitutional specification of Thread I must answer to both: to the feminist demand that care be resourced and its distribution made just, and to the Indigenous specification that kinship is constitutive and that its forms are a matter of Indigenous sovereignty.

# Part Six — Black-Feminist and Women-of-Color Care

## Thread F — Care Under Racialized Extraction, and the Kin Networks That Resist It

The Black-feminist and women-of-color care tradition engaged in this thread stands at primary-articulator standing. It articulates what neither the feminist political theory of care nor social-reproduction theory, in their general registers, fully reaches: care as it is conducted under the specific conditions of racialized poverty and racialized care-extraction, and the kin networks that have survived, sustained life, and resisted under those conditions.

Patricia Hill Collins, in *Black Feminist Thought: Knowledge, Consciousness, and the Politics of Empowerment* (1990), specifies the institution of othermothering. Hill Collins, writing as a Black feminist sociologist, documents the long tradition in which Black women have shared the work of mothering — bloodmothers and othermothers together raising children across households, in women-centered networks that the nuclear-family model cannot see — and she specifies the further institution of community othermothering, in which that ethic of care is extended to the community itself, becoming the ground of Black women's community-building and political activism. Othermothering, in Hill Collins's analysis, is not a deficient substitute for nuclear-family care; it is a distinct and resilient kinship form with its own ethic and its own intergenerational depth. Carol Stack's *All Our Kin: Strategies for Survival in a Black Community* (1974) supplies the close ethnographic study that documents the same structure on the ground: in the poor Black community Stack studied, survival depended on dense, extended networks of kin and fictive kin, across which goods, services, childcare, and children themselves moved — the practice of child-keeping, in which children were raised across several households as need and capacity shifted — networks that distributed scarce resources, absorbed shocks, and constituted a kinship structure that the nuclear-family-focused welfare system was structurally unable to recognize or support.

Audre Lorde's *A Burst of Light: Essays* (1988) specifies care turned toward the self under conditions designed to destroy it. Writing as a Black lesbian feminist poet, and writing the essays of *A Burst of Light* while living with the cancer that would kill her, Lorde articulated the line that has carried furthest: that caring for herself was not self-indulgence but self-preservation, and that under conditions built to grind Black women down, self-preservation is an act of political warfare. The document engages Lorde's claim precisely, and against its commodified misappropriation: Lorde's self-care is not the marketized wellness of Thread B's diagnostic but its opposite — the deliberate, defiant tending of a life that the social order has made expendable. Gloria Anzaldúa's *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza* (1987) specifies care and kinship in the borderland: writing as a Chicana feminist from the US-Mexico border region, Anzaldúa articulates the caring labor, the contradictions, and the kin relations of the borderland — the family as both a source of sustenance and a site of constraint for the woman who lives between worlds — and the consciousness, the new mestiza consciousness, forged in holding those contradictions.

For the cultivation framing, the Black-feminist and women-of-color care tradition establishes that care is not conducted in a neutral medium: it is conducted under conditions of racialized poverty and racialized extraction, conditions under which care work has been coerced from women of color, as Thread D's engagement with Glenn specified, and under which the kin networks that sustain life — othermothering, the extended survival networks, the chosen and fictive kin of communities the dominant institutions could not see — are themselves achievements of resistance. A constitutional architecture that specified care in the abstract, without this tradition, would specify care for a population that does not exist.

# Part Seven — Queer Kinship

## Thread G — Kinship Made and Chosen

The queer-kinship tradition engaged in this thread specifies a claim the document's cultivation specification cannot do without: that kinship is made and chosen, that the kinship the state recognizes is partial and exclusionary, and that the cultivation of care relations therefore cannot be tied to any single normative family form. The thread coordinates with the sixth-pass sexuality-and-gender thread carried in Documents XII.11 and XII.12 and does not duplicate it.

Kath Weston's *Families We Choose: Lesbians, Gays, Kinship* (1991) supplies the tradition's foundational ethnographic articulation. Weston's study of gay and lesbian kinship documented the families we choose — the chosen families that gay men and lesbians, often cut off from or unsafe within their families of origin, built through love, commitment, and sustained mutual care rather than through blood or law. Weston's analytical point is that chosen family is not an imitation of "real" kinship nor a deficient substitute for it; it is a reconstruction of kinship, a demonstration that kinship can be constituted through choice and ongoing care, and a refutation of the assumption that biological and legal relation is the only ground on which durable kinship can stand. Judith Butler's *Antigone's Claim: Kinship Between Life and Death* (2000) supplies the tradition's analysis of the state and the limit. Through the figure of Antigone — who stands at the boundary between kinship and the state, claiming a kinship the state will not recognize — Butler interrogates the structuralist account that had treated kinship as a fixed and universal symbolic order, and specifies that the kinship the state recognizes as legitimate is a particular and exclusionary selection, that there is always a kinship that falls outside the state's recognition, and that the question of which kinship arrangements are granted legitimacy is a question of power.

For the cultivation framing, the queer-kinship tradition establishes that the relational substrate this document specifies is plural in its forms — that kinship is made and chosen as well as inherited, that the state's recognition of kinship is partial and has historically excluded, and that a constitutional architecture which cultivated care while quietly assuming the nuclear family as the unit of care would have built the exclusion into its foundation. The cultivation of care relations must be available to kinship in its plural forms; this is a constraint Thread I carries, and it converges — without being merged into — the Indigenous tradition's account of expansive, made kinship in Thread E and the Black-feminist account of othermothering and fictive kin in Thread F, three distinct traditions arriving, from three distinct histories, at the shared refusal of the single normative family form.

# Part Eight — The Disability-Justice Critique of Care-as-Burden, and Elder Care

## Thread H — Interdependence, Care Webs, and the Care-Receiver as Agent

The disability-justice critique of care-as-burden engaged in this thread stands at primary-articulator standing, and the thread is load-bearing for a specific reason: it corrects the feminist political theory of care of Thread C at the precise point where that tradition is most vulnerable. The document does not synthesize the correction away; it lets it stand as a correction, one tradition revising another.

The vulnerability is this. The feminist care tradition, in framing its analysis around dependency and the dependency worker — Kittay's terms — risks casting the care-receiver as a dependent: an object of care, a recipient, a person to whom care happens and who figures in the analysis as a need rather than as an agent. Pressed further, in the wider culture if not in Kittay's careful text, that framing produces the figure of the disabled or ill or aged person as a burden — the one whose care is a cost, a weight borne by others. The disability-justice tradition refuses that figure. The disability-justice care literature — Mia Mingus's writing on access intimacy, the elusive ease of being with someone who understands one's access needs without explanation, and on care webs, the distributed, collective, multidirectional networks of support that disabled people build in place of reliance on the state, the market, or the isolated nuclear household; and Patty Berne and the Sins Invalid collective's *Skin, Tooth, and Bone: The Basis of Movement Is Our People* (2019), which articulates the principles of disability justice, a framework developed by disabled queer and trans people of color and grounded in interdependence, collective access, and the leadership of those most affected — specifies care otherwise. Care, in the disability-justice articulation, is not the one-directional flow from an able caregiver to a dependent recipient; it is mutual, reciprocal, and collective. The care-receiver is an agent — a person who also gives care, who directs their own care, whose needs are not a burden but an ordinary part of the human interdependence that everyone shares. The Care Collective's *The Care Manifesto: The Politics of Interdependence* (2020) generalizes the frame: interdependence, not dependency, is the human condition, and a caring politics builds outward from it at every scale. And Margaret Cruikshank's *Learning to Be Old: Gender, Culture, and Aging* (2003) carries the analysis into elder care, specifying the cultural devaluation of old age and the way ageism constructs the aged person, like the disabled person, as a diminished dependent rather than as a continuing agent within the web of care.

The correction the disability-justice tradition makes to the feminist care tradition can now be stated precisely, and the document holds it as a correction rather than dissolving the tension. Where the feminist tradition frames dependency, the disability-justice tradition frames interdependence — the ongoing, universal, multidirectional condition, not the asymmetrical relation of a dependent to a worker. Where the feminist tradition can imply the dyad of caregiver and cared-for, the disability-justice tradition specifies the care web — the distributed, collective unit of care. And where the feminist tradition can position the care-receiver as object, the disability-justice tradition insists on the care-receiver as agent. The cultivation framing carries the correction: the care it specifies for cultivation in Thread I is care drafted with the care-receiver as agent, interdependence as the frame, and the care web rather than the dyad as the cultivation unit.

# Part Nine — The Cultivation Specification and the Bridge to the Decision-Rights Architecture

## Thread I — What the Constitutional Architecture Must Enable

The eight preceding threads have established the document's ground. Care, kinship, and social reproduction are the relational substrate of the polity, constitutional terrain rather than a private matter outside the frame (Thread A). The marketization-of-care framing is a design-imposition that constructs care as a commodifiable service and produces the contemporary crisis of care (Thread B). The feminist political theory of care establishes that dependency is universal, that care is political, and that the independent-citizen fiction has already failed the relational substrate (Thread C). Social-reproduction theory establishes that care is the substrate the political economy depends on and structurally refuses to sustain (Thread D). The Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition establishes, at co-equal primary-articulator standing, that kinship is the structure of political life itself and that its forms are a matter of Indigenous sovereignty (Thread E). The Black-feminist and women-of-color tradition establishes that care is conducted under racialized extraction and that the kin networks sustaining life are achievements of resistance (Thread F). The queer-kinship tradition establishes that kinship is made and chosen and that care cannot be tied to one normative family form (Thread G). And the disability-justice tradition establishes, correcting the feminist tradition, that the frame is interdependence, the unit is the care web, and the care-receiver is an agent (Thread H). This closing thread specifies what a constitutional architecture must enable for care and kinship to be cultivated rather than marketized, and bridges to the decision-rights architecture of Part XII and the synthesis of Part XIII.

The cultivation specification must be guarded, as the parallel specifications of the Part VI peers were, against a design-imposition slip-back. The document has argued against the marketization-of-care framing; the symmetrical error would be to engineer, in its place, a replacement care system designed whole and in advance — a single specified architecture of care institutions imposed downward. The cultivation framing does not design the care system. It specifies conditions — the conditions under which care and kinship are cultivated, plurally and locally, by the households, kin networks, communities, and peoples whose relational life is at stake — and what follows is a specification of conditions in that sense, answering to all six traditions without collapsing them into one.

The first condition is that care work be resourced and valued as constitutional terrain. The constitutional architecture must hold care work — paid and unpaid alike — as constitutional terrain rather than a private matter, must resource it, and must address the structural devaluation that Thread D specified as a feature of the political economy rather than an accident of attitude. This is the feminist and social-reproduction traditions' demand carried to constitutional form, and it includes the just redistribution of caring responsibilities against the pattern of privileged irresponsibility.

The second condition is that kinship be recognized in plural forms. The constitutional architecture must not tie the cultivation of care to one normative family form. It must recognize and support care relations across the plural kinship forms that Threads E, F, and G specified — the expansive and made kinship of the Indigenous tradition, whose forms are a matter of Indigenous sovereignty; the othermothering and extended survival networks of the Black-feminist tradition; the chosen families of the queer-kinship tradition — and it must do so without subordinating any of these to the nuclear-family model whose quiet assumption Thread G named as the exclusion to be refused.

The third condition is that care webs be supported at the household and community scales. Following the disability-justice correction of Thread H, the constitutional architecture must support care as interdependence, conducted through distributed care webs rather than isolated dyads, with the care-receiver as agent, at the household, kin-network, and community scales of the nested-scales frame.

The fourth condition is that the universal fact of dependency and interdependence be built into the architecture. The constitutional architecture must be built for the actual human being — dependent at the beginning and end of life and intermittently throughout, and interdependent always — rather than for the independent-citizen fiction that Thread C identified as the founding error. This is the deepest of the conditions, because it is a condition on the architecture's anthropology: the polity the architecture is built for is a polity of interdependent beings sustained by care, and the architecture must say so from its foundation.

These conditions are what this document specifies and hands forward. Document XII.11, on provisioning decision rights, is where care provisioning — the resourcing of care work and the support of care webs — is operationalized as a bounded decision right; this document names what XII.11 must instantiate and does not perform the rights specification. Document XII.12, on cognitive-and-intergenerational decision rights, carries the intergenerational dimension of social reproduction — the reproduction of the polity across generations that this document specified as the substrate's defining work. Document XII.04, on intergenerational rectification, carries the bridge's intergenerational-justice dimension, and Document XII.05, on the neurodivergent polis, carries forward, in coordination with Thread H, the disability dimension of care at the level of constitutional architecture. Document XIII.01 integrates the care-cultivation conditions specified here with the conditions specified across the arc into the cultivation protocol, and Document XIV.01 reads this document, in the capstone, as the relational-substrate document that closes Part VI — the document that specifies the care, kinship, and social reproduction through which the polity is itself reproduced across the generations.

Care, kinship, and social reproduction are cultivation terrain — the relational substrate through which the polity continues — not a private matter the constitutional architecture may pass over. A constitutional order that treats the work of reproducing persons as pre-political, and care as a commodifiable service, has omitted the substrate on which the order itself depends, and the crisis of care, the depleted caregivers, the unvalued and racialized care labor, and the eroded kin networks are the record of the omission. The cultivation framing's alternative is the one this document has specified: care, kinship, and social reproduction held as constitutional terrain; six distinct traditions engaged at primary-articulator standing and their tensions left to stand; care work resourced and valued; kinship recognized in plural forms; care webs supported as interdependence; and the universal fact of dependency built into the architecture's foundation. Of the relational substrate, as of the land, the food, the energy, the materials, the shelter, and the health that Part VI has specified before it, the project's summary phrase holds: the polity is grown, not built — and it is care, finally, that does the growing.
