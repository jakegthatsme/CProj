## Preface — The Inquiry Under the Sixth-Pass Architecture

The sixth-pass constitutional project rests on a load-bearing triad: biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation. The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the triad's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery of what those traditions have carried far longer. "Grown, not built" is the colloquial summary phrase.

This document re-reads the founders' anthropological assumptions — Lockean possessive individualism, the social-contract framing, the bounded-rational-actor model, the property-as-natural-right premise — not as merely incomplete on the modern evolutionary, cognitive, and anthropological evidence but as ontologically mistaken about what kind of formation the polity is. The polity is a biocultural formation cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a complex adaptive system, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled. The founders' four assumptions are design-imposition framings: they mistake the kind of formation in front of them and therefore mis-specify the architectural problem the implementation must solve. The analytical work of this document is to name that mistake precisely and to count what it costs the implementation.

The founding-era documentary record itself — the 1776 and 1787 texts, the Federalist and Anti-Federalist debates, and the founders' post-ratification reflections — is engaged in Part I across Document I.02 (Founding Texts and the 1787 Implementation), Document I.03 (the Federalist–Anti-Federalist Records), and Document I.04 (the Founders in Hindsight). This document reads the anthropological assumptions underlying that record rather than the record itself.

The document stands in continuity with the project record. The evolutionary, cognitive, and anthropological analysis the prior passes developed is preserved through a re-framing that does two things. First, the four founding-era assumptions are read together as one integrated design-imposition framing rather than as four separate empirical errors. Second, the document coordinates with Document IV.01 (Part IV — Deep Substrate) on the relocation of the cultural-evolutionary scaling mechanisms, the comparative-anthropology-of-governance archive, and the sub-species evolutionary backdrop, per the holding file at `project/relocations/Document_4_relocations.md`. The relocated material is preserved in its move; what remains here is the founding-era critique focused on the four design-imposition framings as Part II diagnostic material.

The founders are engaged on their own analytical terms — not as moral failures to be condemned, not as subjects of presentist correction. They were eighteenth-century thinkers working with the analytical resources their period made available. The Lockean possessive-individualism premise was the dominant English political philosophy they had inherited; the social-contract framing was the prevailing mode through which European political philosophy specified the polity's foundations; the bounded-rational-actor assumption tracked the cognitive theory of their period; the property-as-natural-right premise was constitutive of the political-economic order they were attempting to legitimate. The critique developed here is not that they should have known better in a moral or psychological register, but that the analytical resources they worked with were inadequate to the kind of formation the polity is, and the implementation they produced operationalized that inadequacy. Modern evolutionary, cognitive, and anthropological evidence does not show the founders to have been confused; it shows them to have worked within design-imposition framings the evidence cannot accommodate.

Positioned as Part II diagnostic foundation, this document is the analytical ground against which the five disturbance-regime diagnostics — Document II.02 (cultural), Document II.04 (biological), Document II.05 (physical-environmental), Document II.06 (cognitive-and-informational), Document II.07 (political-economic) — and the cross-regime interactions diagnostic, Document II.08, are read. ## Part One — The Rational Actor and the Behavioral Revolution: Bounded-Rational-Actor as Design-Imposition Framing

The founders' political theory rested on a model of the human as bounded but rational actor: capable of deliberation in matters of public concern; susceptible to passion, faction, and self-interest but corrigible through institutional design; substantially uniform across the categories the model recognized — the propertied white male citizen the polity took as its operational subject. The model was sophisticated for its period. *Federalist 10*'s analysis of faction, *Federalist 51*'s analysis of separation of powers, and the broader engagement with the conditions under which rational deliberation might produce republican outcomes articulate a serious reckoning with the cognitive and motivational conditions of governance. The critique here is not that the founders failed to anticipate modern cognitive science; it is that the bounded-rational-actor framing mistakes the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale of the nested-scales reference frame.

### The Core Assumption and Its Failure on the Cognitive-Bias Evidence

The cognitive-bias literature developed under Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's heuristics-and-biases program — initiated through "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" (Tversky and Kahneman, *Science*, 1974), elaborated through Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), and extended through the wider behavioral-economics tradition — establishes that human cognition operates through systematic departures from the rational-actor model: availability heuristics that overweight vivid recent instances; representativeness heuristics that mistake similarity for probability; anchoring effects that bind judgment to arbitrary starting points; loss aversion that asymmetrically weights losses over gains; present bias that discounts the future; framing effects that produce different decisions on logically equivalent presentations. The evidence holds across decades of experimental and observational research and across several methodological approaches.

Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's *Nudge: Improving Decisions about Health, Wealth, and Happiness* (2008) attempted to translate the cognitive-bias evidence into design recommendations for institutional architecture, an effort Thaler continued through *Misbehaving: The Making of Behavioral Economics* (2015) and Sunstein through his regulatory-state corpus. The methodological caveats raised against parts of the cognitive-bias literature deserve their own engagement rather than dismissal: the replication crisis in social psychology has reduced confidence in some heuristics-and-biases findings; Gerd Gigerenzer's *Rationality for Mortals: How People Cope with Uncertainty* (2008) and his ecological-rationality program argue that many apparent biases are well-adapted responses to real-world information structures; Herbert Simon's foundational work on bounded rationality and Vernon Smith's experimental economics complicate any flat reading of cognition as defective.

Taken together, this literature does not show the rational-actor model to be wrong in the simple sense that human cognition is "irrational." It shows the rational-actor model to be inadequate to the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale. Human cognition operates with wide variability across persons, contexts, and conditions, and the variability is structural rather than noise. The bounded-rational-actor framing fails because it treats the cognitive substrate as a uniform input to deliberative architecture when the substrate is plural, contextual, and varying.

### The Cognitive-Plurality and Neurodivergence Literature

Cognitive plurality is articulated at primary-articulator standing well beyond the cognitive-bias literature, in the disability-justice and neurodivergent traditions. The disability-justice tradition — Mia Mingus on access intimacy; Patty Berne and the Sins Invalid corpus including *Skin, Tooth, and Bone: The Basis of Movement Is Our People* (2016); Eli Clare's *Exile and Pride: Disability, Queerness, and Liberation* (1999) and *Brilliant Imperfection: Grappling with Cure* (2017); Alison Kafer's *Feminist, Queer, Crip* (2013); Robert McRuer's *Crip Theory* (2006); Sami Schalk's *Bodyminds Reimagined: (Dis)ability, Race, and Gender in Black Women's Speculative Fiction* (2018); Margaret Price's *Mad at School: Rhetorics of Mental Disability and Academic Life* (2011); and Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha's *Care Work: Dreaming Disability Justice* (2018) — names the cognitive-and-embodied substrate as a site of knowledge rather than of deficit. Schalk and Price together carry the bodymind framework, the refusal to treat cognition as separable from the embodied life that conducts it. The Mad-studies tradition — China Mills's *Decolonizing Global Mental Health: The Psychiatrization of the Majority World* (2014), Brendan Stone on Mad studies in higher education, Bonnie Burstow's *Psychiatry and the Business of Madness* (2015), and the Asylum Magazine corpus — extends the engagement to a critique of the psychiatric system itself. The neurodivergent constitutional articulators — Steve Silberman's *NeuroTribes: The Legacy of Autism and the Future of Neurodiversity* (2015), Devon Price's *Unmasking Autism: Discovering the New Faces of Neurodiversity* (2022), Robert Chapman on neurodivergent personhood, and Damian Milton's "The Double Empathy Problem" (*Disability and Society*, 2012) — name neurodivergence as a form of personhood, not a departure from a cognitive baseline.

The Eastern primary articulators carry their own analysis of cognitive substrate. The Buddhist *bhavanga* and Abhidhamma consciousness analysis — through Bhikkhu Bodhi's *A Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma* (1993) and the *Visuddhimagga* tradition — engages cognition as process rather than as fixed property, as does Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka two-truths analysis in Jay Garfield's translation and his *Engaging Buddhism: Why It Matters to Philosophy* (2015). The Daoist contemplative traditions, through the *Zhuangzi*'s "Inner Chapters" on the *zhenren* in Brook Ziporyn's translation (2009), extend the engagement. The Indigenous traditions on relational cognition — Gregory Cajete's (Tewa, of Santa Clara Pueblo) *Native Science: Natural Laws of Interdependence* (2000) and Tyson Yunkaporta's (Apalech Clan) *Sand Talk: How Indigenous Thinking Can Save the World* (2019) — together with the Andean *kawsay* tradition on living cognition, through the PRATEC corpus and Josef Estermann's *Filosofía Andina* (1998), and the Pacific Islander relational-cognition traditions, through Albert Wendt's "Tatauing the Post-Colonial Body" (1996) and Upolu Lumā Vaai and Aisake Casimira's *Relational Hermeneutics* (2017), specify cognition as relational and embodied at primary-articulator standing.

Under the sixth-pass framing, the cognitive-bias literature is recent and partial recovery of what the disability-justice, Mad-studies, neurodivergent, and Eastern primary articulators have long carried. Cognitive plurality at the organism-and-organ-system scale is not an anomaly to be designed around; it is the condition of the cognitive substrate the polity is constituted on. Document XII.05 (Part XII — the neurodivergent polis as cultivation condition) operationalizes this commitment as bounded decision rights at organism-and-organ-system scales.

### The Bounded-Rational-Actor Framing as Design-Imposition

What the bounded-rational-actor framing mistook is the polity as an aggregation of uniform deliberative subjects whose cognitive substrate can be specified in advance of institutional design. The model presumes the deliberative subject as an abstract category — the citizen, the voter, the juror, the legislator — without engaging the cognitive plurality the substrate actually carries. Institutional architecture is then designed against the abstract category, and the operational reality of cognitive plurality produces systematic mismatches between the designed institutions and the people they are meant to serve. The assumption that the polity's deliberative capacity is uniform across persons, contexts, and conditions does not survive contact with the substrate.

What the biocultural-governance framing puts in its place is this: the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale is plural, and the polity is the biocultural formation cultivated through that plurality rather than through its abstraction. The deliberative capacity of the polity is not a property of abstract subjects; it is a cultivation outcome of the conditions under which embodied cognitive plurality engages real deliberation across nested scales. The implementation's failure to register cognitive plurality is not a design defect to be patched; it is the ontological mistake the design-imposition framing produced. Documents XII.03 (e-democracy as structural public check), XII.05 (neurodivergent polis), and II.06 (the cognitive-and-informational-disturbance regime) carry the cognitive-substrate engagement downstream.

The contemporary failure modes the cognitive-bias and cognitive-plurality literatures together specify — the susceptibility of deliberation to manipulation through framing, the disenfranchisement of cognitively plural persons through accommodation failures, and the wider epistemic-injustice architecture documented through Miranda Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing* (2007), José Medina's *The Epistemology of Resistance* (2013), Kristie Dotson on epistemic oppression, and Rebecca Tsosie on Indigenous epistemic injustice — are not anomalies of the implementation. They are the operational signature of the implementation's ontological mistake about the cognitive substrate.

## Part Two — Anthropology and the Social-Contract Framing as Design-Imposition

The founders' political theory rested on the social-contract framing: the claim that the polity's foundations can be derived from a hypothetical pre-political state of nature in which individual subjects, capable of recognizing their own interests, consent to political association on terms specifiable in advance. The Hobbesian, Lockean, and Rousseauian variants differed in important ways, but each held that the social-contract derivation is the appropriate analytical mode for specifying the polity's foundations. The critique here is not that the founders failed to anticipate modern evolutionary anthropology; it is that the social-contract framing mistakes the empirical-historical formation of polities for an analytical-conceptual derivation from a hypothetical state of nature.

### Hobbes, Locke, and the Pre-Political State

Thomas Hobbes's *Leviathan* (1651) articulated the foundational early-modern social-contract framing: absent government, the human condition is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," and political association is rationally chosen by individuals who would otherwise face the war of all against all. John Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) articulated the variant the founders most directly inherited: natural rights to life, liberty, and property are inherent in individuals prior to political association; association is contracted to secure those rights; the legitimacy of government derives from the consent of the governed; the right to alter or abolish a government that fails its purposes is preserved. Jean-Jacques Rousseau's *Of the Social Contract* (1762) articulated a variant in which the contract produces the general will, the *volonté générale*, as the foundational political category.

The founders engaged the social-contract framing as the mode through which they specified the colonial revolutionary claim. The Declaration of Independence's articulation of life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness as unalienable rights, of governments instituted to secure those rights and deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed, and of the right of the people to alter or abolish a government that fails its purposes, is the Lockean framing rendered as a foundational political claim. The Federalist's engagement with the conditions under which the framing could be operationalized through specific institutional architecture is the design-imposition extension.

### The Evolutionary-Anthropological Record

The evolutionary-anthropological record establishes that the social-contract framing's hypothetical state of nature is not a description of how polities actually form. The empirical-historical record of human political organization across deep time, across cultures, and across modes of subsistence shows wide variability in political formations, none of which reduces to the social-contract derivation. Christopher Boehm's *Hierarchy in the Forest: The Evolution of Egalitarian Behavior* (1999) and *Moral Origins: The Evolution of Virtue, Altruism, and Shame* (2012) document hunter-gatherer egalitarianism maintained through reverse-dominance hierarchies. Polly Wiessner's long-term ethnographic and archaeological work on the Ju/'hoansi tracks small-scale political life across decades. David Wengrow and David Graeber's *The Dawn of Everything: A New History of Humanity* (2021) documents the variability of political organization across the deep human past, dismantling any single developmental sequence. Marshall Sahlins's *Stone Age Economics* (1972), including "The Original Affluent Society," Pierre Clastres's *Society Against the State* (1974) on stateless societies' active resistance to state formation, and James C. Scott's *Against the Grain: A Deep History of the Earliest States* (2017) and *The Art of Not Being Governed: An Anarchist History of Upland Southeast Asia* (2009) complete the picture.

The deep historical record establishes that human political organization across the Pleistocene and Holocene operated through multiple distinct modalities — egalitarian band, larger-scale lineage-based polity, horticultural village, agrarian state, trade-and-tribute polity — none of which is well described as derived from a hypothetical state of nature inhabited by abstract individuals. The Indigenous foundational record, engaged at primary-articulator standing in Document I.01, specifies the empirical-historical alternative: the Haudenosaunee Great Law as a historically situated cultivation-condition specification across a confederacy of nations rather than as a derivation from a hypothetical state of nature, and the comparative archive of Indigenous polity-formation across the Americas, Africa, the Pacific, and Eurasia as an empirical record of polities cultivated through specifiable conditions across deep time.

The cooperative-cognitive-primate evidence carries the point to the cognitive substrate itself. Frans de Waal's *Good Natured: The Origins of Right and Wrong in Humans and Other Animals* (1996) and *The Age of Empathy* (2009); Michael Tomasello's *A Natural History of Human Thinking* (2014), *A Natural History of Human Morality* (2016), and *Becoming Human: A Theory of Ontogeny* (2019) on shared intentionality and joint commitment; and Sarah Blaffer Hrdy's *Mothers and Others: The Evolutionary Origins of Mutual Understanding* (2009) on cooperative breeding as the evolutionary-cognitive substrate of distributed responsibility together establish that the cooperative substrate is not an outcome of contract but a condition the contract framing presupposes without acknowledging.

C. B. Macpherson's *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism: Hobbes to Locke* (1962) anchored the modern critical engagement, reading the Lockean-Hobbesian framing as historically specific to seventeenth-century English political-economic conditions rather than as a universal anthropological derivation. The evolutionary-anthropological record has since extended what Macpherson diagnosed in the history of ideas into the empirical history of the species.

### The Lockean Possessive-Individualism Premise Re-Read

The Lockean possessive-individualism premise — that the human is in the first instance a proprietor of his own person and capacities, owing nothing to society for them, with political association entered into to secure those proprietary interests — operationalizes the social-contract framing through an anthropological commitment the record cannot accommodate. The cooperative-cognitive-primate evidence establishes that the human is in the first instance a cooperative-cognitive being whose individual capacities are constituted through joint intentionality, shared commitment, and cooperative breeding rather than held as prior properties of an abstract self. Tomasello's shared-intentionality framework, developed across the *Natural History* sequence, identifies the species-defining cognitive innovation distinguishing human social cognition from that of other great apes: the capacity to form joint goals, to coordinate around joint commitments, to engage in genuinely collaborative activity structured by mutual awareness of the shared project. Joseph Henrich's *The Secret of Our Success* (2015) and *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020) extend the point through cultural learning as the species-defining capacity, through which human cognition is constituted as relational rather than as individually proprietary.

The Indigenous and Eastern primary articulators carry the cooperative-cognitive substrate at primary-articulator standing. Robin Wall Kimmerer's (Citizen Potawatomi) *Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants* (2013) articulates the honorable harvest as a relational ordering of human and more-than-human relations. Yunkaporta's *Sand Talk* articulates relational cognition as a mode of constitutional reasoning. Mogobe Ramose's *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (1999) articulates personhood-through-relation — "I am because we are" as a foundational ontological commitment that the possessive-individualism premise exactly inverts. The Confucian relational-ontology tradition through Tu Weiming and through Roger Ames and David Hall, the Buddhist dependent-origination tradition through Nagarjuna and Garfield, the Daoist relational ontology of the *Daodejing* and *Zhuangzi*, the Andean *ayni* reciprocity through PRATEC and Estermann, and the Pacific Islander *Vā* relational ontology through Wendt and Vaai each stand as a primary articulation of the cooperative-cognitive substrate the possessive-individualism premise cannot accommodate.

What the possessive-individualism premise mistook is possessive individualism as the natural baseline and the polity as an aggregation of pre-social possessive individuals. What the biocultural-governance framing puts in its place is cooperative cognition as the evolutionary-cognitive substrate and the polity as a biocultural formation cultivated through that substrate. The Indigenous and Eastern primary articulators carry the cooperative-cognitive substrate; the Western cooperative-cognitive-primate canon — de Waal, Tomasello, Hrdy, Henrich — is recent and partial recovery of what they have long carried.

### The Social-Contract Framing as Design-Imposition

The social-contract framing operates as a design-imposition not in its normative commitments — life, liberty, the pursuit of happiness, consent of the governed, the right to alter or abolish — but in its analytical mode. The framing derives the polity from a hypothetical pre-political state through abstract individuals' rational consent, and then specifies the institutional architecture appropriate to the derivation. The normative commitments can survive the critique, and they do survive it across the sixth-pass project at primary-articulator standing in Document I.01's foundational-texts engagement. The analytical mode does not survive. The polity is not derived; the polity is cultivated. The conditions under which it is cultivated are not specified in advance through a contract; they emerge through the relational, cooperative-cognitive, and cultural-evolutionary substrate the polity grows through across deep time.

The cultivation framing does not abandon the normative commitments of the social-contract tradition. It re-specifies their analytical status: the commitments are cultivation conditions rather than contractual derivations. The right to alter or abolish becomes the cultivation-condition specification that any governmental form remains revisable in response to its failure to cultivate the conditions it was instituted to maintain — a commitment articulated across the multi-traditional canonical inheritance Document I.01 engages at primary-articulator standing.

## Part Three — Ecology, Carrying Capacity, and the Founders' Ecological Blindness

The founders had no ecology in the contemporary scientific sense. The Linnaean taxonomic project was underway during their lifetimes; ecology as a discipline — Alexander von Humboldt's *Cosmos* (1845–1862), Frederic Clements and Henry Cowles on succession, Arthur Tansley's coining of "ecosystem" in 1935, Eugene Odum's *Fundamentals of Ecology* (1953), and the contemporary complex-adaptive-systems and disturbance-ecology canon engaged in Document V.01 — was not yet developed. The founders engaged the physical-environmental and ecological substrate through agricultural-practical knowledge, eighteenth-century natural-philosophical work such as Buffon's *Histoire Naturelle* (1749–1788) and Linnaeus's *Systema Naturae* (1735 onward), and the commercial-and-agricultural-improvement tradition of their period. The critique here is not that the founders should have anticipated twentieth- and twenty-first-century ecological science; it is that the absence of ecological substrate from their political theory left the implementation structurally unprepared to register the carrying-capacity, ecological-feedback, and disturbance-regime conditions the polity actually operates within.

### Carrying Capacity and Republican Virtue

The Jeffersonian agrarian-republican vision — articulated through the *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1785), the ward-republic correspondence with John Taylor of Caroline (1816), Joseph Cabell (1816), and Samuel Kercheval (1816), and the wider agrarian-republican tradition through Drew McCoy's *The Elusive Republic: Political Economy in Jeffersonian America* (1980), Lance Banning's *The Jeffersonian Persuasion* (1978), and Joyce Appleby's *Capitalism and a New Social Order* (1984) — engaged the relationship between the polity's agricultural-ecological substrate and its republican character. The vision held that republican virtue depended on a broad distribution of landed independence, and that the polity's political-economic conditions therefore required ecological substrate adequate to sustain widespread freeholding. The vision was not naive about local ecological conditions; the agrarian-republican literature engaged soil quality, agricultural practice, and the relationship between agriculture and the polity's political-economic structure.

Where the agrarian-republican vision fails under modern ecological evidence is not in its engagement with local agricultural conditions but in its inability to scale to the planetary-boundary and biogeochemical-cycle conditions the Anthropocene scholarship has since established — Will Steffen and colleagues' planetary-boundaries framework from the Stockholm Resilience Centre publications of 2009 forward, Johan Rockström and colleagues' "A Safe Operating Space for Humanity" (*Nature*, 2009), and the wider Earth-system-science literature. The agrarian-republican vision could engage county- and state-scale ecological substrate; it could not engage biosphere- and ecozone-scale ecological substrate, because that substrate was not yet known and would not be charted until the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

### The Commons Problem and the Property-as-Natural-Right Premise

The founders' engagement with property was Lockean: property as a natural right derivable from the mixing of labor with previously unowned natural substrate, with the polity instituted to secure the property thereby constituted. The labor-mixing argument in the *Second Treatise* chapter 5 articulated the foundational claim, and the founders operationalized it as a political-economic commitment. The critique here is not that the labor-mixing argument is internally inconsistent but that the property-as-natural-right premise mistakes one historically specific property regime for the natural baseline of human economic life.

The evidence against the natural-right framing of property runs across several literatures. Marcel Mauss's *The Gift: The Form and Reason for Exchange in Archaic Societies* (1925) established the foundational ethnographic engagement with gift-economy regimes as alternatives to commodity-property regimes. David Graeber's *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) and *Toward an Anthropological Theory of Value* (2001) provide the contemporary engagement with the historical-anthropological record of property and exchange. Annette Weiner's *Inalienable Possessions: The Paradox of Keeping-While-Giving* (1992) articulates the keeping-while-giving paradox in gift economies. Marshall Sahlins's *Stone Age Economics* (1972) engages the property regimes operative in foraging and horticultural societies.

The commons-management archive — Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* (1990), the Vincent and Elinor Ostrom polycentric-governance scholarship, Bonnie McCay and James Acheson's *The Question of the Commons* (1987), and Charlotte Hess and Elinor Ostrom's *Understanding Knowledge as a Commons* (2007) — establishes that commons-management institutions sustaining shared resources without private appropriation or central administration are an operational reality across cultures, time periods, and resource types. The "tragedy of the commons" articulated through Garrett Hardin's *Science* paper of 1968 was wrong as an anthropological generalization, though correct as a description of one particular failure mode under specific institutional conditions; Ostrom established the operational reality of commons-management institutions and specified the design principles under which they sustain themselves across generations.

The Indigenous gift-economy and commons traditions stand at primary-articulator standing on the alternative-property substrate. Kimmerer's honorable harvest articulates a relational property regime in which takers are bound by reciprocal obligations to the more-than-human world from which the take is taken; the Andean *ayni* reciprocity tradition through PRATEC and Estermann's *Filosofía Andina* (1998) articulates reciprocity as a foundational economic-ethical category; Ramose articulates the Ubuntu specification of mutual economic obligation; the Pacific Islander *Vā* relational ontology and the Hawaiian *ahupuaʻa* land-management tradition through Davianna Pōmaikaʻi McGregor's (Native Hawaiian) *Nā Kuaʻāina: Living Hawaiian Culture* (2007) articulate place-bound regimes of reciprocal obligation. The wider Indigenous land-relational traditions are engaged in Document I.01 at primary-articulator standing.

The Islamic *waqf* tradition — the perpetual charitable trust under which property is endowed for charitable or public purposes in perpetuity with the principal remaining inviolate — and the Islamic *zakat* obligation of wealth redistribution, engaged in Document I.01 at primary-articulator standing through Wael Hallaq's *The Impossible State* (2013) and Murat Çizakça's *A History of Philanthropic Foundations* (2000), articulate an anti-concentration economic-constitutional commitment the property-as-natural-right premise cannot accommodate. The property regime operating under *waqf* commitments is distinct in kind from the commodity-property regime the Lockean framing operationalizes.

### Future Generations and the Founders' Temporal Blindness

The founders engaged intergenerational continuity at the level of the polity's institutional persistence; how the Constitution might be designed to operate across generations is engaged across the Federalist Papers and the wider founding-period record. What is absent is engagement with intergenerational ecological obligation. The Haudenosaunee seventh-generation criterion — engaged at primary-articulator standing in Document I.01 and operationalized in Document XII.04 (Part XII — Intergenerational Rectification as Cultivation Work) — articulates the intergenerational ecological commitment the founders did not develop. The Andean *Pachakuti* cyclical temporal architecture, the Buddhist *anicca* tradition on impermanence, the Daoist temporal-flow tradition, the Hopi temporal cosmology through Ekkehart Malotki's *Hopi Time* (1983), and the Aboriginal Dreaming each stand at primary-articulator standing on the intergenerational temporal substrate the polity actually operates within.

The contemporary Western engagement with intergenerational ethics — Edith Brown Weiss's *In Fairness to Future Generations* (1989), Stephen Gardiner's *A Perfect Moral Storm: The Ethical Tragedy of Climate Change* (2011), the Long Now Foundation corpus including Stewart Brand's *The Clock of the Long Now* (1999), and Rob Nixon's *Slow Violence and the Environmentalism of the Poor* (2011) — is recent and partial recovery of what the Indigenous and Eastern primary articulators have carried as constitutional commitments far longer.

### Property-as-Natural-Right as Design-Imposition

What the property-as-natural-right premise mistook is the polity as the ground of fee-simple absolute property under Lockean labor-mixing premises, and property as a natural right derivable from pre-political theory. What the biocultural-governance framing puts in its place is property as one cultural-historical specification of cultivation-condition material relations among persons and between persons and the more-than-human world, and the polity as a biocultural formation cultivated through property-and-commons regimes appropriate to nested scales. Documents VI.04 (Part VI — Cradle-to-Cradle and circular material flows), XII.02 (Part XII — Currency Architecture), and XII.11 (Part XII — Provisioning Decision Rights) operationalize the cultivation-condition property regime downstream.

## Part Four — Evolutionary Biology, Political Institutions, and the Limits of the Founders' Anthropology

The founders engaged human nature under the pre-Darwinian anthropology of the late eighteenth century. Evolutionary biology — Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) and *The Descent of Man* (1871), and the twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship that followed — was not available to them. The critique here is not that the founders should have anticipated Darwinian and post-Darwinian biology; it is that the eighteenth-century anthropology they worked within mistook the evolutionary substrate for a stable natural property of an abstract human nature, with consequences for the institutional architecture they specified.

The cultural-evolutionary scaling-mechanisms and multilevel-selection material that earlier passes engaged in this Part relocates to Document IV.01 (Part IV — Deep Substrate), per the holding file at `project/relocations/Document_4_relocations.md`. Document IV.01 carries the Sober-and-Wilson, Boyd-and-Richerson, Henrich, and Tomasello cultural-evolutionary and cooperative-cognitive substrate as cultivation-substrate specification at the level of deep substrate; what remains here is the founding-era critique focused on the design-imposition framings. The relocation follows the move-don't-regenerate principle in the strict sense: the relocated analysis is preserved in its move, not duplicated and not rewritten, and Document IV.01's drafting session engages it from the receiving end.

### Evolution Did Not Optimize for Democracy

The evolutionary record establishes that human cognition, motivation, and social organization were shaped by selective pressures across the Pleistocene that operated in small-band conditions different from the large-scale-polity conditions any modern constitutional architecture must engage. The evolved psychology of political behavior — engaged here with critical attention to the methodological caveats the contemporary scholarship has raised — operates through cognitive-motivational substrates not selected for the conditions of large-scale democratic governance. The implication is not that democracy is impossible; the empirical record establishes that democratic institutions operate across cultures, time periods, and scales. The implication is that the cognitive substrate's adequacy to large-scale democratic governance is a cultivation outcome rather than a natural property.

The dominance-hierarchy substrate that the primatological and evolutionary-anthropological record establishes — including Boehm's engagement with reverse-dominance hierarchies in *Hierarchy in the Forest*, engaged in Part Two above — bears on the persistence of aristocracy, the vulnerability of democratic institutions to authoritarian capture, and the wider political-psychological substrate the polity operates through. The founders engaged the persistence of aristocracy as a political-historical reality; the Federalist Papers reckon with faction, with the risks of executive concentration, and with the conditions under which republican government might be sustained against the pressures toward consolidation. But they operated under the assumption that institutional design could neutralize the substrate. The evolutionary and historical record establishes that the substrate is not neutralized by institutional design alone; it is engaged through cultivation conditions that institutional design specifies and that the ongoing operation of the polity sustains across generations.

### Kin Selection, Nepotism, and the Civil-Service Architecture

W. D. Hamilton's foundational papers, "The Genetical Evolution of Social Behaviour, I and II" (*Journal of Theoretical Biology*, 1964), and the kin-selection literature that followed — engaged with critical attention to the multilevel-selection alternative through Martin Nowak, Corina Tarnita, and E. O. Wilson's "The Evolution of Eusociality" (*Nature*, 2010), the fuller treatment of which relocates to Document IV.01 — establish that the human cognitive-motivational substrate carries inclusive-fitness commitments operating as tendencies toward nepotism, patronage, and kin-based political coalition. The founders engaged nepotism and corruption as constitutional problems; the substrate through which those problems operate was unavailable to them. The civil-service architecture developed through the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883 and comparable reforms across constitutional democracies operates as a cultivation-condition specification through which the kin-selection substrate is engaged at institutional scale. The contemporary scholarship on regulatory capture, on bureaucratic-political coalition, and on the difficulty of sustaining merit-based administration against the substrate's tendencies provides the diagnostic engagement.

### Behavioral Ecology and the Limits of the Jeffersonian Vision

The behavioral-ecology engagement with the Jeffersonian agrarian-republican vision's claims about the relationship between agricultural subsistence and republican character relocates substantively to Document IV.01, which carries the comparative-anthropology-of-governance archive across hunter-gatherer, horticultural, agrarian, pastoral, industrial, post-industrial, and contemporary digital-network political formations. The founding-era critique that remains here is this: the agrarian-republican vision mistook one historically specific subsistence-political formation for the baseline of republican governance. The evidence establishes that republican character is sustained across multiple subsistence-political formations under specifiable cultivation conditions, and that the agrarian-mode-as-republican-baseline framing was a limitation of the founders' analytical resources rather than a finding about the polity.

## Part Five — Human Behavioral Ecology and the Founders' Engagement with Political Sustainability

The founders engaged the conditions under which republican government might be sustained. The Federalist Papers reckon with faction, with the risks of executive concentration, and with the conditions under which deliberative institutions might operate against the pressures toward consolidation. The contemporary human-behavioral-ecology engagement — taken up here at the level of political sustainability rather than at the level of cultural-evolutionary scaling mechanisms, which relocate to Document IV.01 — bears on three areas the founders engaged with limited analytical resources: status hierarchies and democratic stability; tribalism and out-group hostility; behavioral genetics and political attitudes.

### Status, Inequality, and Democratic Stability

The relationship between economic inequality and democratic stability is engaged at depth in Document II.07 (Part II — the political-economic-disturbance regime) through Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, and the wider inequality-and-democracy scholarship. The founders engaged the relationship between concentrated wealth and republican government — through the Federalist Papers' attention to faction, through the Anti-Federalist scale-and-locality critique engaged in Document I.03, and through the founding-era anti-corruption tradition engaged in Document II.03 through Zephyr Teachout's *Corruption in America: From Benjamin Franklin's Snuff Box to Citizens United* (2014). The founders recognized concentrated wealth as a constitutional problem.

The critique under the sixth-pass framing is not that the founders failed to engage status and inequality as constitutional problems; it is that the bounded-rational-actor and possessive-individualism framings under which they engaged the problem cannot register the substrate the problem operates through. That substrate is the cooperative-cognitive substrate engaged in Part Two and the cultural-political-economic substrate engaged in Documents II.02, II.03, and II.07. The contemporary status-and-inequality scholarship establishes that the substrate produces systematic constitutional vulnerabilities under conditions of concentrated wealth that institutional design alone cannot neutralize.

### Tribalism, Out-Group Hostility, and the Costs of Faction Theory

The founders engaged faction theory at depth through *Federalist 10*. The claim that "the latent causes of faction are thus sown in the nature of man" anticipates by more than two centuries the social-identity-theory engagement through Henri Tajfel and John Turner's "An Integrative Theory of Intergroup Conflict" (1979), through Marilynn Brewer's optimal-distinctiveness theory, and through the contemporary engagement with intergroup conflict and cooperation. Where the faction theory fails as a design-imposition framing is not in its recognition of the substrate — the founders recognized the substrate clearly — but in its specification of institutional architecture as sufficient to neutralize it. The expanded-republic argument of *Federalist 10*, that an extended sphere of operation would render faction less likely by multiplying interests and rendering coalition more difficult, operates under the assumption that institutional scale alone neutralizes the substrate. The contemporary evidence establishes that scale alone is insufficient: cultivation conditions for sustaining cross-group cooperation against the substrate's tendencies are required, and those conditions are not specified through institutional design alone.

The costs of out-group hostility are engaged at depth in Document II.02 (Part II — the cultural-disturbance diagnostic) through the racism axis and the wider cultural-disturbance regime analysis. Out-group hostility operates as a disturbance regime the founders' institutional architecture cannot neutralize without sustained cultivation work. The cultivation framing's commitment to the multi-traditional canonical inheritance at primary-articulator standing is the analytical response to the out-group-hostility substrate: the polity adequate to its multi-traditional inheritance is the polity cultivated through engagement with the substrate rather than through institutional design that presupposes the substrate already neutralized.

### Behavioral Genetics and Political Attitudes, Engaged with Critical Caveats

The engagement with behavioral genetics and political attitudes — through the contemporary literature on the heritability of political orientations, including John Hibbing and colleagues' *Predisposed: Liberals, Conservatives, and the Biology of Political Differences* (2014) and the wider political-physiology literature — proceeds here with explicit critical caveats. The behavioral-genetic literature on political attitudes operates within methodological frames that have real limits, and the engagement must attend to them. That political orientations carry heritable components does not entail that they are biologically fixed; the cultural-evolutionary substrate Document IV.01 engages establishes that the relationship between heritable cognitive-motivational substrates and culturally articulated political orientations operates through cultural-evolutionary scaling mechanisms that are themselves cultivation conditions. The founders engaged variation in political orientations across persons and contexts; they did not engage the substrate through which the variation operates. The sixth-pass framing engages that substrate while refusing the reductive move that would specify political orientations as biologically fixed properties.

## Part Six — Synthesis: The Constitution's Biological Blindspots and the Polity-as-Biocultural-Formation Thesis

The four founding-era anthropological assumptions — Lockean possessive individualism, the social-contract framing, the bounded-rational-actor model, the property-as-natural-right premise — operate together as the integrated anthropological substrate of the founding-era design-imposition framing of the polity. Each is a particular analytical mode through which the founders engaged the polity's foundations; together they constitute a coherent, if flawed, anthropological-political theory. The analytical move developed here is to read the four assumptions as one integrated design-imposition framing and to articulate the polity-as-biocultural-formation thesis as the answer.

### The Four Design-Imposition Framings Read Together

The Lockean possessive-individualism premise mistook the human, in the first instance, as a proprietor of his own person and capacities. The cooperative-cognitive-primate evidence and the Indigenous and Eastern primary-articulator engagement with relational personhood establish that the human is, in the first instance, a cooperative-cognitive being constituted through joint intentionality, cultural learning, and relational substrate. The correction is foundational: the human is not, in the first instance, an individual.

The social-contract framing mistook the polity as an analytical-conceptual derivation from a hypothetical state of nature inhabited by abstract individuals. The evolutionary-anthropological and Indigenous-foundational record establishes that the polity is empirically and historically formed through specifiable conditions across deep time, and that no hypothetical state of nature is required for the analysis. The correction is foundational: the polity is not derived from a contract.

The bounded-rational-actor model mistook the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale as uniform across persons, contexts, and conditions. The cognitive-bias and cognitive-plurality literatures establish that the substrate is plural, contextual, and varying. The correction is foundational: the deliberative subject is not abstract.

The property-as-natural-right premise mistook one historically specific commodity-property regime for the natural baseline of human economic life. The gift-economy and commons-management archives, the Indigenous and Eastern primary-articulator engagement with alternative property regimes, and the Islamic *waqf*-and-*zakat* tradition establish that the operational reality of human property and economy is plural, with commodity-property as one historically specific regime among many. The correction is foundational: property is not naturally constituted prior to its cultural-historical articulation.

The four assumptions together constitute the integrated anthropological substrate of the design-imposition framing the founders operated under. The implementation they produced operationalized that substrate: the polity as an aggregation of pre-social possessive individuals contracting under specifiable terms, with rational-deliberative capacities and natural-right property in their own labor and its products. The failure of the implementation under modern evidence is not the failure of any one assumption; it is the failure of the integrated design-imposition framing the four assumptions together specify.

### The Polity-as-Biocultural-Formation Thesis

The analytical answer the sixth-pass framing develops is the polity-as-biocultural-formation thesis: the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a biocultural formation, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled. The biological substrate is cooperative-cognitive-primate evolutionary inheritance shaped by deep-time historical specifications the comparative-anthropology archive records; the cultural substrate is the multi-traditional canonical inheritance Document I.01 engages at primary-articulator standing; the cultivation conditions are specifiable at each scale of the nested-scales reference frame; the temporal orientation is intergenerational across the disturbance landscape Part II diagnostically specifies.

This document anchors the polity-as-biocultural-formation thesis at the diagnostic level — as the analytical answer to the four design-imposition framings. It does not take up the thesis as a constitutional ontology in its own right. That work belongs to Document III.01 (Part III — Biocultural Governance: Constitutional Ontology), which engages the thesis as a developed scholarly architecture. The deep substrate of the thesis — multilevel selection, the hunter-gatherer cognitive baseline, the cultural-evolutionary scaling mechanisms, the comparative-anthropology-of-governance archive, and the sub-species evolutionary backdrop — is carried by Document IV.01 (Part IV — Deep Substrate). The systems-science substrate is carried by Document V.01 (Part V — CAS, Emergence, Disturbance Ecology, Adaptive Governance). The methodological discipline through which the multi-traditional canon is engaged at primary-articulator standing is carried by Document VII.01 (Part VII — Multi-Eyed Seeing as Constitutional Method).

What the founders' anthropological assumptions cannot register is precisely what the thesis names: the polity as a biocultural formation cultivated across nested scales; cooperative cognition as the evolutionary-cognitive substrate; the polity's empirical-historical formation across deep time through specifiable conditions; cognitive plurality at the organism-and-organ-system scale; the alternative property regimes operative across the multi-traditional canonical inheritance; and the disturbance landscape the polity actually operates within. The implementation's failure to register these conditions is not a design defect to be patched; it is the ontological mistake the design-imposition framing produced.

### What the Science Implies and What It Does Not

The evidence engaged in this document implies that the founders' anthropological assumptions are inadequate to the kind of formation the polity is, and that the implementation operationalized that inadequacy. The evidence does not imply that human nature is fixed by biological substrate alone, that political institutions are reducible to cognitive-evolutionary substrates, or that the normative commitments the foundational inheritance carries — engaged in Document I.01 — are extinguished by the critique of the design-imposition framing. The normative commitments survive the critique; the design-imposition framing does not.

Naming what the evidence does not imply is part of the analytical work, not an aside to it. Modern evolutionary, cognitive, and anthropological evidence does not establish biological determinism, does not establish that political institutions reduce to evolved cognition, and does not establish that the normative commitments of the foundational inheritance are mere products of cultural evolution to be revised on biological grounds. The cultivation framing of the sixth-pass project holds that the normative commitments are foundational and that what requires revision under modern evidence is the design-imposition framing through which the founders operationalized them.

### Part II Diagnostic Foundation for the Disturbance-Regime Architecture

The founding-era critique developed here is the analytical foundation against which the five disturbance-regime diagnostics of Part II operate. Each disturbance regime specifies how the biocultural formation is threatened, captured, or eroded under conditions the founders' design-imposition framings cannot register as relevant.

Document II.02, the cultural-disturbance diagnostic, specifies how the polity's cultural plurality is captured along the caste, oligarchy, media, racism, and embodiment axes in ways the founders' bounded-rational-actor and possessive-individualist framings cannot diagnose. Document II.03, on oligarchy and dark money, specifies the concrete capture architecture at the cultural-political-economic intersection. Document II.04, the biological-disturbance diagnostic, specifies how the biological substrate is threatened through pandemic dynamics, biodiversity loss, food-web collapse, soil-system breakdown, vector-borne disease shift, antibiotic resistance, and agricultural-pest dynamics in ways the founders' static-natural-baseline framing cannot anticipate. Document II.05, the physical-environmental-disturbance diagnostic, specifies how the physical environment is destabilized through climate, hydrological regime shift, fire regime shift, geological events, anthropogenic chemical contamination, and atmospheric composition shift in ways the founders' carrying-capacity-blind framing cannot register. Document II.06, the cognitive-and-informational-disturbance diagnostic, specifies how the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale is captured through disinformation cascades, attention-capture architectures, AI-driven epistemic disruption, cognitive-load shocks, and generational cognitive-substrate shifts in ways the founders' uniform-bounded-rational-actor framing cannot diagnose. Document II.07, the political-economic-disturbance diagnostic, specifies how the political-economic substrate is captured through currency-and-monetary shocks, supply-chain collapse, debt-crisis cascades, technological labor displacement, and capital concentration in ways the founders' property-as-natural-right framing cannot diagnose. Document II.08, the cross-regime interactions diagnostic, specifies the compound-disturbance dynamics across the five regimes that the founders' single-regime analytical engagement cannot register.

The polity-as-biocultural-formation thesis is the analytical substrate against which the disturbance-regime diagnostics operate. The diagnostics specify the empirical landscape that the cultivation conditions — Document III.01, the Part VI provisioning documents, the Part XII structural-commitments documents, Document XIII.01, and the capstone, Document XIV.01 — operationalize as the project's constitutional response. The founding-era critique developed in this document is the analytical foundation of that response: the diagnostic ground against which the constitutional cultivation work of the rest of the arc proceeds.
