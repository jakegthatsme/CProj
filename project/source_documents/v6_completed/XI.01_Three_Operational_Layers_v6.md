## Opening — The Document Under the Sixth-Pass Architecture

This is the Part XI anchor on the three operational layers of the multi-decade project. The project rests on a load-bearing triad: biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation. The Indigenous and Eastern traditions stand as the triad's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery. "Grown, not built" is colloquial summary.

The triad bears on this document at three specific points. First, each operational layer is read as cultivation infrastructure rather than as designed institutional architecture. State-level innovation is read as bioregional cultivation work — the slower-scale, region-specific cultivation that builds the cross-watershed, cross-bioregional, cross-tribal-and-state institutional capacity any eventual reorganization phase will draw upon. International and Indigenous governance is read as multi-scale cultivation vocabulary — the institutional grammar through which planetary-scale and Indigenous-scale cultivation work is articulated in forms that the inherited American constitutional architecture cannot generate from within itself. Cognitive-aware political mobilization is read as cultivation of the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale — the slowest-and-fastest cultivation work, operating at the level of the human reasoner whose cognitive architecture is the substrate through which political force is generated or fails to be. Second, the modern empirical examples of CAS-informed and cultivation-aware governance — the Welsh Future Generations Commissioner Act, the Bolivian and Ecuadorian Rights of Nature constitutional architectures, the Whanganui and Atrato and Magdalena and Yamuna and Ganga legal-personhood architectures, German *Mitbestimmung*, the Norwegian Government Pension Fund Global, the French Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat, the Taiwanese vTaiwan and Pol.is digital-deliberation infrastructure, the Brazilian participatory-budgeting tradition, the Indian and South African transformative-constitutionalism architectures, the Chilean and Tunisian constitutional processes — are integrated as empirical archive across the three layers. ## PART ONE — STATE-LEVEL LEGISLATIVE INNOVATION AS BIOREGIONAL CULTIVATION WORK

The state-level layer of the multi-decade project is its primary venue under contemporary federal conditions, and the framing of that claim matters. State innovation is not the project's substitute for federal transformation; it is the bioregional cultivation infrastructure that makes federal transformation possible when the political conditions for it eventually arrive. The framing matters because the alternative framings are widely available and have specific failure modes. The framing of state innovation as a federalist alternative to federal action — the position associated in different forms with libertarian "exit" theorists, with progressive devolutionists, and with state-level political consultants — accepts the inherited federalism as a settled rather than contested institutional architecture, and treats the absence of federal transformation as a permanent rather than cyclical condition. The framing of state innovation as policy laboratory — Justice Louis Brandeis's 1932 dissent in *New State Ice Co. v. Liebmann*, 285 U.S. 262 (1932), invoked the figure of states as "laboratories" that "may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory; and try novel social and economic experiments without risk to the rest of the country" — captures something real about the experimental capacity of state-level institutions but has been deployed in service of programs whose effect has been to constrain rather than enable substantive innovation. The project's framing differs from both. State innovation is bioregional cultivation infrastructure: the slower-scale, region-specific cultivation that, in the panarchy framework `V.01_CAS_Emergence_Adaptive_Governance_v6` carries, accumulates the structural capacity that eventual reorganization at higher scales draws upon, and that operates through the bioregional logic the bioregionalism canon `VI.05_Regenerative_Built_Environment_v6` specifies — region-specific, watershed-scale, place-based cultivation that aligns institutional architecture with the social-ecological systems it governs.

### 1.1 The bioregional cultivation framing and the federalism question

The bioregionalism canon — Patrick Geddes's *Cities in Evolution* (1915), Lewis Mumford's *The Culture of Cities* (1938) and *The City in History* (1961), Peter Berg and Raymond Dasmann's "Reinhabiting California" (*The Ecologist*, 1977), Kirkpatrick Sale's *Dwellers in the Land: The Bioregional Vision* (1985), Robert L. Thayer Jr.'s *LifePlace: Bioregional Thought and Practice* (2003), Doug Aberley's "Interpreting Bioregionalism" in *Bioregionalism* (1999), and Judith Plant's edited *Home! A Bioregional Reader* (1990) — supplies the analytical apparatus through which state-level innovation can be read as bioregional cultivation work rather than as state-by-state administrative-policy delivery. The bioregional framing operates at the scale at which ecological and cultural relations actually compose place — the watershed, the airshed, the foodshed, the bioregion as Sale and Berg articulate it — rather than at the politically-arbitrary scale of state boundaries that the inherited federalism imposes. The bioregional reading does not abolish state boundaries; it reads them as one institutional layer among several, with the bioregional scale operating across, through, and sometimes against the state-level political-administrative scale. The Pacific Northwest bioregion comprises portions of Washington, Oregon, northern California, southern British Columbia, and the Salish Sea; the Great Lakes bioregion comprises portions of multiple states and Canadian provinces and includes the territories of multiple Indigenous nations; the Colorado River basin comprises portions of seven U.S. states, two Mexican states, and the territories of multiple tribal nations. The state-level innovations the multi-decade project values most are those that operate within and across bioregional scales, building the watershed-level, airshed-level, and foodshed-level cultivation capacity that operates as the institutional grammar of the place rather than only as the political grammar of the state.

The federalism literature has been engaged in pieces across the project's prior documents. Heather Gerken's articulation of progressive federalism, developed across her 2012 essay "A New Progressive Federalism" in *Democracy: A Journal of Ideas* and through her subsequent scholarship including *Federalism All the Way Down*, makes the case that federalism's value for racial, religious, and ideological minorities is not a contingent feature dependent on which group happens to hold national power, but a structural feature of a polity in which different communities can constitute different majorities at different scales. Gerken's argument turns on what she calls the "minority's day in the sun" — the capacity of a numerical minority at the national scale to be a working majority in a state, county, or city, and to govern there in ways that build the political capacity, the demonstration cases, and the institutional infrastructure that national-scale change eventually requires. Jessica Bulman-Pozen and Gerken's "Uncooperative Federalism" (*Yale Law Journal*, 2009) develops a complementary argument: that the states' capacity to refuse cooperation with federal programs, or to implement them in ways that reshape their meaning, is a constitutional resource for democratic contestation rather than a defect to be cured. The critique of progressive federalism is equally important. Erin Ryan's *Federalism and the Tug of War Within* and her subsequent work on the "many faces of federalism" emphasize that federalism is an instrument as readily turned to exclusion as to inclusion, and that the same structural features that enable California's climate policy enable Texas's voter suppression, Florida's attacks on academic freedom and on transgender persons, and the systematic preemption of municipal innovation that has accelerated across the contemporary period. Bridget Fahey's "Federalism by Contract" (*Yale Law Journal*, 2020) argues that the contemporary practice of federalism is not the polycentric structure of Madisonian theory but a series of ad hoc bargains between federal agencies and individual states that produces governance whose democratic legitimacy is less defensible than either federalist or nationalist accounts would suggest. The honest answer to whether federalism enables or constrains the polycentric governance Elinor Ostrom's framework prescribes is that it does both, and that which effect predominates depends on the substantive policy domain, the partisan configuration, and the institutional-design choices made within the federalist structure.

The project's framework does not require defending federalism in the abstract; it requires assessing the design features of state-level institutions for whether they produce the long-cycle cultivation capacity the panarchy framework identifies as the work of the contemporary phase. Polycentricity in Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) and her 2009 *Science* paper "A General Framework for Analyzing Sustainability of Social-Ecological Systems" refers to multiple, overlapping centers of governance authority operating at different scales, communicating with one another, and adapting their boundaries and rules to the resource systems they govern. American federalism is one possible institutional realization of polycentricity, but a peculiar one: its scales are fixed by constitutional text rather than adapted to resource systems, its communication channels among levels are mediated by partisan and judicial institutions whose dynamics are not optimized for resource governance, and its authority allocations are products of historical contingency rather than design. The project's framework specifies that state-level innovation is most generative when it explicitly builds polycentric structures that align governance scales with the social-ecological systems being governed — watershed councils, regional climate compacts, multi-state grid governance, tribal-state-federal co-management arrangements — even when these structures fit awkwardly into the formal federalism categories. Carl Folke's work on social-ecological systems at regional scales, particularly his synthesis essays for the Stockholm Resilience Centre, specifies what kinds of multi-scale institutional arrangements have actually performed under the cross-scale stresses the contemporary period imposes.

### 1.2 The empirical record of state-level innovation read as bioregional cultivation

Within the domains the project has identified as most consequential, state-level innovation in the contemporary period has produced a record substantial enough to assess. The record is not uniform. Some innovation has built durable cultivation capacity that survives partisan transitions and propagates across jurisdictions. Some has depended on specific political alignments and dissipated when those alignments changed. The project's framework distinguishes these categories, because only the first contributes the long-cycle cultivation work the strategic project depends on.

Climate policy is the domain where the empirical record is most extensive and the cultivation learning curve most legible. California's cap-and-trade program, established under Assembly Bill 32 (2006) and operationalized through the Air Resources Board's regulations in 2012, has run for more than a decade and produced regulatory experience, technical staff capacity, and design lessons the project's framework can assess. The program's record is mixed in ways that are themselves informative. The cap has bound, allowance prices have generally tracked policy intent, and the program has linked with Quebec's system to create a multi-jurisdictional carbon market. The program has also encountered design pathologies the cap-and-trade literature anticipated — over-allocation of allowances in early phases, the offsets controversy involving forest projects whose additionality is disputed, the environmental-justice critique that aggregate emission reductions can coexist with local pollution concentrations in disadvantaged communities, the political vulnerability of the program to industry lobbying for allowance set-asides. California's Senate Bill 100 (2018), which established the 100-percent zero-carbon electricity target by 2045, builds on a different regulatory architecture — the renewable portfolio standard — and operates through utility planning rather than market pricing. The Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative, the multi-state cap-and-trade compact for the power sector that began operations in 2009 with ten Northeastern and Mid-Atlantic states, has produced documented emission reductions in the covered sector, has survived the temporary New Jersey withdrawal under Governor Christie and the subsequent rejoining under Governor Murphy, has expanded to include Virginia (which withdrew under Governor Youngkin and rejoined the policy conversation in altered form), and demonstrates that interstate climate compacts can sustain themselves across partisan transitions when their design includes credible exit costs. Renewable portfolio standards now operate in approximately thirty states with significant variation in stringency, eligible technologies, and compliance mechanisms; the empirical literature including work by Galen Barbose at Lawrence Berkeley and Sanya Carley at Indiana University has produced reasonable consensus that these standards have driven substantial renewable deployment, with effects partially offsetting in adjacent states without standards.

The New York Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act of 2019 deserves particular attention as a more recent and architecturally ambitious instance. The Act establishes binding statutory targets for greenhouse gas emissions (a forty percent reduction below 1990 levels by 2030 and an eighty-five percent reduction by 2050), requires the Department of Environmental Conservation to promulgate regulations to achieve those targets, establishes a Climate Action Council to develop a scoping plan with statutory standing, and includes a Climate Justice Working Group with explicit authority over the criteria for identifying disadvantaged communities and a thirty-five-percent investment-benefit floor for those communities. The Act's design includes features the project's framework specifies as important: binding statutory targets that constrain agency discretion, explicit equity provisions that are not advisory, a deliberative council structure that includes both expert and community representation, and feedback mechanisms that require periodic plan updates. The Act's implementation has been contested in ways that test whether the design features hold under political pressure: the cap-and-invest regulation has been delayed, the gas-system transition planning has encountered the predictable utility-industry opposition, and the workforce-transition provisions have lagged behind the emission-reduction provisions. The relevant question for the project's framework is not whether the Act has succeeded on its own terms — that question is premature — but whether the cultivation capacity it has built (the agency staff, the technical models, the deliberative council, the community organizations now operating within the Act's framework) constitutes durable infrastructure that survives political transition and propagates beyond New York. The disadvantaged-communities criteria methodology has been borrowed by other states. The Climate Action Council structure has been studied as a model. The agency capacity has been built in ways that would be difficult for a future hostile administration to dismantle quickly. The political-economy weakness of relying on a single state's continued majority commitment remains real.

Building electrification ordinances in cities including Berkeley (whose 2019 ordinance was struck down by the Ninth Circuit in *California Restaurant Association v. City of Berkeley*, 89 F.4th 1094 (9th Cir. 2023), on preemption grounds), New York City, Seattle, Washington, and Denver have produced a litigation record that is itself instructive about the constraints on state and municipal cultivation work. The Ninth Circuit's Berkeley decision, holding that the city's ordinance was preempted by the federal Energy Policy and Conservation Act, illustrates how federal preemption doctrines can constrain municipal innovation in ways that progressive federalism scholarship has tracked. The institutional response — building-code and air-quality-regulation pathways that achieve similar outcomes through different legal authority — illustrates the adaptive capacity that durable cultivation infrastructure makes possible.

Voting rights and democratic reform constitute a second domain with a substantial state-level record. Automatic voter registration, first enacted in Oregon in 2015 and now operating in approximately twenty-five states and the District of Columbia, has produced a documented increase in registration rates and has begun to produce empirical literature on whether the registration increase translates into voter participation increase (the answer appears to be modestly yes, with effects concentrated in low-information voters who would not have registered through prior processes). Same-day registration, operating in approximately twenty states, has similarly produced empirical evidence of participation effects. Ranked-choice voting has been adopted at the state level in Maine for federal elections in 2016, in Alaska for state and federal elections in 2020, and at the municipal level in approximately fifty jurisdictions including New York City, Minneapolis, San Francisco, and Salt Lake City. The empirical literature on ranked-choice voting's effects — work by Jack Santucci, Lee Drutman, and others — suggests modest effects on candidate diversity and on negative campaigning, with the institutional learning curve still in progress. The Michigan Independent Citizens Redistricting Commission, established by ballot initiative in 2018 and operationalized for the 2020 redistricting cycle, represents one of the most institutionally ambitious instances of structural democratic reform: a commission of randomly selected citizens (with statutory criteria for selection that approximate sortition principles), with binding redistricting authority constitutionally protected from legislative interference, producing maps that have been litigated and largely upheld. The Commission's design includes features the deliberative-democracy literature specifies as important — citizen selection by lot, support staff with technical capacity, transparent deliberation, and binding output. Its performance through one cycle is not yet a verdict, but the cultivation capacity built — staff, procedures, public legitimacy, demonstration that citizen redistricting can produce technically credible maps — constitutes durable infrastructure of the kind the project's framework values. The Oregon Citizens' Initiative Review (operating since 2010 with statutory authorization since 2011) and the various climate assemblies at city and state levels constitute a smaller but expanding set of institutional experiments in deliberative democracy that Part Three engages in more detail.

Labor policy at the state level has produced a record the project's framework engages carefully because the political economy of labor regulation differs from the political economy of climate or democratic-reform policy in ways that affect what state innovations propagate. California's Assembly Bill 5 (2019), which codified the *Dynamex Operations West v. Superior Court* (2018) standard for distinguishing employees from independent contractors, and the subsequent Proposition 22 (2020) that exempted gig-economy companies from AB 5, illustrate both the potential for state-level labor reform and the political-economy vulnerability of such reform when concentrated industry interests can mobilize ballot-initiative resources. The California Private Attorneys General Act (PAGA, 2003) authorizes employees to sue employers for Labor Code violations on behalf of the state and to recover civil penalties, producing enforcement that operates parallel to and partially independent of the state Labor Commissioner's enforcement capacity. State minimum wage laws above the federal floor now operate in approximately thirty states with significant variation in level and indexing; the empirical literature including work by Arindrajit Dube, Michael Reich, and others has produced reasonable consensus that minimum-wage increases at the levels enacted have produced employment effects smaller than the predictions of competitive-market models would suggest. Public-sector bargaining frameworks, varying substantially across states, have proved both creative (the Hawaii and Oregon expansions to public-sector workers) and vulnerable (Wisconsin's Act 10 of 2011 effectively eliminated public-sector bargaining for most workers; Indiana, Michigan, and other states have enacted similar measures). The Washington State long-term care insurance program, the Massachusetts Fair Share Amendment (2022) that established a state surtax on incomes above one million dollars dedicated to education and transportation, and the various state-level paid-family-leave programs constitute additional instances of state-level labor and social-insurance cultivation.

Rights of Nature and ecological constitutionalism at the sub-federal level have produced a more limited but instructive record, whose strategic significance is best understood in conjunction with the international Rights of Nature jurisprudence Part Two engages in detail. The Lake Erie Bill of Rights, adopted by Toledo voters in 2019 and struck down by federal district court in *Drewes Farms Partnership v. City of Toledo* (2020) on vagueness and preemption grounds, illustrates both the political possibility of municipal Rights of Nature ordinances and the legal vulnerability of such ordinances under existing federal jurisprudence. The White Earth Band of Ojibwe's adoption of the Rights of Manoomin (wild rice) in 2018, and the subsequent litigation including the tribal-court proceedings against the Minnesota Department of Natural Resources, represents a different institutional pathway — tribal sovereignty-based Rights of Nature recognition that operates within a different legal framework than municipal ordinances and may be more durable for that reason. The Yurok Tribe's Rights of the Klamath River, adopted in 2019, represents a similar institutional development. The Community Environmental Legal Defense Fund corpus of municipal Rights of Nature ordinances — adopted in Pittsburgh (2010), Santa Monica (2013), and dozens of other municipalities — constitutes a body of cultivation work that has not yet been substantially tested in litigation but has built political constituencies and institutional vocabulary. Tamaqua Borough's 2006 ordinance, often cited as the first municipal Rights of Nature provision in the United States, addressed the specific concern of sewage sludge disposal and demonstrates that the ordinance form can be deployed against specific extractive threats with local political viability.

Right-to-food constitutional amendments have emerged as a second category of substantive state-level constitutional cultivation. Maine's right-to-food amendment, adopted by referendum in 2021 as Article I, Section 25 of the Maine Constitution, establishes the right to grow, raise, harvest, produce, and consume food. The amendment operates as substantive constitutional commitment whose subsequent jurisprudential development will determine its operational content, and it represents the first explicit right-to-food provision in an American state constitution. Vermont's pending right-to-food work and the broader food-sovereignty movement that the Community Environmental Legal Defense Fund and the Northeast Organic Farming Association have catalyzed constitute the broader cultivation context. The Hawaii constitutional amendments on agricultural cultivation, water rights, and Indigenous Hawaiian practices, the New York State Green Amendment of 2021 (Article I, Section 19) establishing the right to clean air and water and a healthful environment, the Pennsylvania Environmental Rights Amendment of 1971 (Article I, Section 27) whose contemporary jurisprudence the *Pennsylvania Environmental Defense Foundation v. Commonwealth*, 161 A.3d 911 (Pa. 2017), decision has reactivated, and the Montana constitutional environmental rights provision activated in *Held v. State of Montana* (2023) constitute the broader category of state-constitutional environmental-rights cultivation. The state-constitutional layer represents a different cultivation modality than state-statutory innovation — constitutionally protected, less easily reversed by legislative majority, available to the long jurisprudential development that constitutional rights typically receive.

Indigenous co-governance arrangements constitute the final and in many ways most institutionally substantive category of state-level innovation. The Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition — comprising the Hopi Tribe, the Navajo Nation, the Ute Mountain Ute Tribe, the Ute Indian Tribe, and the Pueblo of Zuni — and the management framework established under President Obama's 2016 designation, partially rescinded under President Trump in 2017, restored under President Biden in 2021, and contested again under the current administration, illustrates both the potential for substantive co-governance between tribal and federal authorities and the political vulnerability of administrative co-governance arrangements that lack statutory or constitutional grounding. The Klamath Basin restoration, including the dam removal completed in 2024 — the largest dam removal project in American history — demonstrates how decades-long coordination among tribal nations (the Klamath Tribes, the Yurok Tribe, the Karuk Tribe, the Hoopa Valley Tribe), federal agencies, state governments, irrigators, and environmental organizations can produce substantive ecological restoration even under conditions where the political coalition is fragile and the legal architecture is contested. The Bad River Band of Lake Superior Chippewa's defeat of the Enbridge Line 5 reroute through tribal sovereignty assertion, treaty-rights litigation, and Wisconsin state coordination demonstrates the strategic possibilities of tribal-state coordination on infrastructure questions that operate at scales the federalism literature has not adequately theorized. Tribal water settlements — the Navajo-Gallup Water Supply Project, the Gila River Indian Community settlement, the Yakama Nation settlement, among many others — constitute a body of institutional-design experience in tribal-federal-state co-governance of water resources that the project's framework engages as some of the most substantively polycentric arrangements that operate in contemporary American governance.

### 1.3 What the cultivation framework specifies for state-level institutional design

The cultivation framework that `V.01_CAS_Emergence_Adaptive_Governance_v6` carries (panarchy, resilience, adaptive management, polycentricity) specifies a distinction the immediate-policy assessment does not capture: the distinction between innovation that builds durable cultivation capacity and innovation that depends on specific political alignments to operate at all. The distinction is not between formal and informal institutions, nor between regulatory and legislative innovation, nor between executive and legislative authority. It is between institutional designs that include the feedback mechanisms, technical-capacity foundations, and adaptive-rule-revision procedures adaptive management identifies as constitutive of resilient governance, and institutional designs that operate as static rule-sets vulnerable to political reversal.

The California Air Resources Board exemplifies the first category. Its statutory authority predates the contemporary climate policies, its technical staff capacity has been built over decades and is not easily reconstituted by political appointees, its rulemaking procedures include feedback mechanisms that incorporate compliance data and emerging science, and its regulatory output has propagated through Section 177 of the Clean Air Act to other states that have adopted California's vehicle standards. A future hostile California administration could rewrite the Board's rules, but the cultivation capacity — the staff, the procedures, the data systems, the regulated-community expertise that has formed around the Board's standards — would persist as latent infrastructure that could be reactivated. The New York Department of Environmental Conservation, the Massachusetts Department of Energy Resources, the Washington Department of Ecology, and a small set of other state agencies operate at similar cultivation levels. The Michigan Independent Citizens Redistricting Commission, although it has operated for only one cycle, has built procedural infrastructure with constitutional grounding that would be substantially harder to dismantle than legislative redistricting authority.

The first category has the property the panarchy framework identifies as essential to lower-scale cultivation: the cultivation capacity persists across cycles of political release and reorganization, accumulating over time and becoming available as resources for higher-scale reorganization when conditions permit. The second category does not have this property. A renewable portfolio standard enacted by simple legislative majority, without an associated regulatory agency with built capacity, can be repealed by simple legislative majority. A climate target without statutory standing, an executive order without legislative ratification, an administrative co-governance arrangement without congressional or constitutional grounding — all of these can be reversed in a political cycle. They contribute to immediate policy yield, but they do not contribute to long-cycle cultivation work.

The strategic implication is that the project's framework prioritizes a specific subset of state-level innovation: the construction of regulatory agencies with technical staff capacity that exceeds political-appointee turnover; the establishment of standing deliberative bodies, particularly those grounded in sortition or constitutional protection, with adaptive procedures and feedback mechanisms; the negotiation of legally protected co-management structures, particularly those grounded in tribal sovereignty or interstate compact, that operate across political cycles; and the institutionalization of decision-quality procedures, particularly adaptive-management-derived rule-revision protocols, that can absorb new information and revise rules without requiring new legislation. J. B. Ruhl and Robin Kundis Craig's "Designing Administrative Law for Adaptive Management" (*Vanderbilt Law Review*, 2014) has specified what the legal-architectural requirements for adaptive management actually are; their analysis suggests that most state-level adaptive-management efforts have implemented partial versions that lack the full architectural support adaptive management requires, and that the project's framework should distinguish state innovations including the architectural elements (standing rule-revision authority, technical capacity, structured uncertainty-management procedures, transparency requirements) from those that adopt the adaptive-management vocabulary without the underlying architecture.

The panarchy framework adds a further consideration: the propagation question. Holling's panarchy specifies that lower-scale innovation propagates to higher-scale reorganization through specific causal pathways — accumulation of demonstrated alternatives, training of personnel, formation of constituencies that can mobilize at higher scales, generation of institutional vocabulary that higher-scale actors can adopt. Some state-level innovations propagate well — California's vehicle emission standards through Section 177, renewable portfolio standards through interstate emulation, the Affordable Care Act's draw on Massachusetts's Romneycare. Some do not — Wisconsin's Act 10 has propagated but in directions hostile to the project's framework, while progressive labor reforms have generally failed to propagate beyond their originating states. The propagation patterns are not random. They depend on the institutional features of the originating innovation (whether its design includes elements that other states can readily borrow), on the existence of professional networks across states (the National Conference of State Legislatures, regional governors' associations, state-attorneys-general networks), on the political coalitions that mobilize for emulation, and on the federal regulatory environment that may permit, encourage, or obstruct propagation.

### 1.4 The structural pathologies of state-level innovation

The project's framework cannot honestly assess state-level innovation as cultivation infrastructure without engaging the structural pathologies that the same federalist architecture enabling progressive innovation also enables. The framing of state innovation as project-relevant cultivation work does not require that all state innovation be valorized; it requires honest assessment of which state innovations contribute to the project's framework and which obstruct it.

The federalism that enables California's climate policy enables Texas's voter suppression and its abortion bounty regime, Florida's "Don't Say Gay" legislation and its attacks on academic freedom and on transgender persons, the Tennessee legislature's expulsion of duly elected legislators and its preemption of Memphis and Nashville's local authority, the proliferation of book bans and curriculum restrictions across Republican-controlled states, and the systematic state-level legislation directed at restricting the substantive citizenship of LGBTQ+ persons, immigrants, racial minorities, and women. These are not aberrations within a generally progressive federalism; they are operations of the same institutional structure that enables the progressive innovations the project's framework values. A federalism evaluation honest enough to be useful must hold both sides of this ledger together.

State preemption laws — state legislation that constrains or prohibits municipal authority on specific policy questions — have accelerated dramatically across the contemporary period. The Local Solutions Support Center has documented the proliferation of preemption laws across multiple policy domains: minimum wage preemption, sick-leave preemption, anti-discrimination preemption, environmental-regulation preemption, paid-leave preemption, plastic-bag-regulation preemption, sanctuary-city preemption, gun-regulation preemption, broadband-municipalization preemption. The asymmetry is structural: state legislatures, particularly under Republican control in states whose political geography concentrates Democratic voters in urban areas, have systematically preempted municipal innovation when that innovation has run ahead of state-level political consensus. The Supreme Court's expansion of federal preemption doctrine, including its preemption of California's vehicle emission rule under specific provisions of the Clean Air Act in *Engine Manufacturers Association v. South Coast Air Quality Management District*, 541 U.S. 246 (2004), and the Berkeley building-electrification preemption in 2023, has compounded the constraint on innovation at sub-state scales. Richard Schragger's *City Power: Urban Governance in a Global Era* (2016) has developed the theoretical and historical case that the city is a constitutional actor whose authority has been systematically constrained by state preemption in ways that the federalism literature has under-engaged. Schragger's analysis suggests that the contemporary state-level innovation conversation cannot be conducted without explicit attention to the asymmetric preemption pattern: progressive states permit substantial municipal innovation, while conservative states preempt it, and the aggregate effect is to constrain municipal innovation more in jurisdictions where its value to historically marginalized communities would be greatest.

The political-geography literature, including Jonathan Rodden's *Why Cities Lose: The Deep Roots of the Urban-Rural Political Divide* (2019), Lily Geismer's work on the suburban-progressive coalition, and the broader literature on the geographic concentration of partisan preferences, specifies how the federalism structure interacts with electoral geography to produce asymmetric outcomes. The geographic concentration of Democratic voters in urban areas, combined with single-member-district legislative representation, produces durable Republican majorities in state legislatures even where state-level partisan preference is roughly balanced. The result is that the federalism structure functions less as a polycentric system of multiple majorities and more as a specific asymmetric mechanism that empowers rural and exurban majorities to constrain urban and suburban majorities. The project's framework does not require resolving this geographic asymmetry — that is a structural feature of the inherited constitutional architecture any reorganization phase will need to address — but it does require honest reckoning with how the asymmetry constrains the strategic possibility of state-level cultivation work.

### 1.5 Decision-quality and what cultivation requires of state legislative process design

The decision-science literature engaged across `IV.01_Deep_Substrate_v6` and `VIII.01_Minds_Markets_Sacred_v6` provides specific design prescriptions for state legislative processes that have not yet been internalized in state-level innovation. The decision-quality framework, developed in Ronald Howard and James Matheson's *The Principles and Applications of Decision Analysis* (1984) and subsequent work, specifies six elements of decision quality: appropriate framing, useful alternatives, meaningful information, clear values and trade-offs, logically correct reasoning, and commitment to action. State legislative processes systematically underperform on most of these elements. Framing is dominated by partisan framings inherited from national politics rather than developed for the substantive issue. Alternatives are typically restricted to the two or three options that have been politically pre-vetted, with the substantive option-space inadequately explored. Information is filtered through partisan staff structures and lobbyist channels rather than through structured information-elicitation procedures. Values and trade-offs are usually implicit rather than explicit. Logical reasoning is constrained by the cognitive limitations identified in the Kahneman, Tversky, and Gigerenzer literature. Commitment to action is variable, with much state legislation amounting to symbolic position-taking rather than implementation-grounded policy.

Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner's *How Big Things Get Done* (2023) applies directly to state-level infrastructure and policy planning. Flyvbjerg's empirical record across thousands of large projects shows that planners systematically underestimate costs, overestimate benefits, and underestimate completion times, with the magnitude of the bias substantial enough to invert the cost-benefit analyses that justified the projects. The reference-class forecasting methodology Flyvbjerg has developed — comparing a planned project's parameters to the realized parameters of comparable completed projects rather than relying on bottom-up estimation — has been adopted in some jurisdictions including the United Kingdom's Treasury Green Book guidance and is beginning to be adopted in some American state-level processes, but its adoption is far from systematic. Robert Lempert and the RAND Pardee Center's Robust Decision Making methodology developed in *Shaping the Next One Hundred Years: New Methods for Quantitative, Long-Term Policy Analysis* (2003) has been deployed in California's climate adaptation planning, in Louisiana's coastal master plan, in the Colorado River Basin study, and in various municipal water-management contexts. RDM begins not from probability assessments — which the cognitive-bias literature suggests are systematically unreliable for novel and consequential decisions — but from identifying decision strategies that perform reasonably well across a wide range of plausible futures, and then identifying the conditions under which strategies fail. The state-level innovations that incorporate RDM-style approaches are distinguishable from those that rely on point-estimate cost-benefit analysis, and the project's framework values the former more highly because they produce decisions whose validity does not depend on forecasts the cognitive-bias literature suggests we cannot reliably make.

The Citizens' Initiative Review, established in Oregon in 2010 and now operating in several states with varying institutional support, exemplifies a different decision-quality intervention: the use of randomly selected citizens, supported by trained moderators and balanced expert testimony, to evaluate ballot measures and produce statements that appear in the voters' pamphlet. The empirical literature on the Citizens' Initiative Review, including work by John Gastil and Katherine Knobloch, suggests that the process produces voter information products that are more accurate, more comprehensive, and more usable by voters than the partisan-produced alternatives. The institutional investment required is modest, and the procedural innovation is replicable; the project's framework values the Citizens' Initiative Review as an instance of the kind of decision-quality infrastructure that can be built incrementally at state scales and that propagates well because its design is portable.

### 1.6 The Part One synthesis: which state innovations are highest-leverage as bioregional cultivation

The strategic implication of the analysis is that not all state-level innovation contributes equally to the multi-decade cultivation project the prior document prescribed, and that the project's framework can specify which categories contribute most. The highest-leverage state-level innovations share four properties. They build durable regulatory or technical-agency capacity that exceeds political-appointee turnover. They establish standing deliberative or decision-quality procedures, particularly those grounded in sortition or constitutional protection, with adaptive-revision authority. They produce institutional vocabulary and design templates that propagate to other states. They generate constituencies and personnel networks that build the political capacity for higher-scale reorganization when conditions permit.

By these criteria, the highest-leverage state-level innovations of the contemporary period include the institutional architecture of the New York Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act, particularly its Climate Justice Working Group and disadvantaged-communities methodology; the California Air Resources Board's regulatory infrastructure; the Michigan Independent Citizens Redistricting Commission as a sortition-grounded constitutionally protected deliberative body with binding output; the tribal-state co-management arrangements grounded in treaty rights and tribal sovereignty rather than in administrative discretion, including the Klamath restoration and the Bears Ears coalition under conditions of statutory grounding; the Citizens' Initiative Review and analogous deliberative-democracy procedures with statutory support; the state-constitutional environmental-rights provisions in New York, Pennsylvania, Montana, and others; the right-to-food amendments emerging at the state-constitutional level; and the multi-state regulatory compacts including the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative and the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact.

By the same criteria, lower-leverage innovations despite immediate political appeal include legislation without associated regulatory-capacity building; executive orders without legislative ratification; symbolic resolutions and target-setting without implementation infrastructure; programs whose funding depends on annual appropriation rather than dedicated revenue or trust-fund structures; advisory bodies without binding authority; and pilot programs whose institutional integration into permanent structures is not specified at the outset. These innovations have policy value, and the project's framework does not dismiss them, but they do not produce the long-cycle cultivation work the strategic project depends on.

The state-level layer of the multi-decade project is not separable from the layers Parts Two and Three develop. The highest-leverage state innovations are those whose design facilitates integration with the international and Indigenous-governance vocabularies that Part Two engages and the cognitive-aware mobilization methodologies that Part Three engages. State innovations that build institutional vocabulary translatable across the international Rights of Nature jurisprudence, that institutionalize co-governance arrangements with tribal nations consistent with the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples and Free, Prior and Informed Consent norms, and that generate concrete demonstration cases usable by cognitive-aware mobilization methodology constitute the integration target. The state-level layer is most generative when it operates as the bioregional instantiation of the multi-scale cultivation project the next two parts continue to specify.

## PART TWO — INTERNATIONAL AND INDIGENOUS GOVERNANCE AS MULTI-SCALE CULTIVATION VOCABULARY

The international and Indigenous-governance layer of the multi-decade project supplies institutional vocabulary, normative authority, demonstration cases, and ontological frames that the inherited American constitutional structure cannot generate from within itself. Engagement with these venues is therefore not optional supplementation to a domestically focused project but a necessary component of the multi-decade cultivation work the panarchy framework specifies as the work of the contemporary phase. The American constitutional structure as engaged in Documents `I.01` through `I.04` was designed before the planetary-boundaries scientific framework existed, before the Universal Declaration of Human Rights existed, before the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples existed, before the legal personhood of natural systems was an active jurisprudential question, before Indigenous governance traditions were treated as live institutional resources rather than as historical curiosities or as legitimacy claims to be settled through extinguishment. The vocabulary the inherited structure provides is not adequate to the institutional questions the contemporary moment poses. Part Two specifies where the additional vocabulary comes from, what it consists of, and how engagement with it integrates with the state-level cultivation Part One developed.

### 2.1 The international climate and biodiversity governance frameworks

The international climate and biodiversity governance frameworks operate at a scale and through institutional logics that American constitutional doctrine has historically treated with suspicion. The Senate's repeated refusal to ratify environmental treaties — the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, the Convention on Biological Diversity, the Kyoto Protocol — and the formal withdrawal mechanisms that allow successive administrations to enter and exit international frameworks have produced a pattern in which American participation in international environmental governance is inconsistent across political cycles and in which the United States has often been the principal obstacle to ambition rather than its principal supporter.

The Paris Agreement of 2015 represents the contemporary architecture of international climate governance. Its principal features include the long-term temperature goal of holding global warming "well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels" (Article 2(1)(a)), the nationally determined contributions architecture under which states submit and update emission-reduction commitments (Articles 3 and 4), the global stocktake every five years (Article 14), the financial provisions for climate finance from developed to developing countries (Article 9), and the loss-and-damage provisions whose institutional content has been substantially developed since the Agreement's adoption. The architectural design choice of nationally determined contributions — bottom-up commitment rather than top-down quotas — was made in response to the failure of the Kyoto Protocol's top-down architecture to produce American participation, and it has produced near-universal participation but at the cost of aggregate ambition that falls substantially short of the Agreement's own temperature goals. The 2023 Synthesis Report of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change found that the aggregated nationally determined contributions, if fully implemented, would produce a global temperature increase of approximately 2.5 to 2.9°C, with the gap to 1.5°C-compatible pathways requiring roughly a forty-three percent reduction from 2019 emission levels by 2030 that the current contributions do not achieve.

The Loss and Damage Fund, established by decision at the 27th Conference of the Parties (COP27) in Sharm El-Sheikh in 2022 and operationalized at COP28 in Dubai in 2023 with the World Bank as interim host, represents an institutional development whose significance the project's framework specifies. The Fund addresses the climate-justice claim that vulnerable countries — particularly small island developing states, least developed countries, and African states — have suffered and will suffer climate impacts disproportionate to their historical emissions, and that the developed-country emitters who produced the bulk of cumulative atmospheric carbon owe compensation for the resulting losses and damages. The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, adopted at the 15th Conference of the Parties to the Convention on Biological Diversity in December 2022, establishes targets for global biodiversity governance through 2030, including the protection of thirty percent of terrestrial and marine areas by 2030, targets on the reduction of pollution, on the restoration of degraded ecosystems, on the integration of biodiversity values into economic decision-making, on the recognition of Indigenous and traditional knowledge, and on the financial mobilization for biodiversity conservation. The High Seas Treaty, formally the Agreement on Marine Biodiversity of Areas Beyond National Jurisdiction, adopted in March 2023 after nearly two decades of negotiation, addresses the governance gap in marine areas beyond national jurisdiction that constitute approximately two-thirds of the ocean. The planetary boundaries framework, developed by Johan Rockström, Will Steffen, and colleagues at the Stockholm Resilience Centre and articulated initially in their 2009 *Nature* paper "A Safe Operating Space for Humanity," updated in the 2015 *Science* paper "Planetary Boundaries: Guiding Human Development on a Changing Planet" and in Katherine Richardson and colleagues' "Earth Beyond Six of Nine Planetary Boundaries" (*Science Advances*, 2023), has not yet been operationalized into formal international governance architecture comparable to the Paris Agreement or the Convention on Biological Diversity. Its institutional traction operates through incorporation into the Sustainable Development Goals' analytical framework, through Kate Raworth's adaptation in *Doughnut Economics: Seven Ways to Think Like a 21st-Century Economist* (2017) into the doughnut framework that Amsterdam and other jurisdictions have adopted, and through its uptake in scientific and advocacy communities.

The honest critique of these international frameworks engages both realist and climate-justice strands. The realist critique is that international environmental governance has produced commitments that exceed enforcement: the absence of central enforcement authority, the asymmetric distribution of costs and benefits across states, the susceptibility of long-term commitments to short-term political reversal. The climate-justice critique, articulated by Mary Robinson in *Climate Justice: Hope, Resilience, and the Fight for a Sustainable Future* (2018) and by the Global South negotiating bloc across decades of UNFCCC negotiations, is that the architecture of international climate governance has been shaped by the negotiating leverage of historical emitters in ways that inadequately address the just-transition obligations the empirical record entails. The failure-rate critique, articulated empirically by the United Nations Environment Programme's *Emissions Gap* reports and by the Climate Action Tracker, is that the actual emissions trajectory has not been substantially altered by the Paris architecture: emissions continued to rise after 2015 and only began to decline in some developed economies in the most recent years, while developing-economy emissions continued to rise. These critiques are accurate. They do not refute the project's framework's claim that international governance is a venue for institutional-vocabulary cultivation; they specify what international governance can and cannot do.

What international climate and biodiversity governance can do for the project's framework: generate normative resources whose authority within domestic American politics exceeds what the inherited constitutional architecture can generate; produce demonstration cases of institutional design (the Loss and Damage Fund, the Te Awa Tupua structure that Part Two engages below, the Whanganui co-governance arrangement) that domestic actors can reference; create the evidentiary record on which domestic litigation, legislation, and political mobilization can draw; influence the soft-power consequences for American international standing in ways that affect domestic political calculations. What international governance cannot do: substitute for domestic political mobilization; bind the United States to obligations a domestic political coalition does not support; produce institutional change inside the United States without domestic actors translating international norms into domestic political and legal action.

### 2.2 The Welsh Future Generations Commissioner Act as multi-scale cultivation architecture

The Welsh Well-being of Future Generations Act 2015 deserves particular engagement as a contemporary instance of multi-scale cultivation architecture operating within a Western constitutional framework. The Act, adopted by the National Assembly for Wales in 2015 and operational since April 2016, establishes the Future Generations Commissioner for Wales as a statutory officer with the duty to act as the "guardian of the interests of future generations in Wales," and requires public bodies in Wales to act in pursuit of seven well-being goals: a prosperous Wales, a resilient Wales, a healthier Wales, a more equal Wales, a Wales of cohesive communities, a Wales of vibrant culture and thriving Welsh language, and a globally responsible Wales. The Act operationalizes the seven goals through five ways of working: long-term thinking, integration across well-being goals, involvement of those with an interest, collaboration across organizations, and prevention of problems. The institutional architecture includes the Commissioner's office, the well-being objectives published by each public body, the well-being statements required of public services boards established across local authority areas, and the Auditor General's role in auditing compliance.

Sophie Howe served as the first Future Generations Commissioner from 2016 to 2023, and Derek Walker has served since 2023. The institutional record includes the Commissioner's reports including *The Future Generations Report 2020* and *Cymru Can: The Strategy of the Future Generations Commissioner 2023-2030*, the legal challenges that have begun to operationalize the Act's substantive content, and the propagation of the Welsh model to other jurisdictions including the United Nations' designation of a Special Envoy for Future Generations and the various national-level future-generations institutions established in countries including Hungary, Israel, Finland, Norway, and New Zealand. The Act's significance for the project's framework is multiple. First, the Act operationalizes the seven-generation decision rule that the Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace specifies as institutional principle into a contemporary Western statutory framework — a translation of Indigenous institutional vocabulary into a contemporary constitutional architecture that the project's framework recognizes as cultivation-vocabulary construction at scale. Second, the Act's five ways of working embody the polycentric, adaptive, integrative principles the cultivation framework specifies, with the institutional architecture supporting the principles rather than treating them as exhortation. Third, the Act's institutional design includes the Commissioner role as standing institutional voice for the temporal interests the inherited political-electoral cycle systematically underweights — a structural cultivation of intergenerational decision capacity. The Welsh model has limitations that the project's framework engages honestly: the Act applies only to public bodies, the Commissioner's enforcement authority is largely advisory rather than binding, the compliance has been uneven across public bodies, and the integration with the broader UK constitutional framework has produced tensions the Act has not fully resolved. The model nonetheless represents the most institutionally substantive contemporary instance of future-generations cultivation architecture operating within a Western constitutional framework, and supplies institutional vocabulary that state-level innovation in the United States can draw on.

### 2.3 Rights of Nature jurisprudence as comparative cultivation frontier

The Rights of Nature jurisprudence developed across multiple jurisdictions over the last two decades constitutes the most institutionally substantive comparative legal frontier the project's framework can engage. The jurisprudence is not unitary. Its ontological premises, legal architectures, implementing institutions, and empirical performance vary substantially across jurisdictions. Engaging it as a single body of law conflates importantly different institutional designs.

Ecuador's 2008 Constitution, adopted under the constitutional process initiated by President Rafael Correa and shaped by the Pachakutik political movement and the broader Indigenous and ecological coalitions of the period, established Rights of Nature in Articles 71 through 74. Article 71 provides that "Nature, or Pacha Mama, where life is reproduced and exists, has the right to integral respect for its existence and for the maintenance and regeneration of its life cycles, structure, functions and evolutionary processes." Article 72 establishes the right to restoration of nature, distinct from human compensation for damage. Article 73 requires the State to apply precautionary and restrictive measures to activities that can lead to species extinction, ecosystem destruction, or permanent natural-cycle alteration. Article 74 recognizes the right of communities to benefit from environmental services. The implementing jurisprudence has developed across several major cases. The Vilcabamba River case (*Wheeler v. Provincial Government of Loja*, Provincial Court of Loja, 2011), the first successful Rights of Nature litigation under the new constitutional provisions, ruled in favor of the river against road-construction activities that had altered its course and damaged its banks. The Yasuní-ITT case, while not strictly a Rights of Nature decision, addressed the protection of Yasuní National Park against oil development and culminated in the 2023 referendum in which Ecuadorian voters approved the indefinite suspension of oil extraction in Block 43-ITT. The Los Cedros Forest case (Constitutional Court of Ecuador Case No. 1149-19-JP/21, decided 2021) established the most substantial constitutional jurisprudence on Rights of Nature to date, with the Court ruling that mining concessions in the Los Cedros protected forest violated the rights of nature and ordered their cancellation.

The Mihnea Tănăsescu literature, including *Understanding the Rights of Nature: A Critical Introduction* (2022), has specified the legal-architectural questions Rights of Nature jurisprudence raises and not yet fully answered: whether nature is a rights-bearer comparable to a person or a different kind of legal entity; what entities (rivers, forests, ecosystems, species, individual non-human animals) hold what rights; who has standing to litigate on behalf of nature; what remedies follow from rights violations; how Rights of Nature interact with property rights, with economic-development imperatives, with Indigenous sovereignty, and with state authority. Craig Kauffman and Pamela Martin's *The Politics of Rights of Nature: Strategies for Building a More Sustainable Future* (2021) has documented the implementation gap between constitutional and statutory adoption of Rights of Nature and on-the-ground ecological outcomes: in many jurisdictions, including parts of Ecuadorian and Bolivian implementation, the formal legal recognition of Rights of Nature has not translated into changed outcomes on extractive activity, sometimes because of lack of judicial enforcement, sometimes because of conflicting development imperatives, sometimes because of the political-economy power of extractive industries.

Bolivia's Law of the Rights of Mother Earth (Ley de Derechos de la Madre Tierra), adopted in 2010 and supplemented by the Framework Law of Mother Earth and Integral Development for Living Well (Ley Marco de la Madre Tierra y Desarrollo Integral para Vivir Bien) in 2012, establishes a different architectural design. The 2010 Law recognizes Mother Earth as a collective subject of public interest and establishes specific rights including the right to life, the right to the diversity of life, the right to water, the right to clean air, the right to equilibrium, the right to restoration, and the right to live free from pollution. The 2012 Framework Law operationalizes the rights through the broader concept of *vivir bien* (living well, drawn from the Andean concept of *suma qamaña* in Aymara and *sumak kawsay* in Quechua) and establishes implementing institutions including the Plurinational Mother Earth Authority.

New Zealand's Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017 represents an institutional design distinct from the Ecuadorian and Bolivian constitutional frameworks. The Act recognizes the Whanganui River as Te Awa Tupua, a legal person with all the corresponding rights, duties, and liabilities of a legal person. The Act establishes Te Pou Tupua as the human face of Te Awa Tupua, comprising two persons appointed jointly by the Crown and by Whanganui Iwi (the Maori tribal nation whose traditional territory includes the river). The Act's design grounds the legal personhood not in an abstract rights-of-nature framework but in the Treaty of Waitangi settlement process and in the specific *tikanga* (Maori law and practice) of the Whanganui Iwi, including the foundational principle that "Ko au te awa, ko te awa ko au" — I am the river, the river is me. The institutional design includes the Te Pou Tupua structure for representing the river, the Te Karewao advisory group, the Te Heke Ngahuru strategy group, and the Te Kōpuka management plan structure. Anne Salmond's *Tears of Rangi: Experiments Across Worlds* (2017) supplies the comparative-anthropological framing through which the Whanganui arrangement can be understood as ontological-cultivation work — the institutional articulation of relational ontology within a Western legal framework. The Te Urewera Act 2014, addressing the former Te Urewera National Park, establishes a similar but distinct framework: the legal personhood of Te Urewera as a former-national-park area, with the Te Urewera Board appointed by Tūhoe (the iwi whose territory includes Te Urewera) and the Crown jointly. The strategic significance for the project's framework is that the New Zealand experiments demonstrate the legal personhood of natural systems can be grounded in Indigenous law and treaty relationships rather than in abstract Rights of Nature constitutional language, and that the resulting institutional designs have specific co-governance properties that the broader Rights of Nature jurisprudence has not generally achieved.

Colombia's Constitutional Court Decision T-622 of 2016, addressing the Atrato River, recognized the river as a "subject of rights" entitled to "protection, conservation, maintenance, and restoration," and established a guardianship structure for the river's representation. The decision was followed by Decision STC-4360 of 2018 from the Supreme Court of Justice's Civil Cassation Chamber, which recognized the Colombian Amazon as a "subject of rights" and ordered the executive branch to develop and implement a plan to address deforestation. Subsequent decisions have addressed the Magdalena River, the Cauca River, the Pisba River, the La Plata River, and other ecosystems. Daniel Bonilla Maldonado's work on Colombian constitutional law situates the Rights of Nature jurisprudence within the broader Colombian constitutional tradition of progressive jurisprudence. The Colombian implementation has encountered the political-economy and security challenges that constrain Colombian environmental governance generally — the assassination of environmental defenders, the persistence of armed-group operations in resource-extraction zones, the political-economy power of mining and agribusiness interests — but the constitutional jurisprudence itself has continued to develop and has influenced comparative jurisprudence in other Latin American jurisdictions.

The Indian jurisprudence on the Ganges (Ganga) and Yamuna rivers, developed initially by the Uttarakhand High Court in 2017 in *Mohd. Salim v. State of Uttarakhand* and subsequent decisions, recognized the rivers as legal persons with the corresponding rights. The decisions were stayed by the Supreme Court of India shortly thereafter on the ground that the legal-personhood designation produced practical difficulties including the question of who would be liable for damage caused by river flooding. The Indian jurisprudence is therefore an instance of Rights of Nature recognition that has not stabilized institutionally, and it serves the project's framework as a cautionary case about the design questions that legal-personhood frameworks must resolve to operate durably.

The White Earth Band of Ojibwe's Rights of Manoomin, adopted by tribal council resolution in 2018, recognizes the rights of wild rice (manoomin in Anishinaabemowin) within the territory of the White Earth Band's reservation. The litigation that followed — the White Earth Band's tribal-court action against the Minnesota Department of Natural Resources over the issuance of water-rights permits to the Enbridge Line 3 pipeline that the Band claimed would harm wild rice — represents one of the first instances of tribal-court Rights of Nature jurisprudence in the United States. The Yurok Tribe's Rights of the Klamath River, adopted in 2019, represents a similar institutional development; the Yurok Tribe's involvement in the Klamath dam-removal coalition, culminating in the 2024 dam removal, demonstrates the operational capacity of tribal Rights of Nature recognition when integrated with broader environmental coalitions.

### 2.4 UNDRIP and the Free, Prior and Informed Consent cultivation vocabulary

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, adopted by the General Assembly in September 2007 with 144 states in favor, four against (the United States, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand), and eleven abstentions, establishes the framework of international Indigenous-rights law that the contemporary period has continued to develop. The four states that initially opposed the Declaration have subsequently endorsed it in modified form: Australia in 2009, New Zealand in 2010, Canada in 2010 (and through legislation in 2021), and the United States in 2010. The endorsement is not the same as ratification or domestic-law incorporation, and the United States's endorsement was accompanied by interpretive statements that constrained its domestic effect.

The Declaration's substantive content includes the right of Indigenous peoples to self-determination (Article 3), the right to autonomy or self-government in matters relating to internal and local affairs (Article 4), the right to maintain and strengthen distinct political, legal, economic, social, and cultural institutions (Article 5), and a series of more specific rights including the right to Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) for relocation (Article 10), for the use of cultural property (Article 11), for legislative or administrative measures that may affect them (Article 19), and for projects affecting their lands or territories and other resources (Article 32). The FPIC framework operates as a procedural right whose substantive content has been developed across two decades of jurisprudence, treaty-body interpretation, and practical implementation.

S. James Anaya (Apache), who served as the United Nations Special Rapporteur on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples from 2008 to 2014 and whose *International Human Rights and Indigenous Peoples* (2009) constitutes one of the most authoritative legal analyses of the Declaration's content, has specified what FPIC requires: that consent be free of coercion, intimidation, or manipulation; that it be obtained prior to authorization or commencement of activities; that it be informed, with adequate information about the project's nature and impacts; and that consent be the substantive standard, with specific guidance on when consent is required versus when consultation may suffice. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights has applied the FPIC framework in cases including *Saramaka People v. Suriname* (2007), *Kichwa Indigenous People of Sarayaku v. Ecuador* (2012), and *Kaliña and Lokono Peoples v. Suriname* (2015), establishing regional jurisprudence that operates as comparative law for jurisdictions including the United States.

Canada's United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act, adopted in June 2021, requires the federal government to take all measures necessary to ensure that the laws of Canada are consistent with the Declaration, to develop and implement an action plan to achieve the Declaration's objectives, and to report annually on progress. The Act does not directly incorporate the Declaration as binding domestic law, and its implementation has been contested, but it represents a more substantive operationalization of UNDRIP norms than the American non-implementation, and the comparative material has been used in American advocacy and litigation. The strategic litigation that has used UNDRIP norms in domestic American courts — the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe's litigation over the Dakota Access Pipeline including *Standing Rock Sioux Tribe v. U.S. Army Corps of Engineers* and the subsequent litigation through 2020; the Mauna Kea protests against the Thirty Meter Telescope; the Bears Ears litigation over the boundaries of the national monument; and various pipeline and extractive-industry challenges — has drawn on UNDRIP norms in advocacy and amicus briefing, although the controlling legal framework has been domestic. The norms have operated as interpretive resources, advocacy framings, and soft-law constraints on administrative discretion. The project's framework values these uses for the cultivation work they perform: the construction of a body of precedent in which UNDRIP norms have been deployed in domestic American legal contexts, the development of advocacy infrastructure that can mobilize UNDRIP norms in future cases, and the gradual normalization of FPIC standards in administrative and corporate decision-making.

Kyle Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi), particularly in "Indigenous Climate Change Studies: Indigenizing Futures, Decolonizing the Anthropocene" (*English Language Notes*, 2017) and his subsequent work, articulates the theoretical framework within which UNDRIP norms operate as more than procedural protections: as the institutional architecture for the recognition of Indigenous peoples as polities with their own governance traditions, decision procedures, and ecological relationships. Glen Coulthard's (Yellowknives Dene) *Red Skin, White Masks: Rejecting the Colonial Politics of Recognition* (2014) articulates a critique of the recognition framework itself, arguing that institutional recognition by colonial states can substitute for the substantive return of land and political authority that Indigenous self-determination actually requires. The project's framework engages both Whyte's institutional account and Coulthard's structural critique without resolving the tension between them, because the tension is itself a feature of the institutional terrain the multi-decade project must navigate.

### 2.5 Indigenous governance traditions as load-bearing cultivation vocabulary

The project's framework specifies that Indigenous governance traditions are not optional supplementation to the institutional vocabulary the multi-decade project requires but load-bearing components of that vocabulary. The framing matters because the alternatives are widely available and have specific failure modes. Treating Indigenous traditions as historical curiosities, as repositories of "wisdom" detached from operational governance, as ornament added to a Western institutional framework, or as one example among many of "alternative" approaches all underestimate what the traditions actually are: continuing, operational, intellectually substantive governance institutions whose principles, decision procedures, and ontological frames the project's framework must engage on their own terms. The engagement under `VII.01_Multi_Eyed_Seeing_Constitutional_Method_v6`'s methodological discipline requires citing Indigenous scholars and primary sources directly. The vocabulary is cross-referenced to `III.01_Biocultural_Governance_v6` and `IV.01_Deep_Substrate_v6` rather than duplicated here; this section names the traditions and the institutional vocabulary they supply at the operational-layer scale.

The Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace (Kaianere'kó:wa) supplies the seven-generation decision rule as procedural standard, the consensus-with-clan-deliberation procedures, the Clan Mother veto, the explicit provision for the removal of chiefs who fail to act consistently with the Great Law's principles, and the dedication of decision procedures to "peace, power, and righteousness" (skennen, kasastensera, kariwiio) as substantive standards. Oren Lyons (Onondaga), John Mohawk (Seneca), and Doug George-Kanentiio (Mohawk) constitute primary sources for the tradition. Oren Lyons and John Mohawk's co-authored *Exiled in the Land of the Free: Democracy, Indian Nations, and the U.S. Constitution* (1992) establishes the historical case for the Great Law's influence on the American constitutional founders and articulates the principles that distinguish the Great Law from the constitutional architecture the founders eventually adopted.

The Andean *buen vivir* or *sumak kawsay* tradition, articulated in Quechua as *sumak kawsay* and in Aymara as *suma qamaña*, supplies the principles of living well rather than living better, the centrality of reciprocity (*ayni*) in social and ecological relationships, the recognition of Pacha Mama as a subject of relationship rather than as an object of use, the integration of human and ecological flourishing rather than their separation, and the rejection of the development model that frames progress as accumulation. Eduardo Gudynas's *Derechos de la Naturaleza: Ética biocéntrica y políticas ambientales* (2014), Catherine Walsh's work on interculturality and decolonial thought including her *Interculturalidad, Estado, Sociedad: Luchas (de)coloniales de nuestra época* (2009), and Marisol de la Cadena's *Earth Beings: Ecologies of Practice across Andean Worlds* (2015) supply the analytical infrastructure. The ayllu-and-cabildo political structures, the Andean territorial governance traditions, and the integration of ecological and political relationships in *buen vivir* constitute the institutional vocabulary the project's framework engages.

The Pacific Islander Talanoa tradition — the institutionalized practice of inclusive, participatory, transparent dialogue developed across the Pacific Islander region and operationalized in the UNFCCC's Talanoa Dialogue of 2017-2018 under the Fijian COP23 presidency — supplies institutional vocabulary for deliberative practice that integrates story-telling, relational accountability, and structured listening into formal policy deliberation. The Pacific Islander climate-justice movement, including the work of Tuvalu, the Marshall Islands, and the Alliance of Small Island States, has deployed the Talanoa tradition as institutional vocabulary in international climate negotiations with documented effects on the substance of negotiation outcomes.

The Māori marae-based deliberation tradition, operationalized through Te Tiriti o Waitangi and the contemporary Te Tiriti settlement process, supplies institutional vocabulary for treaty-grounded co-governance arrangements that the Whanganui and Te Urewera arrangements operationalize. The Matike Mai Aotearoa report (2016), produced by the Independent Working Group on Constitutional Transformation chaired by Moana Jackson (Ngāti Kahungunu, Ngāti Porou) and Margaret Mutu (Ngāti Kahu, Te Rarawa, Ngāti Whātua), and developed across more than 250 hui (gatherings) over five years, articulates a constitutional vision for Aotearoa New Zealand grounded in Te Tiriti o Waitangi and in *tikanga Māori*, including the institutional architecture of the *rangatiratanga* (Māori chiefly authority) sphere alongside *kāwanatanga* (Crown government), with the work of integration across the spheres conducted through the *relational* sphere. The Matike Mai work supplies institutional vocabulary for treaty-grounded plural constitutional architecture that the American context can engage as comparative cultivation work.

The African palaver tradition, articulated in the political philosophy of figures including Léopold Sédar Senghor and operationalized in various contemporary African deliberative institutions, supplies institutional vocabulary for sustained deliberation oriented to consensus formation rather than to majority decision. The Ubuntu philosophical tradition, articulated principally in Southern African political philosophy through Mogobe Ramose's *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (1999) and operationalized in South African post-apartheid jurisprudence through cases including *S v Makwanyane* 1995 (3) SA 391 (CC), *Port Elizabeth Municipality v. Various Occupiers* 2005 (1) SA 217 (CC), and *Dikoko v. Mokhatla* 2006 (6) SA 235 (CC), supplies the relational ontology in which legal and political subjects are not pre-political individuals whose rights are negotiated but persons constituted through relationship. Drucilla Cornell and Kenneth Muvangua's co-edited *uBuntu and the Law: African Ideals and Postapartheid Jurisprudence* (2011) articulates how Ubuntu operates as a substantive jurisprudential principle.

### 2.6 German *Mitbestimmung*, the Norwegian Government Pension Fund Global, and the Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat as multi-scale cultivation architectures

Three contemporary European institutional architectures deserve engagement as multi-scale cultivation architectures whose institutional vocabulary the multi-decade project draws on. The German *Mitbestimmung* (codetermination) tradition, established in its contemporary form through the Codetermination Act (Mitbestimmungsgesetz) of 1976 and the broader codetermination legislation extending from the post-war Works Constitution Act through subsequent decades, establishes the institutional architecture through which German workers participate in corporate governance — supervisory-board representation in companies above 2,000 employees, works councils (Betriebsräte) at the workplace level under the Works Constitution Act, and the broader cooperative-bargaining infrastructure that integrates labor representation with corporate decision-making. Otto Kahn-Freund's foundational comparative-labor-law analysis, including *Labour and the Law* (1972) and his broader work on comparative codetermination, articulates the institutional architecture. The contemporary German-codetermination scholarship, including the work of Lutz Bellmann and the Hans-Böckler-Stiftung research, has produced empirical analysis of the institutional record across multiple decades. The model's significance for the project's framework is that it operationalizes the principle of stakeholder governance — workers as stakeholders in corporate decision-making, not as parties whose interests are mediated entirely through labor markets — into a contemporary Western legal architecture. The model has limitations the project's framework engages honestly: codetermination has not prevented the broader neoliberal restructuring of the German economy, the supervisory-board representation has been substantially symbolic in many companies, and the integration with the contemporary platform-economy work has been incomplete. The institutional vocabulary nonetheless supplies cultivation resources for the American context.

The Norwegian Government Pension Fund Global, established in 1990 as the Government Petroleum Fund and renamed in 2006, operates as the contemporary world's largest sovereign wealth fund with assets exceeding $1.6 trillion as of 2024. The Fund is capitalized through Norwegian petroleum revenues and operated with the institutional architecture of intergenerational cultivation: the spending rule limits annual withdrawals to approximately three percent of fund value (the real return target), the investment strategy follows ethical guidelines including exclusions for companies involved in coal extraction, tobacco production, controversial weapons production, and serious violations of human rights or environmental harm, and the institutional governance includes the Norges Bank Investment Management operational structure and the Council on Ethics oversight body. The Fund's significance for the project's framework is that it operationalizes the principle of intergenerational cultivation as institutional architecture — the systematic accumulation of resource-extraction revenues into intergenerational capital, with the spending rule operating as an institutional constraint on the present generation's drawing down of intergenerational resources. The institutional vocabulary the Fund supplies — the intergenerational spending rule, the ethical-investment guidelines, the Council on Ethics structure — connects forward to `XII.04_Intergenerational_Rectification_v6` on intergenerational rectification and to `XII.02_Currency_Architecture_v6` on currency architecture as structural cultivation. The Fund's limitations include the dependence on petroleum revenues whose climate-policy implications the Fund's ethical guidelines do not fully address, the political-economy questions about Norwegian access to global financial markets, and the operational questions about whether the Fund's institutional architecture is transferable to non-resource-rich economies. The cultivation vocabulary nonetheless supplies institutional resources the American context can engage.

The French Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat, conducted from October 2019 through June 2020 under the institutional architecture established by President Macron in the aftermath of the *gilets jaunes* protests, convened 150 randomly selected citizens to produce recommendations on climate policy that would reduce French emissions by forty percent below 1990 levels by 2030 in a manner consistent with social justice. The Convention produced 149 recommendations across multiple policy areas — housing, transportation, food and agriculture, consumption, work, and governance — of which the Macron government formally rejected three and committed to advancing the others. The subsequent legislative implementation through the Climate and Resilience Law of August 2021 was substantially weaker than the Convention's recommendations had specified, producing the political controversy and the broader deliberative-democracy literature critique that the institutional design did not include adequate guarantees that the recommendations would be implemented. Mathilde Imer and Cyril Dion's documentation of the Convention process, and the broader academic literature including Yves Sintomer's work on sortition and deliberative democracy and Hélène Landemore's analysis in *Open Democracy: Reinventing Popular Rule for the Twenty-First Century* (2020), supplies the institutional record. The Convention's significance for the project's framework is multiple: it demonstrates that sortition-based deliberative architectures can produce substantive policy recommendations on contested issues at scales of national consequence; it illustrates the institutional design questions about the relationship between deliberative-assembly recommendations and the elected-representative architecture; and it constitutes a contemporary instance of cultivation-of-the-cognitive-substrate work conducted through institutional rather than communicative means.

### 2.7 The Taiwanese vTaiwan and Pol.is and the Brazilian participatory-budgeting tradition as digital-deliberation and participatory cultivation architectures

Two contemporary architectures of participatory cultivation deserve engagement as institutional vocabulary the multi-decade project draws on. The Taiwanese vTaiwan platform and the Pol.is open-source software, developed in the context of the post-Sunflower Movement political reorganization of 2014 and operationalized through Audrey Tang's tenure as Taiwan's Digital Minister (2016-2024), constitute the most institutionally substantive contemporary instance of digital-deliberation infrastructure operating at scales of national consequence. The vTaiwan platform operates through a structured process: open online consultation in which citizens submit statements about a policy issue; Pol.is statistical analysis that identifies points of agreement and disagreement across statements; structured deliberation among participants oriented to finding the consensus-points the Pol.is analysis surfaces; and the integration of the consensus-points into the formal policy-making process. The platform has been deployed on policy issues including the regulation of Uber and other gig-economy platforms, the legalization of cannabis for medical purposes, the regulation of online alcohol sales, and various other questions on which the conventional political process produced gridlock. Glen Weyl (2024) and the broader Plurality corpus that Tang and Weyl have developed with the broader Plurality community, articulates the theoretical framework within which the Taiwanese institutional experiments operate. The model's significance for the project's framework is multiple: it demonstrates that digital-deliberation infrastructure operating at scales of national consequence is institutionally feasible; it integrates the cognitive-aware design principles Part Three develops with the institutional architecture Part Two develops; and it operationalizes a specific contemporary cultivation methodology that has produced measurable substantive outcomes. The institutional vocabulary nonetheless supplies cultivation resources for the American context. The integration with `XII.03_E_Democracy_Structural_Public_Check_v6` (Part XII e-democracy as structural public check) operates at the level of operational instantiation, with the e-democracy document specifying the institutional architecture in greater detail.

The Brazilian participatory-budgeting tradition, established in Porto Alegre in 1989 and propagated across multiple Brazilian cities and subsequently across approximately 11,000 municipalities globally by 2022, constitutes the most extensively documented contemporary instance of participatory governance at municipal scales. Gianpaolo Baiocchi's *Militants and Citizens: The Politics of Participatory Democracy in Porto Alegre* (2005), drawing on extended ethnographic work in Porto Alegre during the participatory-budgeting period, supplies the institutional analysis. The participatory-budgeting methodology operates through structured deliberation in neighborhood assemblies, the election of delegates to regional and municipal assemblies, the construction of investment proposals through cross-sector and cross-neighborhood deliberation, and the integration of the proposals into the formal municipal budget. The Porto Alegre implementation produced documented improvements in the spatial distribution of municipal investment, with substantial increases in infrastructure investment in historically underserved neighborhoods, alongside the institutional cultivation of citizen participation capacity that propagated beyond the participatory-budgeting institution itself. The Decidim platform, developed by Barcelona-en-Comú during Ada Colau's mayoral administration (2015-2023) and operationalized as open-source digital infrastructure for participatory democracy that has subsequently been adopted by municipalities across Spain, France, Belgium, Italy, Mexico, and elsewhere, constitutes the contemporary digital instantiation of the participatory-democracy tradition. The Helsinki participatory-budgeting documentation, the New York City participatory-budgeting program operating through City Council districts, and the various American municipal participatory-budgeting experiments constitute the contemporary American record. The institutional vocabulary the participatory-budgeting tradition supplies — structured deliberation, delegate-and-assembly architecture, integration with formal budgetary process, propagation across municipal scales — operates at the operational scale that the project's framework engages as cultivation of multi-scale governance capacity.

### 2.8 Indian and South African transformative-constitutionalism architectures, and the Chilean and Tunisian constitutional processes

The Indian and South African transformative-constitutionalism architectures supply institutional vocabulary at the constitutional-architecture scale that complements the operational-layer architectures Parts Two engages above. Upendra Baxi's *The Future of Human Rights* (2002), articulating the framework of transformative constitutionalism as constitutional-legal architecture oriented to substantive social transformation, specifies the Indian Constitution's institutional architecture: the Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV), the Fundamental Rights chapter (Part III), and the expansive interpretive jurisprudence the Indian Supreme Court has developed across cases from *Kesavananda Bharati v. Karl Klare's "Legal Culture and Transformative Constitutionalism" (*South African Journal on Human Rights*, 1998), articulating the South African transformative-constitutionalism framework, specifies the institutional architecture of the 1996 South African Constitution: the substantive-equality jurisprudence under Section 9, the socio-economic-rights jurisprudence under Sections 26 through 29 (housing, healthcare, food, water, social security, education), the Chapter 9 institutions (the Public Protector, the Human Rights Commission, the Gender Commission), and the broader constitutional architecture of transformative justice. The Chilean constitutional process initiated by the *estallido social* of October 2019 and the Tunisian constitutional process initiated by the Jasmine Revolution of December 2010 through January 2011 supply contemporary case material on the limits and conditions of constitutional cultivation. The Chilean Constitutional Convention of 2021-2022 produced a draft constitution that was the most ambitious progressive constitutional proposal in the contemporary Latin American context — articulating plurinationality, environmental and Rights of Nature provisions, expansive social-rights guarantees, the abolition of the Senate and creation of a unicameral legislature, and a broader transformative-constitutional architecture — but the draft was rejected by a 62-38 referendum margin in September 2022. The Tunisian process produced the 2014 Constitution that was widely treated as the most institutionally articulated democratic constitution in the post-Arab-Spring Middle Eastern context — establishing a semi-presidential architecture with constitutional protections for civil liberties, judicial independence, electoral integrity, and decentralization — and the post-2014 operational record was substantially complicated by the cumulative economic difficulties and political-coalition fragmentation that produced the 2021 political crisis and Saied's subsequent constitutional consolidations through the 2022 referendum-approved constitution that concentrated executive power. The Chilean and Tunisian cases supply cautionary cultivation material: constitutional-refoundation processes can produce regressive as well as progressive outcomes, can fail to consolidate even after constitutional adoption, and require the integration with the broader cultivation work the project's framework specifies rather than treating constitutional adoption as the end point.

## PART THREE — COGNITIVE-AWARE POLITICAL MOBILIZATION AS CULTIVATION OF THE COGNITIVE SUBSTRATE

Parts One and Two specified the institutional venues and vocabularies the multi-decade project requires. The state-level layer builds bioregional cultivation; the international and Indigenous-governance layer supplies multi-scale cultivation vocabulary. Neither layer produces political force without translation. The translation is the work of the third layer this Part addresses: the cultivation of the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale, through which institutional resources at lower scales generate the political pressure that scales them up. `X.01_Political_Economy_Transformative_Legislation_v6` identified the political-force gap — the gap between empirical crisis severity and the political pressure actually mobilized in response — as the binding constraint on transformative action under contemporary conditions. The gap is not produced by ignorance, by indifference, or by the moral failures the moralizing literature has often invoked. It is produced by the systematic interaction between the empirical features of the contemporary crises and the cognitive architecture human reasoners actually have, with the interaction mediated by the political and informational institutions through which crises are processed into political pressure or fail to be. Closing the gap is not a problem of better facts or louder messaging. It is the cultivation of the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale, conducted through communication, organizing, deliberative-institutional, and adaptive-leadership methodologies that work with the cognitive architecture human reasoners actually have.

### 3.1 What the cognitive science specifies about the substrate the cultivation work operates on

The cognitive-science literature engaged across `II.06_Cognitive_Informational_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v6` and `VIII.01_Minds_Markets_Sacred_v6` provides specific resources for diagnosing why environmental, democratic-reform, and economic-justice messaging has systematically underperformed relative to its empirical basis. The diagnosis is not unitary; multiple cognitive features interact to produce the underperformance, and effective cultivation interventions must address the specific features rather than generic "better communication."

Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), synthesizing decades of work with Amos Tversky and others, specifies that human reasoning operates through two systems with substantially different properties. System 1 operates fast, automatic, intuitive, low-effort; System 2 operates slow, controlled, analytical, high-effort. The interaction matters for the political-force question because System 1 substantially shapes political reasoning even when System 2 is engaged: judgments of evidence quality, of source credibility, of risk magnitude, and of action priorities are heavily influenced by System 1 features that operate before System 2 deliberation. The cognitive features Kahneman specifies are not bugs to be removed; they are features of human cognition that have been adaptive across the long arc of human evolutionary history and that operate by default in political reasoning. Effective communication does not pretend to bypass them but works with them.

Yaacov Trope and Nira Liberman's construal-level theory, developed in their joint work including "Construal-Level Theory of Psychological Distance" (*Psychological Review*, 2010), specifies a cognitive feature directly relevant to climate, democratic-decline, and other long-term-stakes communication. The theory holds that psychological distance — temporal, spatial, social, and hypothetical — systematically affects how people construe events. Events that are psychologically distant are construed in abstract, decontextualized, high-level terms; events that are psychologically near are construed in concrete, contextualized, low-level terms. The implication is that the empirical features of climate communication that have been most prominent — temporally distant effects (impacts decades hence), spatially distant effects (impacts in other regions), socially distant effects (impacts on out-groups), hypothetically distant effects (probabilistic projections rather than confirmed observations) — produce abstract construal that systematically reduces the urgency and concreteness with which the issue is processed.

Dan Kahan and the Cultural Cognition Project at Yale Law School, developing across more than a decade of empirical work including Kahan's "Cultural Cognition and Public Policy" (*Yale Law Journal*, 2006), specify a cognitive feature whose implications the political-communication community has been slower to absorb than its empirical robustness warrants. Cultural cognition operates through identity-protective cognition: people process information about empirically contested questions in ways that protect the identity of the cultural-political group with which they identify. Kahan's empirical work has demonstrated cultural-cognition effects across a wide range of empirical questions — climate change, gun policy, vaccine safety, fracking risk, nuclear waste — with the magnitude of the effect substantial enough to invert the relationship between scientific literacy and accurate empirical judgment for individuals strongly identified with cultures whose received view conflicts with the scientific consensus. The implication is that informational-deficit communication strategies — strategies premised on the assumption that providing more or better information will change the empirical judgments of those whose current judgments are inconsistent with the evidence — systematically fail when the empirical questions are entangled with cultural-identity questions.

Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber's *The Enigma of Reason: A New Theory of Human Understanding* (2017) develops the argumentative theory of reason that significantly revises the standard account of how human reasoning actually operates. The theory holds that reason is best understood as evolved primarily for argumentation — for producing reasons that justify positions to others and for evaluating reasons others give — rather than for unbiased individual truth-seeking. The empirical pattern of human reasoning, including the systematic operation of confirmation bias, the asymmetry between argument production and argument evaluation, and the substantial improvement in reasoning that occurs in deliberative discussion, is best explained by this argumentative theory. The implication for political communication is substantial: communication strategies premised on individual reasoners independently weighing evidence misunderstand the cognitive architecture, while strategies that engage reasoning in social and deliberative contexts work with the architecture. Mercier's *Not Born Yesterday: The Science of Who We Trust and What We Believe* (2020) develops the further implication that humans are not as gullible as the disinformation-panic literature often suggests; people are reasonably careful about which sources they trust, but their care operates through specific heuristics that are vulnerable to specific manipulations.

George Lakoff's cognitive-frames work in *Don't Think of an Elephant!* (2004) and *The All New Don't Think of an Elephant!* (2014) specifies how the framing of political issues systematically shapes their cognitive processing. Lakoff's analysis, drawing on the cognitive-linguistics tradition, holds that political issues are processed through cognitive frames — coherent structured representations of situations and their participants — and that the choice of frame substantially determines what counts as a relevant fact, who counts as a relevant actor, and what counts as an appropriate response.

Joshua Kalla and David Broockman's empirical work on deep canvassing and persuasion, including "Reducing Exclusionary Attitudes through Interpersonal Conversation: Evidence from Three Field Experiments" (*American Political Science Review*, 2020) and their subsequent work, has produced what is currently the most empirically robust finding about durable attitude change in political contexts. The deep-canvassing methodology, developed in practice by the Leadership LAB at the Los Angeles LGBT Center and tested empirically through multiple randomized controlled trials, produces durable attitude change on contested social issues including transgender rights, immigration, and racial justice through extended one-on-one conversations that include perspective-taking exercises, narrative exchange, and explicit engagement with the canvassed person's own experience. The finding is significant in two respects. First, the durability of the effect — measured at intervals up to several months after the conversation — substantially exceeds what most political-persuasion interventions produce, where effects typically decay rapidly. Second, the methodology's mechanism appears to operate through cognitive features the standard political-communication models do not target: the activation of the canvassed person's own related experiences, the construction of common ground through narrative exchange, the engagement of perspective-taking processes the cognitive-empathy literature identifies as powerful.

The diagnostic synthesis is that the political-force gap is produced by the interaction of multiple cognitive features — the System 1 / System 2 architecture, construal-level effects, cultural cognition, the argumentative structure of reasoning, frame effects — with the empirical features of the contemporary crises that activate the cognitive features unfavorably. The standard environmental, democratic-reform, and economic-justice messaging has underperformed because it has frequently presented temporally distant, spatially distant, statistically framed, identity-threatening content through individual-reasoner channels, in ways that activate availability and construal-level effects unfavorably, threaten cultural-identity processing, and rely on individual evidence-weighing rather than deliberative-argumentation processes. Closing the gap requires cultivation methodologies that work with rather than against the cognitive architecture.

### 3.2 Adaptive leadership as cultivation-condition specification at the organism-and-organ-system scale

The adaptive-leadership material the multi-decade project requires is integrated here as cultivation-condition specification at the organism-and-organ-system scale under bounded-cognition-at-scale conditions. The framing matters because adaptive leadership is commonly read as personality-based or skills-based leadership theory — a particular set of capacities individuals develop and deploy in leadership positions. That reading misses what the adaptive-leadership material actually specifies. Adaptive leadership is the governance response to the bounded-cognition-at-scale problem `IV.01_Deep_Substrate_v6` (Part IV) carries: the structural conditions under which complex adaptive systems generate the kind of disturbances that individual reasoners and conventional institutional architectures cannot absorb without adaptive cultivation work.

Ronald Heifetz's *Leadership Without Easy Answers* (1994), developed through his teaching at the Harvard Kennedy School and his work with practitioners across multiple sectors, articulates the distinction between technical and adaptive challenges. Technical challenges are those for which the institutional knowledge and procedures already exist; the work is to apply them. Adaptive challenges are those for which the institutional knowledge and procedures do not yet exist; the work is to cultivate the conditions under which the polity itself can develop the necessary learning, identity-revision, and capacity-building. Heifetz's framework specifies that the standard institutional response to adaptive challenges — treating them as if they were technical, applying available expertise, expecting authoritative answers — systematically fails. The adaptive response requires cultivating the conditions under which the polity itself does the adaptive work: distinguishing technical from adaptive elements of complex challenges, holding the polity in the "productive zone of disequilibrium" within which adaptive work can be done, regulating the distress the adaptive work produces, and constructing the institutional containers within which the work can proceed.

Ronald Heifetz and Marty Linsky's *Leadership on the Line: Staying Alive Through the Dangers of Leading* (2002, updated edition 2017) develops the practical and political analysis of adaptive-leadership work. The analysis specifies the specific institutional dynamics that adaptive-leadership work confronts — the systematic resistance from the polity to adaptive work that disturbs settled identities, the deflection of adaptive challenges onto technical-problem framings, the attempts to delegate adaptive work to authority figures whose response is expected to substitute for the polity's own work, the institutional infrastructure within which adaptive work can or cannot proceed. The work specifies what cultivation-condition specification at the organism-and-organ-system scale actually consists of: not the development of individual leadership capacity in isolation, but the construction of institutional conditions under which adaptive work can be conducted by the polity. Heifetz, Linsky, and Alexander Grashow's *The Practice of Adaptive Leadership: Tools and Tactics for Changing Your Organization and the World* (2009) operationalizes the framework into practitioner-applicable methodology.

The adaptive-leadership framework's significance for the project's framework is multiple. First, the framework specifies the governance-response logic under bounded-cognition-at-scale conditions that the project's diagnostic phase identified. The bounded-cognition-at-scale problem is that individual reasoners and conventional institutional architectures cannot process complex adaptive system dynamics in real time; the adaptive-leadership response is not to overcome the bound but to cultivate the institutional conditions within which the bounded reasoners and conventional architectures can do adaptive work despite the bound. Second, the framework operates at the organism-and-organ-system scale — the human reasoner as cognitive organism with specific cognitive-architecture features, the institution as organ with specific operational-architecture features, the integration of the two as the cultivation site at which the political-force gap is closed. Third, the framework integrates with the cognitive-science findings the previous section engaged: adaptive-leadership methodology operates through structured disequilibrium that activates System 2 deliberation, through narrative and identity work that engages cultural-cognition processing in service of adaptive learning rather than identity protection, through institutional containers that support the deliberative-argumentation processes the Mercier-Sperber literature identifies as essential to durable reasoning shifts. Fourth, the framework specifies the institutional-design questions the multi-decade project's cognitive-aware mobilization work must address: not the cultivation of individual leadership capacity in isolation, but the construction of institutional conditions within which adaptive work can be conducted by the polity at scales beyond the individual.

### 3.3 Cognitive-aware methodologies the practitioner literature has developed

The practitioner literature on cognitive-aware cultivation work has developed substantially across the contemporary period, with several methodologies producing measurable durable attitude change and several others producing only short-term mobilization. The project's framework distinguishes these categories.

Marshall Ganz's public narrative methodology, developed at the Harvard Kennedy School and articulated in his teaching, training materials, and *Why David Sometimes Wins: Leadership, Organization, and Strategy in the California Farm Worker Movement* (2009), specifies a structured approach to political narrative that integrates personal, collective, and immediate dimensions. The story of self addresses the speaker's own formation and motivations; the story of us addresses the collective identity of the audience and movement; the story of now addresses the urgent moment of choice. The methodology was deployed with substantial impact in the Obama 2008 campaign's Camp Obama trainings, in the subsequent organizing infrastructure of the New Organizing Institute and successor organizations, and in the international training programs that Ganz and his colleagues have conducted across multiple countries and movements. The methodology operationalizes several of the cognitive-science findings simultaneously: the personal narrative addresses construal-level distance, the collective narrative engages cultural-identity processing in service of mobilization, and the call to action addresses the action-mobilization component of communication.

Hahrie Han's *How Organizations Develop Activists: Civic Associations and Leadership in the 21st Century* (2014) and her subsequent *Prisms of the People: Power and Organizing in Twenty-First-Century America* (2021, with Elizabeth McKenna and Michelle Oyakawa), specify a distinction central to the project's framework: the distinction between organizing and mobilizing. Mobilizing operates by aggregating individuals to act on positions they already hold, through channels (mass communication, online petition, digital ad-targeting) that minimize the friction of participation. Organizing operates by building relationships among people through which positions, identities, and political capacities are developed over time, through channels (face-to-face conversation, membership organizations, collective action) that require substantial friction to participate. Han's empirical work demonstrates that the organizations that produce durable political power and durable issue-shift are those that engage in genuine organizing rather than relying on mobilizing infrastructure. The contemporary funder ecosystem, the digital-organizing technology stack, and the consultant infrastructure have all systematically biased toward mobilizing at the cost of the organizing that Han's empirical work identifies as the mechanism for durable power-building.

Jane McAlevey's *No Shortcuts: Organizing for Power in the New Gilded Age* (2016) constitutes the labor-side complement to Han's work. McAlevey's analysis specifies "deep organizing" — the methodology of organic-leader identification, structure-building through workplace mapping, and supermajority strike capacity-building — as distinct from the "advocacy" and "mobilizing" approaches that have dominated American labor since the mid-twentieth-century retrenchment. The contemporary labor revival — the Amazon Labor Union victory at JFK8 in 2022, the Starbucks Workers United organizing, the United Auto Workers Stand Up Strike of 2023, the Writers Guild of America and SAG-AFTRA strikes of 2023 — has deployed deep-organizing methodologies and provides a contemporary empirical record the project's framework engages.

The More in Common research program, established in 2017 with the *Hidden Tribes* report and continuing through subsequent reports including *The Perception Gap* (2019) and *Two Stories of Distrust in America* (2021), has developed empirical research on political polarization and on the methodologies that work across cultural-cognition divides. The Hidden Tribes typology organizes the American electorate into seven segments (Progressive Activists, Traditional Liberals, Passive Liberals, Politically Disengaged, Moderates, Traditional Conservatives, Devoted Conservatives), specifying that the active-political segments at the ideological extremes constitute approximately a third of the population, while the "exhausted majority" comprising the remaining segments is substantially less ideologically engaged and substantially more open to cross-cutting communication than the active-segment messaging tends to assume. Anat Shenker-Osorio's race-class narrative work, developed with the Race-Class Narrative Project beginning in 2017, specifies an approach to integrated economic and racial messaging that operates by explicitly naming both the economic interests and the racial divisions through which those interests are pursued, connecting the audience's lived economic experience to the broader political-economic structure, and articulating a multiracial coalition narrative that integrates rather than separates economic and racial concerns. George Marshall's *Don't Even Think About It: Why Our Brains Are Wired to Ignore Climate Change* (2014) constitutes the most systematic application of cognitive-science findings to climate communication specifically. The Yale Program on Climate Change Communication, directed by Anthony Leiserowitz, has developed the empirical research program that operationalizes these findings, including the Six Americas typology of climate-attitude segments and the regular tracking of climate-attitude shifts across segments.

### 3.4 Deliberative-democracy methodologies as institutional cultivation

The deliberative-democracy methodologies engaged in this section operate as institutional methodologies — methodologies for designing the institutions within which political decisions are made, with the institutional design itself contributing to decision quality and political legitimacy. The distinction matters because the project's framework does not view communication and institutional design as separable cultivation elements but as integrated components of the multi-decade infrastructure.

James Fishkin's Deliberative Polling methodology, developed across more than three decades of empirical research and articulated in *Democracy When the People Are Thinking: Revitalizing Our Politics through Public Deliberation* (2018) and earlier works including *The Voice of the People: Public Opinion and Democracy* (1997), specifies an institutional methodology with substantial empirical record. The methodology operates by recruiting a representative sample of the public through random selection and convening them for structured deliberation that includes balanced briefing materials, small-group discussion with trained moderators, plenary sessions with experts, and surveys before and after the deliberation that measure attitude change and learning. The methodology has been deployed in approximately one hundred Deliberative Polls across approximately thirty countries, with documented effects including attitude change toward better-informed positions, increases in policy-knowledge, and increased political efficacy of participants.

Hélène Landemore's *Open Democracy: Reinventing Popular Rule for the Twenty-First Century* (2020) articulates a more architecturally ambitious institutional theory in which the deliberative-democracy methodologies are integrated into a comprehensive institutional architecture combining lottocratic representation (selection by lot rather than only by election), enhanced accessibility of decision-making, transparency, and inclusion. David Van Reybrouck's *Against Elections: The Case for Democracy* (2016) articulates the historical and theoretical case for sortition as an alternative to electoral democracy.

The practical record of Citizens' Assemblies provides empirical material for assessing what sortition-based deliberative architectures can and cannot do. The Irish Citizens' Assemblies on the Eighth Amendment (abortion), conducted in 2016-2017 and producing recommendations the subsequent referendum substantially adopted, represent one of the most consequential deployments of sortition-based deliberation in contemporary politics. The Irish Citizens' Assembly on Climate (2022), the French Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat (engaged in Part Two), the UK Climate Assembly, the Oregon Citizens' Initiative Review (operating since 2010), and the approximately six hundred deliberative processes catalogued in the OECD's *Innovative Citizen Participation and New Democratic Institutions: Catching the Deliberative Wave* (2020) constitute a body of institutional experimentation whose empirical lessons the project's framework draws upon. The critique of the deliberative-democracy program, articulated in the academic literature including Cristina Lafont's *Democracy without Shortcuts* (2020), addresses several substantial concerns about scaling, capture, and legitimacy that the project's framework engages honestly.

### 3.5 Digital and information-environment cultivation

The contemporary period requires cultivation methodologies for digital and information-environment intervention that the cognitive-aware framework specifies as integral to the multi-decade project. The information environment within which political deliberation operates has been substantially restructured by the platform architecture of the contemporary period in ways that the institutional designs of prior periods did not anticipate.

Renée DiResta's work, including her writing through the Stanford Internet Observatory and *Invisible Rulers: The People Who Turn Lies into Reality* (2024), specifies how the contemporary platform architecture has produced specific dynamics — algorithmic amplification of high-engagement content, the construction of niche audiences through targeted-advertising infrastructure, the propagation of coordinated inauthentic behavior, the substitution of platform-native epistemic communities for institutional epistemic authority — that affect democratic deliberation in ways the standard theoretical frameworks did not anticipate. Yochai Benkler, Robert Faris, and Hal Roberts's *Network Propaganda: Manipulation, Disinformation, and Radicalization in American Politics* (2018) provides empirical analysis of how the right-wing media ecosystem operates as a structurally distinct epistemic community whose dynamics are not symmetric with mainstream media dynamics.

The empirical work on what specifically combats the cognitive features that disinformation exploits has produced several methodologies with documented effectiveness. Sander van der Linden's prebunking research, articulated in *Foolproof: Why Misinformation Infects Our Minds and How to Build Immunity* (2023), specifies that pre-exposure to weakened forms of manipulation techniques (the inoculation methodology) produces measurable resistance to subsequent manipulation attempts. The Sam Wineburg-led work on lateral reading at the Stanford History Education Group, articulated in Wineburg, Joel Breakstone, and Sarah McGrew's *Verified: How to Think Straight, Get Duped Less, and Make Better Decisions about What to Believe Online* (2022), specifies the specific reading methodologies that distinguish effective from ineffective approaches to evaluating online sources.

The platform-regulatory frameworks that have begun to operationalize specific responses to the platform-algorithm dynamics include the European Union's Digital Services Act, which entered into force in 2022 and became fully applicable in February 2024, establishing tiered obligations on platform operators including transparency requirements, user-control provisions, and risk-management requirements for the largest platforms. The Brazilian Marco Civil da Internet, the Australian News Media Bargaining Code, the various American state-level legislation, and the federal-level proposals including the Platform Accountability and Transparency Act constitute a body of regulatory experimentation whose institutional lessons the project's framework engages. The integration with the Taiwanese vTaiwan and Pol.is infrastructure that Part Two engaged operates at the level of operational instantiation; the digital-deliberation architecture supplies cultivation tools for cognitive-substrate work at scales beyond the individual reasoner. The American constitutional architecture, particularly the First Amendment and the Section 230 immunity framework of the Communications Decency Act, constrains the regulatory architecture available within American jurisdictional control more than the European architecture.

### 3.6 Integration with the prior layers

The integration of the cognitive-aware cultivation layer with the state-level innovation and international/Indigenous-governance layers is the central strategic claim of this document. The integration operates along specific causal pathways.

The state-level innovations Part One engaged produce demonstration cases that cognitive-aware cultivation can deploy as concrete narrative material. The construal-level theory the cognitive-science section engaged specifies that abstract messaging about climate, democratic erosion, and economic injustice systematically activates psychological-distance processing that reduces engagement. Concrete demonstration cases — the Klamath dam removal, the New York Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act's environmental-justice provisions, the Michigan citizen redistricting commission's maps, the Bears Ears coalition's co-management arrangement, the Welsh Future Generations Commissioner's office — supply the concrete, specific, present-tense narrative material that cognitive-aware communication can deploy in ways that the abstract framing systematically cannot. The state-level innovations also supply the "us" component of Ganz's public narrative: the demonstration that "this is what we are already doing in our state, our region, our community."

The international and Indigenous-governance frameworks Part Two engaged supply institutional vocabulary that cognitive-aware cultivation can translate into culturally accessible frames. The seven-generation decision rule, the legal personhood of natural systems, the doughnut framework, the *buen vivir* principle, the Ubuntu relational ontology, the Two-Eyed Seeing methodology, the grammar of animacy — these institutional vocabularies operate as frames in the Lakoff sense, organizing cognitive processing of substantive issues in ways the inherited Western vocabularies systematically constrain. The cognitive-aware cultivation layer's work is to translate these vocabularies into communication and organizing methodologies that engage American cultural-cognition processing in service of the project's framework.

The cognitive-aware cultivation layer in turn closes the political-force gap that allows state innovation to scale beyond demonstration-case status and that allows international norms to penetrate domestic political deliberation. State innovations remain demonstration cases until political pressure produces their adoption at higher scales. International norms remain comparative-law citations until domestic political mobilization translates them into domestic political force. The closing of these gaps is the work of cognitive-aware cultivation, operating through the communication, organizing, deliberative-institutional, adaptive-leadership, and digital-environment methodologies this Part has specified.

## CLOSING — THE THREE LAYERS AS ONE CULTIVATION STRATEGY

The integration thesis of this document is that the three operational layers — state-level legislative innovation as bioregional cultivation, international and Indigenous-governance engagement as multi-scale cultivation vocabulary, cognitive-aware political mobilization as cultivation of the cognitive substrate at the organism-and-organ-system scale — constitute one panarchy-cycle cultivation project rather than three separable initiatives, and that the discipline of holding them together as one strategy is itself the strategic question the contemporary moment poses.

The three layers operate at distinct spatial and temporal scales — sub-national bioregional, international and cross-civilizational, cognitive and individual — but the panarchy framework `V.01_CAS_Emergence_Adaptive_Governance_v6` carries specifies that distinct scales are not separable systems but components of one cross-scale dynamic. The lower scales accumulate the structural capacity, demonstration cases, and personnel networks that higher-scale reorganization draws upon. The higher scales generate the normative authority, institutional vocabulary, and political pressure that constrain and shape what is possible at lower scales. The cognitive and individual scale operates as the medium through which the structural and normative resources at other scales translate into political force, and through which political force translates back into structural and normative change. The architecture is not a hierarchy with one scale dominant and the others derivative; it is a cross-scale cultivation system in which each scale operates simultaneously as cause and effect of the others.

The causal pathways by which each layer reinforces the others are operationally specific. State-level innovation produces demonstration cases that international frameworks can translate into the American conversation: California's cap-and-trade program is not merely a state policy but a contribution to the institutional record that international actors can cite as evidence that climate policy is feasible at scale; New York's environmental-justice provisions are not merely state law but a translation of climate-justice norms developed in international negotiation into operational American legal architecture; the Klamath dam removal is not merely an ecological restoration project but a demonstration that the Free, Prior and Informed Consent norms developed in international Indigenous-rights jurisprudence can operate within American constitutional structure. International and Indigenous frameworks supply institutional vocabulary that state-level innovation can borrow and adapt within the constraints of the inherited federalism. The Te Awa Tupua co-governance structure provides architectural vocabulary that the Klamath restoration's tribal-state-federal coordination has implicitly drawn on and could explicitly reference. The Ecuadorian and Bolivian Rights of Nature frameworks provide architectural vocabulary that the White Earth Rights of Manoomin and Yurok Rights of the Klamath River have implicitly drawn on and could explicitly reference. The Welsh Future Generations Commissioner Act supplies institutional vocabulary that the right-to-food and Green Amendment movements at the state-constitutional level can engage. Cognitive-aware cultivation closes the political-force gap that allows state innovation to scale beyond demonstration-case status and that allows international norms to penetrate domestic political deliberation.

The integration thesis is contestable, and several specific failure modes can produce the appearance of integration without its substantive operation. The three layers can degenerate into three separable initiatives, each with its own funder networks, conferences, journals, and professionalized infrastructure, with no operational integration across layers. State-level innovation can degenerate into a substitute for federal action rather than infrastructure for it. International engagement can degenerate into foreign-policy adjacency rather than institutional-vocabulary cultivation. Cognitive-aware cultivation can degenerate into messaging consultancy rather than infrastructure-building. The cumulative pattern across all three failure modes is the substitution of professionalized activity within each layer for the strategic-political work of cross-layer integration. The strategic discipline is the explicit construction of cross-layer integration mechanisms — funder strategies, convenings, personnel pipelines, publications, training programs — that channel the accumulated capacity within each layer toward cross-layer cultivation deployment.

The forward implications coordinate across the project's continuing work. The eco-mimetic policy-making method that operationalizes the cultivation work as the operational method of governance is the subject of `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v6`. The e-democracy structural-public-check architecture that operationalizes the digital-deliberation infrastructure the cognitive-aware cultivation layer engages is the subject of `XII.03_E_Democracy_Structural_Public_Check_v6`. The intergenerational-rectification architecture that operationalizes the intergenerational cultivation the Welsh Future Generations Commissioner Act and the Norwegian Government Pension Fund Global instantiate is the subject of `XII.04_Intergenerational_Rectification_v6`. The currency-architecture that operationalizes the intergenerational cultivation at the financial-system scale is the subject of `XII.02_Currency_Architecture_v6`. The decision-rights cluster that operationalizes the cultivation work at the bounded-decision-rights-at-scale level is the subject of `XII.09_Foundational_Decision_Rights_v6` through `XII.12_Cognitive_Intergenerational_Decision_Rights_v6`. The cultivation-protocol synthesis integrating decision rights across the architecture is the subject of `XIII.01_Decision_Rights_Synthesis_v6`. The capstone reading of the full v6 corpus, including this document's specification of the three operational layers as one cultivation strategy, is the subject of `XIV.01_Capstone_Synthesis_v6`. The cultivation work proceeds.

The honest acknowledgment in closing is that the cultivation strategy this document has specified operates on a multi-decade time horizon that the urgency of the contemporary crises strains. The empirical severity of climate change, biodiversity collapse, democratic erosion, and the broader polycrisis demands faster transformation than the cultivation work can produce; the strategic-prescription's recognition that the political conditions for transformative legislation comparable to the historical waves are not present does not match the urgency the empirical severity would demand if urgency alone could produce political force. The cultivation work proceeds nonetheless because the alternatives — immediate legislative action without the political conditions to sustain it, constitutional convention under conditions that would produce regressive rather than progressive outcomes, exit strategies that abandon the federal architecture, after-the-collapse prefiguration that defers engagement with the inherited framework — underperform the cultivation work on grounds the analytical work has specified. The strategic posture is committed rather than optimistic. The commitment is to the cultivation work across the three operational layers, under the recognition that the work matters across a wide range of possible futures, that the alternative postures all underperform on grounds the analysis has specified, and that patient multi-decade cultivation has the historical record's support as the strategy through which transformative change has actually been produced.
