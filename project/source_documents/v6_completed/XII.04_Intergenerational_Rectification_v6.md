## PART SIX — THE HAUDENOSAUNEE SEVENTH-GENERATION CRITERION AS PRIMARY ARTICULATION AT CONSTITUTIONAL STANDING

### 6.1 The criterion at constitutional standing

The Haudenosaunee seventh-generation criterion operates as primary articulation of the intergenerational cultivation commitment at constitutional standing. The criterion's content, drawn from the Kaianere'kó:wa (the Great Law of Peace), is that decisions must consider their effects on those whose faces are not yet visible — those who will live seven generations hence. The criterion is not exhortation; it is procedural standard requiring that decision processes include explicit assessment of long-term effects, that the long-term effects be substantively rather than discountedly weighted, and that decisions whose long-term effects are inconsistent with the principles the Great Law specifies must be rejected.

### 6.2 The Haudenosaunee constitutional tradition

The Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace (Kaianere'kó:wa, also rendered Gayanashagowa) constitutes one of the longest-continuously-operating governance institutions in the world. The Confederacy of the Five Nations (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca) — joined by the Tuscarora in the early eighteenth century to form the Six Nations — operates the Great Law as constitutional framework sustained through Indigenous governance institutions despite centuries of colonial disruption. The Great Law's institutional design includes features the project's framework engages: the seven-generation decision rule, the consensus-with-clan-deliberation procedures, the Clan Mother veto and the structural locating of women in the selection and removal of male chiefs, the explicit provision for the removal of chiefs who fail to act consistently with the Great Law's principles, and the dedication of decision procedures to "peace, power, and righteousness" (skennen, kasastensera, kariwiio) as substantive standards.

### 6.3 Primary articulators

Engaging the Great Law on its own terms requires citing Haudenosaunee scholars and traditional teachers as primary sources. Oren Lyons (Onondaga) has articulated the Great Law's principles across decades of teaching, writing, and international advocacy, including his work at the United Nations on Indigenous rights. His co-authored *Exiled in the Land of the Free: Democracy, Indian Nations, and the U.S. Constitution* (1992) with John Mohawk and others establishes the historical case for the Great Law's influence on the American constitutional founders and articulates the principles that distinguish the Great Law from the constitutional architecture the founders eventually adopted. John Mohawk (Seneca) elaborated these arguments across his teaching and writing including *Utopian Legacies: A History of Conquest and Oppression in the Western World* (1999) and his subsequent work on Iroquois corn agriculture and ecological governance. Doug George-Kanentiio's *Iroquois Culture and Commentary* (2000) and *Iroquois on Fire: A Voice from the Mohawk Nation* (2006) constitute another body of primary-source material on contemporary Haudenosaunee governance. Robin Wall Kimmerer's (Citizen Potawatomi) *Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants* (2013) articulates the seven-generations cognitive substrate through the broader Indigenous-philosophical apparatus.

### 6.4 The criterion as foundational decision-rule

The seven-generation criterion operates as foundational decision-rule the architecture inherits, distinct from the Western discount-rate apparatus that dominates contemporary cost-benefit analysis. Standard cost-benefit analysis applies discount rates that systematically reduce the weight of future effects: a future cost or benefit fifty years out is weighted at perhaps twenty percent of an equivalent present cost or benefit, and a future effect a hundred and fifty years out — within the seven-generation horizon — is weighted at less than five percent. The seven-generation rule does not apply this discounting. It treats the welfare of those seven generations hence as substantively comparable to present welfare. The contrast with the discount-rate apparatus is structural rather than incremental, and the rule supplies foundational institutional vocabulary the contemporary architecture engages at constitutional standing.

### 6.5 Adaptations in non-Indigenous governance contexts

The rule has been adapted in various forms in non-Indigenous governance contexts: Sweden's intergenerational-equity provisions in environmental policy, the Welsh Well-being of Future Generations Act 2015 the Future Generations Commissioner architecture operationalizes (engaged at `XI.01_Three_Operational_Layers_v6`), the Norwegian Government Pension Fund Global's intergenerational-spending architecture (engaged at `XI.01_Three_Operational_Layers_v6`), various municipal long-term planning frameworks, and the contemporary international future-generations-institution architecture. The project's framework values these adaptations while specifying that the rule's authority and content derive from the Haudenosaunee tradition rather than from these adaptations.

The *Pachakuti* tradition supplies cultivation-condition vocabulary on the temporal architecture of historical-cosmological transformation, with implications for the institutional architecture of rectification work that engages cyclical rather than linear temporal cultivation.

Josef Estermann's *Filosofía Andina: Estudio Intercultural de la Sabiduría Autóctona Andina* (1998) supplies the analytical apparatus on Andean temporal cosmology, with particular attention to the relationship between *pacha* as integrated time-place-world and the broader Andean philosophical commitments to relational ontology. The contemporary Andean-scholarship tradition including the work of Aníbal Quijano on coloniality and the broader contemporary Andean philosophical apparatus extends the analytical apparatus.

The integration with the rectification commitment operates at primary-articulator standing. The *Pachakuti* architecture supplies temporal-architectural vocabulary on cyclical historical-cosmological transformation that the linear-progressive Western temporal frame does not generate from within itself, with the cultivation work of rectification operating within the cyclical temporal architecture the *Pachakuti* tradition specifies.

### 7.2 The Buddhist *anicca* tradition

The Buddhist *anicca* tradition on impermanence operates as foundational doctrine of the Buddhist tradition across multiple Buddhist schools and across more than twenty-five centuries of practice. The doctrine articulates that all conditioned phenomena are impermanent, that the content of the phenomenal world is constituted through dependent origination (*paṭiccasamuppāda*) rather than through substantial enduring entities, and that the appropriate engagement with phenomena requires the cultivation of awareness of impermanence as foundational orientation.

Walpola Rahula's *What the Buddha Taught* (1959), the foundational accessible articulation of Theravada Buddhist doctrine, supplies the foundational articulation of *anicca* for non-specialist readers. Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka critique of temporal substantiality, articulated in the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* and engaged through Jay L. Garfield's translation and commentary (*The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way*, 1995), supplies the philosophical extension into the analysis of temporal substantiality and the emptiness (*śūnyatā*) of independently-existing temporal-phenomenal entities. The contemporary Engaged Buddhist temporal-ethics corpus including Thich Nhat Hanh's writings on interbeing, David Loy's *A New Buddhist Path* (2015) on the contemporary articulation of Buddhist temporal-cosmological apparatus, and the broader contemporary Buddhist-temporal-ethics literature extends the apparatus.

The integration with the rectification commitment operates at primary-articulator standing. The *anicca* tradition supplies temporal-architectural vocabulary on the impermanence of phenomenal configurations including the contemporary configurations that produced the historical wrongs the rectification commitment engages, with the cultivation work operating within the temporal-cosmological framework the Buddhist tradition specifies. The implication is not quietistic acceptance — the Engaged Buddhist tradition has articulated at length that *anicca* requires active cultivation work rather than passive acceptance — but the cultivation of rectification work within a temporal-architectural framework distinct from the linear-progressive Western temporal frame.

### 7.3 The Daoist temporal-flow tradition

The Daoist temporal-flow tradition operates as articulation of temporal architecture distinct from both the linear-progressive Western frame and the cyclical-cosmological frame the Andean *Pachakuti* tradition articulates. The *Zhuangzi*, engaged through Brook Ziporyn's translation *Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings* (Hackett, 2009), supplies the foundational Daoist articulation of temporal-flow architecture, with the chapters on temporal-flow specifying that the appropriate engagement with temporal phenomena requires the cultivation of *ziran* (spontaneous-natural process) rather than the imposition of linear-progressive ordering. The *Dao De Jing*'s articulation of *wu wei* (non-interfering action) extends the apparatus, with the implication for cultivation work that the appropriate engagement with rectification requires the cultivation of conditions under which natural processes of restoration and rectification can operate rather than the imposition of programmatic interventions.
