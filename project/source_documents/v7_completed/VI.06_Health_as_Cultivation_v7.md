# VI.06 — Health as Cultivation

## Part VI — The Material Substrate

*New sixth-pass document. Working designation in the planning record: Document Y / "Health." This document is the sixth document of Part VI, the bodily-substrate document, following the physical-infrastructure documents and preceding `VI.07_Care_Kinship_Social_Reproduction_v7.md`.*

Health is the sixth of Part VI's material-substrate cultivation terrains, and it closes a coverage gap. The fifth-pass material-substrate Part architected the polity's relation to land, food and soil, energy, water, seed, materials, and shelter — every material substrate of the polity's life except the one in which all the others are finally registered: the living human body. A constitutional project that specifies the soil but not the body that the soil feeds, the shelter but not the health of those it shelters, has stopped one scale short of where its own logic runs. This document closes that gap. It specifies health as cultivation terrain — the body understood not as a repairable mechanism but as a biocultural formation cultivated within ecological, social, and economic conditions, across the bodily and ecological scales of the nested-scales reference frame.

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad — biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation — articulated in full in Document III.01 and not re-argued here. For health the triad bears with particular force. Biocultural governance is foregrounded: health is the paradigm case of a biocultural formation, because a body is, more plainly than any other substrate, both biological inheritance and the product of the cultural, social, and ecological conditions it is cultivated within, the two coupled at constitutive depth rather than at the level of correlation. Disturbance-adapted implementation bears because the biological-disturbance regime that Document II.02's biological-regime section diagnoses acts directly on the body, and the cultivation framing must hold pandemic and environmental-health disturbance within its temporal architecture. Eco-mimetic policy-making bears on the closing specification of the health architecture the constitutional order must enable. The document carries the Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition at primary-articulator standing, with the engineered-medical-system framing as the design-imposition tradition under diagnosis and the Euro-American medical and public-health canons engaged as partial recovery where they gesture past it.

The document assumes the constitutional ontology and nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, the biological-disturbance diagnostic of Document II.02's biological-regime section, and the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01. It rests on the Part VI peers as the substrate of health: Document VI.01 on land, Document VI.02 on food and soil, and Document VI.05 on the built environment each carry a determinant of health, and this document cites them rather than re-deriving their analyses. It does not specify the bounded decision rights or the cultivation protocol; those are the work of Part XII and Part XIII, which the document points forward to, coordinating in particular with Document XII.05 on neurodivergent and disability cultivation. The analytical work proceeds in four phases across nine threads: the establishment of health as cultivation terrain; the engineered-medical-system diagnostic; the engagement of the primary-articulator traditions — structural violence and the social determinants, health as membership and One Health, Indigenous health sovereignty, medical anthropology, the disability-justice critique, and the global-health and environmental-health literatures; and the closing cultivation specification.

# Part One — Health as Cultivation Terrain

## Thread A — The Bodily and Ecological Scales

The dominant framing of health is a framing of repair. Health, on this account, is the normal functioning of the body's mechanisms; ill health is the breakdown of a mechanism; and the production of health is the work of a medical system that diagnoses the breakdown and intervenes to repair it. The framing is encoded in the very institutions through which health is governed — the health system understood as a system of hospitals, clinics, practitioners, and procedures, its budget the health budget, its output measured in interventions delivered and conditions treated. The framing reads as plain description, but it is a particular and contestable construction, and the cultivation framing of this project rejects it on the same grounds it has rejected the parallel constructions of land, food, energy, and material flow: it mistakes the substrate for a mechanism, and the cultivation of a condition for the repair of a fault.

The cultivation framing begins from a different account. The body is not a mechanism but a biocultural formation: a biological organism, carrying an evolutionary and genetic inheritance, that is continuously cultivated — grown, sustained, and either flourishing or failing — within a particular set of ecological, social, and economic conditions. What a body becomes, across a life, is not the unfolding of a mechanism but the outcome of a cultivation: the food it was grown on, the air and water it was exposed to, the shelter it was held in, the work it performed, the stresses and securities of the life it lived, the meanings and relations within which it lived them. Health, on this account, is not the output of a repair system; it is the state of a body that has been well cultivated within good conditions, and the production of health is the cultivation of those conditions. Document III.01 specified the polity itself as a biocultural formation in which biological substrate and cultural plurality are coupled; the body is where that coupling is most directly visible, and health is the name of its flourishing.

Health, so understood, operates across the nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, and two of its scales are load-bearing for this document. At the bodily scale — the organism scale of the reference frame, with its organ-system sub-scale — health is the flourishing of the particular body, cultivated across the lifetime temporal horizon: the body grown from conception through childhood, sustained through working life, carried into age, each phase a cultivation with its own conditions. At the ecological scale, the body is coupled to the ecosystems it inhabits and depends on: the body is fed by a soil, breathes an air, drinks a water, is exposed to or sheltered from the chemical and biological environment of its place, and is therefore not a bounded mechanism but a permeable formation continuous with the ecological conditions around it. A health architecture adequate to the body must address both scales — the body cultivated, and the ecological conditions of its cultivation — and an architecture that addresses only the bodily scale, through the repair of bodies one at a time, has stopped, again, one scale short.

The cultivation framing must, at the outset, be distinguished from a corruption of itself, because the corruption is easy and consequential. To say that health is cultivated within conditions can slide into the claim that health is the individual's own project — that each person, through diet, exercise, and the management of personal "wellness," is the cultivator of their own health and therefore the party responsible for its failures. This wellness individualism is not the cultivation framing; it is the engineered-medical-system framing's mirror image, relocating responsibility from a repair system onto a self-managing consumer while leaving the conditions — the soil, the air, the work, the security, the distribution of power that the later threads of this document specify — entirely untouched. The cultivation framing specifies the cultivation of conditions, and the conditions of health are overwhelmingly collective, ecological, and structural rather than personal. A body cannot, by any exercise of personal discipline, cultivate itself out of a poisoned watershed, an unaffordable shelter, a food system that delivers calories without nourishment, or a life held under chronic structural stress. Health is cultivation terrain, and the terrain is a shared and unequally distributed one. The threads that follow specify it.

# Part Two — The Engineered-Medical-System Diagnostic

## Thread B — Health as the Output of a Repair System

The framing the cultivation account rejects has a name and a diagnostician. The framing is the engineered-medical-system framing: the understanding of health as the output of a medical system that intervenes on bodies as repairable mechanisms. And its diagnostician is Ivan Illich, whose *Medical Nemesis: The Expropriation of Health* (1976) remains the most thoroughgoing analysis of what that framing does.

Illich's analysis is built on the concept of iatrogenesis — harm caused by the healer — and its central move is to specify that iatrogenesis operates at three levels, each deeper and less visible than the last. Clinical iatrogenesis is the most familiar: the direct harm done by medical treatment itself — the adverse drug reactions, the surgical injuries, the hospital-acquired infections, the cascade of intervention in which each procedure generates the complication the next is required to address. Illich documented that this harm was, even in 1976, of a magnitude that made the medical system itself a significant cause of ill health. Social iatrogenesis is deeper: the medicalization of life — the steady expansion of medical jurisdiction over conditions and stages of life that had not been medical, so that birth, aging, unhappiness, deviance, and death are progressively redefined as medical events requiring medical management, and the medical system grows by converting more and more of ordinary human experience into demand for its services. Cultural iatrogenesis is the deepest and the one that gives Illich's analysis its constitutional weight: the destruction, by the medical system's monopoly, of the cultural and personal capacities through which persons and communities had borne pain, suffering, impairment, and death — the capacities that every human culture had cultivated, and that the medical system, in claiming sole authority over the body, expropriates. Illich's title names this: the expropriation of health. Health, in Illich's analysis, had been a capacity — the capacity of persons and communities to live, to cope, to suffer, and to die on their own terms and within their own meanings — and the engineered medical system, by monopolizing authority over the body, converts that capacity into a service, dependence on which is then the measure of access to health.

The diagnostic's analytical move is to ask what survives of the medical system under the cultivation framing and what does not. The move is not the rejection of medicine, and the document is precise about this, because Illich's analysis has been misread as such. What survives, and survives as a genuine and substantial good, is the medical system's capacity for acute and crisis care: the surgery that repairs a traumatic injury, the antibiotic that arrests a sepsis, the obstetric intervention that saves a life in a failing birth, the emergency medicine that carries a body through a crisis it could not otherwise survive. These are real goods, the cultivation framing affirms them without qualification, and a constitutional architecture must secure them. What does not survive is the framing: the construction of the body as a mechanism whose health is the medical system's output; the expansion of that construction, through social iatrogenesis, across ever more of human life; the expropriation, through cultural iatrogenesis, of the capacities of persons and communities to cultivate their own health and bear their own suffering; and, above all, the displacement that the framing accomplishes — the displacement of the conditions of health, which are ecological, social, and structural, by an ever-growing intervention on the failures of health, which are bodily and individual. The engineered-medical-system framing is, in the precise sense this project's diagnostics have used the term throughout, a design-imposition: it imposes on the body the model of a repairable machine, and it builds a system that grows by repairing machines while the conditions that break them are left, by the framing's own logic, outside the health system's concern. The threads that follow specify those conditions.

# Part Three — Structural Violence and the Social Determinants of Health

## Thread C — Health as Conditioned, and the Conditions as Unjustly Distributed

If the conditions of health are what the engineered-medical-system framing displaces, the traditions engaged in this thread are the ones that have specified those conditions most rigorously, and specified, with them, that the conditions are not neutrally distributed but track the distribution of power.

Paul Farmer's *Pathologies of Power: Health, Human Rights, and the New War on the Poor* (2003) supplies the sharper of the two analyses. Farmer, a physician and anthropologist whose work was grounded in the clinical realities of Haiti and other sites of extreme poverty, built his analysis on the concept of structural violence: the harm done not by an identifiable assailant but by the social, political, and economic structures that systematically place some populations in the path of disease, deprivation, and early death. Structural violence, in Farmer's specification, is violence because it injures and kills, and structural because the injury is the predictable product of an arrangement of power rather than the act of an individual. Its signature is epidemiological: the distribution of tuberculosis, of HIV, of maternal death, of the diseases of poverty does not fall randomly across a population but tracks, with grim precision, the distribution of poverty and powerlessness — the same people, in the same places, bearing the same diseases, generation after generation, because the structures that produce the diseases are stable. Farmer's analytical conclusion is that health is, at its foundation, a question of justice: that to treat the diseases of the poor as a technical problem of medical delivery, while leaving the structures that generate them intact, is to mistake the nature of the problem, and that the response structural violence demands is not only treatment but the dismantling of the structures — what Farmer, drawing on liberation theology, named a preferential option for the poor in the architecture of health.

The social-determinants-of-health tradition supplies the second analysis, in a register more institutional and less polemical but convergent in its finding. Margaret Whitehead's foundational work on health equity established a distinction the field has carried since: between health inequalities, which are simply differences in health across populations, and health inequities, which are the subset of those differences that are systematic, socially produced, and unjust — and therefore avoidable, and therefore a matter for political and constitutional remedy rather than for resigned acceptance. The World Health Organization's Commission on Social Determinants of Health carried the analysis to its most authoritative statement in its 2008 report *Closing the Gap in a Generation*, which specified, in a sentence that states the thread's whole finding, that social injustice is killing people on a grand scale. The Commission documented the social gradient in health — the finding that health tracks social position continuously, at every step of the ladder, not merely at its bottom — and specified the determinants that produce it: the conditions of daily life, which is to say income, housing, working conditions, education, food, and environment, and, beneath those, the inequitable distribution of power, money, and resources that shapes the conditions of daily life. The social-determinants framework is, in this document's terms, a clear instance of Western recovery: the public-health canon, pressing on its own evidence, arrived at the recognition that health is overwhelmingly produced upstream of the medical encounter, by conditions the medical system does not touch.

For the cultivation framing the two analyses together establish three things. Health is conditioned: it is the outcome of the conditions of daily life, not the output of a repair system. The conditions are unjustly distributed: the gradient and the structural-violence pattern are not natural facts but the health registration of an arrangement of power. And therefore a constitutional architecture that addresses only the medical system has addressed only the downstream failure — has built a repair shop at the bottom of the cliff, in Steingraber's image that Thread H will carry, while the conditions that push bodies off it are left to the structures Farmer names. The cultivation framing specifies the cultivation of the conditions, and the conditions are a matter of justice.

# Part Four — Health as Membership and the One Health Framework

## Thread D — Health as an Ecological-Scale Phenomenon

The structural-violence and social-determinants traditions of Thread C established that health is conditioned by the social arrangement. This thread carries the analysis to the ecological scale: it engages the framings that specify health as a property not of an isolated body but of a body's membership in a community and a place, and of the coupling of human health with the health of animals and ecosystems.

Wendell Berry's essay "Health Is Membership," published in *Another Turn of the Crank* (1995), supplies the framing in its most concentrated form. Berry begins from the word itself: health belongs, etymologically, to a family of words — heal, whole, wholesome, hale, and also holy — and the kinship is not accidental, because health, in its root sense, is wholeness. And wholeness, Berry argues, cannot be a property of an isolated individual, because no individual is whole in isolation; a person is whole only as a member — of a family, a community, a place, and the order of living things — and the health of the person is therefore inseparable from the health of the memberships the person lives within. Berry's sharpest formulation states the thread's claim directly: the community, understood in the fullest sense as a place together with all its creatures, is the smallest unit of health, and to speak of the health of an isolated individual is a contradiction in terms. The engineered medical system, by Berry's account, enacts the contradiction: it isolates the sick body, removes it to the institution, treats it as a case abstracted from the memberships that constitute it, and so addresses the body while severing the wholeness that is health.

The One Health framework supplies the same recognition in the register of contemporary science and international institutions. One Health is the framework — carried by the quadripartite collaboration of the Food and Agriculture Organization, the World Organisation for Animal Health, the World Health Organization, and the United Nations Environment Programme, and elaborated by the One Health High-Level Expert Panel — built on the recognition that human health, animal health, and the health of ecosystems are not separable domains but a single coupled system. The framework was driven into prominence by the epidemiology of zoonotic spillover — the emergence of new human pathogens from animal reservoirs, under ecological conditions that human activity creates — and by the recognition that antimicrobial resistance, food safety, and a range of other health questions cross the human-animal-environment boundary continuously. One Health is, like the social-determinants framework, an instance of Western recovery: the health sciences, pressing on their own evidence, arrived at the recognition that human health is an ecological-scale phenomenon, coupled to the health of the living systems the human body is a member of.

Berry's membership framing and the One Health framework together establish, for the cultivation framing, that health operates at the ecological scale of the nested-scales reference frame and not only at the bodily scale — that the health of a body is a function of the health of the land, the watershed, the animal and plant communities, and the human community it is a member of. This connects health directly to the biocultural-governance ontology of Document III.01, in which the polity is a biocultural formation coupling biological substrate and cultural plurality: health is the name, at the bodily and ecological scales, of that formation's flourishing, and a health architecture must be an architecture of memberships and ecological relations, not only of bodies.

# Part Five — Indigenous Health Sovereignty

## Thread E — Health, Land, Sovereignty, and the Continuity of a People

The Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition engaged in this thread stands at primary-articulator standing, and it is the document's load-bearing thread. The claim must be stated with the precision the plan's first What-to-Avoid demands, because the strongest risk this document faces is the absorption of the tradition into the Euro-American canons — the rendering of Indigenous health sovereignty as a variety of "traditional healing" or "cultural competence," a complementary practice or a sensitivity that Western public health manages alongside its real apparatus of social-determinants analysis and structural-violence theory. The relation is the reverse. The Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition is a primary articulator of what health is and of the sovereignty that health requires, and the Euro-American canons of the preceding threads are engaged, against it, as partial recovery. The thread is conducted under the multi-eyed-seeing discipline of Document VII.01, each strand of the tradition in its own register, and it does not collapse the distinct Indigenous nations and traditions into a single composite.

Lewis Mehl-Madrona (Lakota), in *Coyote Medicine* (1997) and the integrative-Indigenous-medicine corpus that followed it, articulates one strand. Mehl-Madrona, trained in Western medicine and working from a Lakota inheritance, specifies that healing is relational and narrative rather than only biomedical: that an illness is held within a person's story, their relations, and their community, and that healing proceeds through the work done on that story and within those relations — through ceremony, through the gathering of family and community, through the restoration of a person to right relation — and not only through the biomedical intervention on a disease entity. Mehl-Madrona's account is not a claim that biomedicine is useless; it is a claim that biomedicine, taken alone, addresses only one layer of what illness is and what healing requires, and that the relational and narrative layers, which the Indigenous tradition holds at the center, are not ornamental but constitutive of health.

The contemporary Indigenous health-sovereignty corpus articulates the thread's constitutional core. The Indigenous Health Adaptation to Climate Change framework, and the wider scholarship gathered in venues such as the *International Journal of Indigenous Health*, specify that Indigenous health is inseparable from land, from climate, and from sovereignty — that the determinants of Indigenous health include, at their foundation, colonization, land dispossession, and the presence or absence of self-determination, so that the "social determinants of health" of Thread C are, for Indigenous peoples, more precisely named the Indigenous determinants of health, with colonial dispossession as their root. The framework specifies that the health of an Indigenous people rises and falls with that people's continued relation to its land and its capacity to govern its own life — that health is, for a people whose land was taken and whose self-government was suppressed, a direct function of the recovery of both. And the work of Indigenous-controlled health institutions — the Native American Health Center, the Urban Indian Health Institute, and the wider movement of Indigenous-governed health provision — specifies health sovereignty as the operative constitutional claim: the principle that Indigenous nations must control the provision of Indigenous health, on their own terms, through their own institutions. The Urban Indian Health Institute's work specifies, further, that health sovereignty requires data sovereignty — that a people cannot govern its own health if the data about that health is collected, held, and interpreted by others, and that the control of health data is therefore itself a dimension of the sovereignty health requires.

What the Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition articulates that the Euro-American canons do not can now be stated as the thread's finding. The social-determinants framework of Thread C specifies that health is conditioned by social arrangements; the Indigenous tradition specifies that, for a colonized people, the determining arrangement is colonization itself, and that the remedy is therefore not the adjustment of determinants within the colonial frame but the recovery of sovereignty. The membership framing of Thread D specifies that health is membership in a community and a place; the Indigenous tradition specifies that the place is a specific land from which a specific people was dispossessed, and that health is inseparable from that land and from the continuity of that people upon it. And against the engineered-medical-system framing of Thread B, the Indigenous tradition specifies that healing is relational, narrative, and ceremonial, and that the expropriation of health that Illich named was, for Indigenous peoples, an explicit instrument of colonization — the suppression of Indigenous healing traditions and the imposition of the colonial medical system being one of the means by which a people's capacity to cultivate its own life was attacked. Health sovereignty — Indigenous control over Indigenous health, inseparable from land and self-determination — is, the tradition specifies, a constitutional question, and the cultivation framing carries it as such into the closing specification of Thread I.

# Part Six — Medical Anthropology and the Meaning of Illness

## Thread F — Disease, Illness, and the Biocultural Body

The medical-anthropology tradition engaged in this thread specifies a dimension of health that the structural and ecological threads do not reach: the dimension of meaning. Illness is not only a biological event and a structurally distributed one; it is a lived and culturally constituted experience, and the tradition engaged here specifies that the meaning of illness is part of its reality and therefore part of what a health architecture must address.

Arthur Kleinman's *The Illness Narratives: Suffering, Healing, and the Human Condition* (1988) supplies the tradition's foundational distinction: between disease and illness. Disease, in Kleinman's specification, is the entity as the biomedical practitioner constructs it — the pathophysiological process, the diagnosis, the case as the medical system sees and treats it. Illness is the lived experience of the condition — the suffering, the disruption, the meaning the condition carries for the person who lives it and for the family and community around them. Kleinman's analytical point is that the two are not the same, that the engineered medical system attends almost entirely to disease and treats illness as, at best, a soft accompaniment to the real biomedical work, and that this is a failure with consequences: a health practice that addresses the disease while ignoring the illness has not addressed what the patient actually suffers. Kleinman's call is for the practitioner to attend to the illness narrative — the patient's own account of what the condition is, what it means, and what they fear and hope — as itself a part of the clinical reality, and his concept of explanatory models specifies that practitioner and patient each bring a model of what the illness is, that the models often diverge, and that healing requires their negotiation rather than the simple imposition of the biomedical one.

Byron Good's *Medicine, Rationality, and Experience: An Anthropological Perspective* (1994) carries the analysis a level deeper, to medical knowledge itself. Good specifies that biomedical knowledge is not a neutral, culture-free description of the body that other cultures' accounts merely approximate; it is itself a culturally constituted formation, and the training of a physician is, among other things, a socialization into a particular way of perceiving, constructing, and narrating the body. Good's analysis denies biomedicine the position it claims for itself — the position of the one true account against which all others are measured — and specifies it instead as one powerful, historically and culturally located account among the accounts of the body. Margaret Lock's work carries the medical-anthropology tradition to its sharpest claim through the concept of local biologies. Lock's comparative research — on the experience and the physiology of aging and menopause across Japan and North America, and, in *Twice Dead: Organ Transplants and the Reinvention of Death* (2002), on the divergent reception of brain death and organ transplantation in Japan and North America — specifies that biology itself is not everywhere the same: that biological processes are shaped, in their physical unfolding and not only in their cultural interpretation, by the local material and cultural worlds in which bodies live, so that biology and culture are co-produced rather than layered one atop the other.

For the cultivation framing, the medical-anthropology tradition establishes that the body is biocultural in the strong sense — that its illnesses, its biologies, and the medical knowledge that describes them are all, at once, biological and cultural — and that a health architecture which addresses only disease, only the biomedical entity, misses illness, misses meaning, and misses the actual suffering and actual healing of actual people. This is the biocultural-governance ontology of Document III.01 registered at the scale of the body: the body, like the polity, is a formation in which the biological and the cultural are coupled at constitutive depth, and health is the cultivation of that coupled formation.

# Part Seven — The Disability-Justice Critique of the Medical Model

## Thread G — Against the Cure Imperative as a Measure of Citizenship

The disability-justice tradition engaged in this thread stands at primary-articulator standing, and the thread is load-bearing for a precise reason: the cultivation framing of health is vulnerable, more than at any other point, to a corruption that this tradition exists to name and refuse. The corruption is the slide from "health as cultivation" into a wellness norm — an ideal of the healthy, functional, cured body against which lives are measured, and against which disabled lives are found wanting. The disability-justice tradition holds the line, and the document holds it with them.

Alison Kafer, a disabled feminist scholar, in *Feminist, Queer, Crip* (2013) supplies the thread's central analysis. Kafer specifies a political and relational model of disability — distinct both from the medical model, which locates disability as a defect within the individual body to be corrected, and from a purely social model, which can imply that disability is only social barriers and nothing of the body. The political-relational model holds disability as a political category and a relation: a matter of how bodies and minds are positioned within an arrangement of power, access, and value. Kafer's sharpest contribution is her analysis of the curative imaginary and of futurity. She specifies that the dominant imagination of a desirable future is one in which disability has been eliminated — in which the future is imagined as healthy, and the disabled person figures only as a problem the future will have solved — and she names this for what it is: a refusal of disabled futurity, a vision of the good future from which disabled people are, by definition, absent. Against it Kafer articulates crip futurity: the insistence that desirable futures must be ones that include disability and disabled people, not as a residue to be cured away but as part of the human range a good future holds.

Eli Clare, a disabled writer working from his own bodily experience, in *Brilliant Imperfection: Grappling with Cure* (2017) supplies the thread's most careful engagement with the question of cure. Clare's analysis is precise and it refuses the easy positions. He does not claim that all desire for bodily change is illegitimate; he holds, with full honesty, the reality that a person may want relief from pain or from a body's difficulty. What Clare interrogates is the ideology of cure — the structure of meaning in which cure operates as the eradication of what is defined, by the very promise of cure, as broken, defective, and not properly belonging in the world. The cure imperative, in Clare's analysis, does not merely offer to relieve a condition; it defines the bodies it targets as wrong, and it makes the disappearance of those bodies the measure of success. Clare specifies the body as home — as the place a person lives, to be inhabited and not eradicated — and specifies the difference between a change a person seeks and a demand, made upon them by others, that they cease to exist in the form they take.

For the cultivation framing, the disability-justice tradition specifies a constraint that the document must carry into every later thread and into the closing specification. Health, as a cultivation aim, must never become a norm of the cured and functional body against which disabled lives are measured and found deficient — must never become, in the plan's phrase, a citizenship test, an implicit standard that the disabled body fails. The cure imperative is itself a design-imposition on bodies, of exactly the kind this document's diagnostic of Thread B named: it imposes on the body the model of a thing to be corrected to a specification. The cultivation framing's commitment is that the cultivation of conditions is owed to disabled people on their own terms — that good conditions, access, support, and the material substrate of a flourishing life are owed to every body in its actual form — and not as a way station toward a cured body that the architecture has decided every body ought to become. The disability-justice tradition is the primary articulator of this commitment, and the cultivation framing holds it as a non-negotiable line.

# Part Eight — Global Health, Pandemic Governance, and Environmental Health

## Thread H — Health as a Cross-Scale and Cross-Regime Phenomenon

This thread engages the literatures that specify health as a phenomenon crossing the scales of the nested reference frame and the regimes of the disturbance landscape — the global-health and health-justice literature, the pandemic-governance literature, and the environmental-health literature. It draws on the biological-disturbance diagnostic of Document II.02's biological-regime section and the food-system analysis of Document VI.02 as context, and does not re-derive their substance.

The global-health and health-justice literature specifies health as a question that crosses the scale of the polity. Solomon Benatar's work, including the volume *Global Health and Global Health Ethics* (2011) edited with Gillian Brock, specifies the ethical dimensions of a world in which health and the conditions of health are radically unequally distributed across nations, and in which the health of any population is increasingly bound to global ecological, economic, and political systems. Jonathan Mann's foundational work — his leadership of the early international response to HIV and his founding of the *Health and Human Rights* journal — specified the inextricable linkage of health and human rights: that violations of rights have health consequences, that health policies can themselves violate rights, and that health is therefore properly understood within a rights framework, a specification that Document I.01's foundational-normative inheritance carries.

The pandemic-governance literature is engaged critically. Jonathan Quick's *The End of Epidemics* (2018), together with the World Health Organization's pandemic-preparedness frameworks, specifies the preparedness program — the leadership, surveillance, prevention, and response architecture through which the emergence of epidemic disease is to be detected and contained. The document engages this program as a genuine contribution and engages it critically alongside the global-South critiques of pandemic governance, which specify that the preparedness paradigm has too often operated as a securitized framework serving the interests of wealthy nations, and that the actual conduct of pandemic response — the vaccine inequity, sometimes named vaccine apartheid, that marked the global distribution of COVID-19 vaccines, the protection of intellectual-property monopolies over the manufacturing capacity of the global South — reproduced the colonial patterns that the structural-violence analysis of Thread C and the Indigenous health-sovereignty analysis of Thread E both name. Pandemic governance, the thread specifies, is not a neutral technical architecture; it is a terrain on which the justice questions of the whole document are registered.

The environmental-health literature specifies health as a phenomenon crossing the regimes of the disturbance landscape. Rob Wallace's *Big Farms Make Big Flu* (2016) specifies the industrial food system as pandemic-generative architecture: the monocultural concentration of genetically uniform livestock, the destruction of the ecological buffers that once limited spillover, and the agribusiness political economy that drives both, together constitute, in Wallace's analysis, a structure that manufactures new influenzas and other pandemic pathogens — so that the biological-disturbance regime of Document II.02's biological-regime section is coupled, through the food system of Document VI.02, to the political-economic regime of Document II.02's political-economic-regime section. Sandra Steingraber's *Living Downstream* (1997) specifies the coupling of health with the physical-environmental disturbance regime. Steingraber, an ecologist and a cancer survivor, traced the environmental causes of cancer — the body's lifelong, involuntary exposure to the industrial chemicals dispersed through its air, water, and food — and supplied the literature its governing image: the parable of the village whose people are forever pulling drowning bodies from the river, until someone thinks to walk upstream and ask who is pushing them in. The image states the whole document's argument. The engineered-medical-system framing of Thread B is the village downstream, ever more elaborately equipped to pull bodies from the water; the cultivation framing is the walk upstream, to the conditions — the food system, the chemical environment, the structures of power — that are putting the bodies in. The thread establishes that health is cross-scale and cross-regime, and that a health architecture must be built upstream.

# Part Nine — The Cultivation Specification and the Bridge to the Decision-Rights Architecture

## Thread I — What the Constitutional Architecture Must Enable

The eight preceding threads have established the document's ground. Health is cultivation terrain, operating at the bodily and ecological scales, the body a biocultural formation cultivated within conditions rather than a mechanism to be repaired (Thread A). The engineered-medical-system framing is a design-imposition that constructs the body as a machine and displaces the conditions of health by intervention on its failures (Thread B). Health is conditioned, and the conditions are unjustly distributed along the lines of power that structural violence and the social gradient trace (Thread C). Health is membership, an ecological-scale phenomenon coupling human, animal, and ecosystem health (Thread D). The Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition articulates, at primary-articulator standing, that health is inseparable from land, sovereignty, and the continuity of a people, and that health sovereignty is a constitutional question (Thread E). The body and its illnesses are biocultural, and a health architecture that addresses only disease misses illness and meaning (Thread F). The disability-justice tradition specifies, at primary-articulator standing, that health must never become a cure norm against which disabled lives are measured (Thread G). And health is cross-scale and cross-regime, to be built upstream of the failures the medical system repairs (Thread H). This closing thread specifies what a constitutional architecture must enable for health to be cultivated rather than engineered, and bridges to the decision-rights architecture of Part XII and the synthesis of Part XIII.

The cultivation specification must be guarded, as the parallel specifications of the Part VI peers were, against a design-imposition slip-back. The document has argued against the engineered-medical-system framing; the symmetrical error would be to specify, in its place, a replacement health architecture engineered in advance — a new system, designed whole and imposed downward, that would have committed the design-imposition error over again with a different content. The cultivation framing does not design the health system. It specifies conditions — the conditions under which health is cultivated, plurally and locally, by the communities and peoples whose bodies and lives are at stake — and what follows is a specification of conditions in that precise sense.

The first condition is the upstream conditions of health as a constitutional commitment. The constitutional architecture must hold the conditions of health — the food, the water, the air, the shelter, the security, the work, and the just distribution of power that Threads C and H specified as the actual determinants of health — as a constitutional commitment, so that the production of health is understood as the cultivation of those conditions and not as the funding of a repair system. This is the structural meaning of building upstream, and it binds the health architecture to the Part VI peers: the land of Document VI.01, the food and soil of Document VI.02, and the built environment of Document VI.05 are, the cultivation framing specifies, health infrastructure, and the constitutional architecture must hold them as such.

The second condition is health sovereignty for Indigenous nations. The constitutional architecture must hold, as a standing commitment, the principle Thread E articulated: that Indigenous nations control the provision of Indigenous health, on their own terms and through their own institutions, including the data sovereignty that health sovereignty requires, and that this is inseparable from the land and self-determination commitments that Document VI.01 carried forward through the Land Back articulation. Health sovereignty is the health-domain form of the jurisdictional question that runs through the whole of Part VI.

The third condition is the refusal of the cure imperative as a measure of citizenship. The constitutional architecture must hold the disability-justice line of Thread G as a constitutional commitment: that the cultivation of conditions, access, and the material substrate of a flourishing life is owed to every body in its actual form, and that "health" must never operate as a norm of the cured and functional body that conditions a person's standing, their access, or their belonging. The cultivation of conditions is owed to disabled people on their own terms.

These conditions are what this document specifies and hands forward. Document XII.09's provisioning scale band, on provisioning decision rights, is where the right to health provisioning — the constitutional securing of the upstream conditions and of access to the genuine goods of acute and crisis care — is operationalized as a bounded decision right; this document names what XII.09's provisioning scale band must instantiate and does not perform the rights specification. Document XII.09, on foundational decision rights, is where the right not to be harmed at the biospheric scale — the right against the toxic and pandemic-generative exposures that Thread H specified — is carried at constitutional weight. Document XII.05, on the neurodivergent and disability cultivation, carries forward, in coordination with Thread G, the disability-justice commitments at the level of constitutional architecture, and this document's closing bridge is coordinated with it. Document XIII.01 integrates the health-cultivation conditions specified here with the conditions specified across the arc into the cultivation protocol, and Document XIV.01 reads this document, in the capstone, as the bodily-substrate document of Part VI — the document that specifies the health of the body in which the cultivation of land, food, energy, materials, and shelter is finally registered.

Health is cultivation terrain, not the output of a repair system. A constitutional order that treats health as the product of a medical system intervening on bodies as repairable mechanisms has mistaken the downstream repair of failures for the upstream cultivation of conditions, and the iatrogenic harm, the expropriated capacities, the unjustly distributed disease, and the bodies pushed into the river by structures the health system never looks at are the record of the mistake. The cultivation framing's alternative is the one this document has specified: health as the flourishing of a biocultural body cultivated within ecological, social, and economic conditions; the conditions held as a constitutional commitment; the Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition at primary-articulator standing; the disability-justice refusal of the cure norm held as a constitutional line; and the genuine goods of acute care secured without the framing that made them the whole of health. Of health, as of the land, the food, the energy, the materials, and the shelter the body depends on, the project's summary phrase holds: it is grown, not built — and a constitution adequate to the body cultivates the conditions of its flourishing.
