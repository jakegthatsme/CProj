The argument the document advances has three movements. ## PART ONE — WAR AS META-DISTURBANCE: THE COMPOUND DISTURBANCE ACROSS ALL FIVE REGIMES

### 1.1 War is not a regime; it is the compound

The five-regime disturbance diagnostic `II.02_Five_Regime_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v7` partitions the disturbance landscape into the cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, and political-economic regimes, and the cross-regime interactions diagnostic `II.08_Disturbance_Regime_Interactions_v7` specifies how disturbances in multiple regimes couple, amplify, and interfere with one another's recovery. War occupies a distinctive position in this architecture: it is not a sixth regime to be added to the five, but the compound disturbance that propagates across all five simultaneously and whose cross-regime amplification is the most severe the landscape contains. The document specifies war as meta-disturbance rather than as regime because the analytical apparatus `II.08` supplies — non-additivity, recovery interference, cross-scale amplification — is precisely the apparatus war requires, and because treating war as one regime among five would understate the compound character that makes it the terrain of catastrophic constitutional failure.

War propagates across the cultural regime through militarization — the reorganization of the cultural substrate around the anticipation, glorification, and normalization of organized violence, the production of the enemy as a cultural category, and the subordination of the cultural plurality the biocultural formation requires to the unity the war effort demands. It propagates across the biological regime through the direct biological devastation of war (the killing and maiming that is war's immediate work) and through the biological consequences of its weapons (chemical, biological, nuclear, and the long biological afterlife of contamination, unexploded ordnance, and ecosystem destruction). It propagates across the physical-environmental regime because organized military activity and warfare are among the largest single drivers of physical-environmental destruction — the carbon footprint of military establishments, the scorched-earth devastation of warfare, the long contamination of test sites and battlefields, and the diversion of the physical-environmental-restoration capacity the climate disturbance `IX.01_Constitutional_Climate_Redesign_v7` diagnoses into the war economy. It propagates across the cognitive-and-informational regime through the propaganda apparatus, the manufacture of consent, the wartime suspension of the deliberative substrate, and the epistemic capture that the security classification system institutionalizes. And it propagates across the political-economic regime through the war economy, the permanent armament industry, and the capture `II.03_Oligarchy_and_Dark_Money_v7` diagnoses operating through the military-industrial configuration.

### 1.2 The recovery-interference and cross-scale amplification of war

The recovery-interference finding `II.08` specifies — that recovery from one disturbance is impaired by the presence of others — operates at maximum severity in war, because war does not merely add a disturbance to each regime but actively destroys the recovery capacity of all five at once. The cultural plurality that would recover the cultural substrate is suppressed as disunity; the biological and physical-environmental restoration capacity is diverted into the war effort; the deliberative substrate that would recover the cognitive commons is suspended under wartime secrecy and emergency; and the political-economic capacity that would fund recovery is captured by the war economy. War is the disturbance that disables the architecture's recovery functions across the board, which is why constitutional orders that enter war frequently do not recover their pre-war constitutional condition: the emergency powers persist, the security apparatus persists, the militarized culture persists, and the war economy persists, each impairing the recovery of the others.

The cross-scale amplification `II.08` specifies operates with equal severity, and the modern war power amplifies it catastrophically. The nuclear weapon is the limiting case: a decision made at the scale of a single executive, in minutes, can extinguish the biocultural formation at the planetary scale across the full intergenerational horizon. No other disturbance in the landscape couples a decision at so small a scale to a consequence at so large a scale across so long a horizon, and the bounded-decision-rights architecture's organization by scale band (Thread D of the capstone `XIV.01_Capstone_Synthesis_v7`) is for exactly this reason most stressed by the war power: the war power is the decision whose scale-of-decision and scale-of-consequence are most radically mismatched, and the architecture's absorption of it must operate at the foundational band (biosphere-and-ecozone scale) even though the decision originates at the scale of an individual office.

## PART TWO — THE WESTERN CONSTITUTIONAL WAR-POWERS TRADITION AS RECENT AND PARTIAL RECOVERY

### 2.1 The declare-war clause and the founding-era restraint intuition

The Western constitutional war-powers tradition carries a genuine and important restraint intuition, and the document engages it as recent and partial recovery of what the older traditions carry rather than as the originating wisdom on the restraint of organized violence. The United States Constitution of 1787 vests the power to declare war in Congress (Article I, Section 8) while vesting the command of the armed forces in the President as Commander-in-Chief (Article II, Section 2), and the founding-era debate the Federalist and Anti-Federalist records `I.03_Federalist_AntiFederalist_Records_v7` carry registers a clear restraint intuition: the separation of the power to commit the polity to war from the power to conduct it was designed precisely so that the decision to make war would require the deliberation of the many rather than the will of the one. James Madison's records of the Constitutional Convention's deliberations register the deliberate substitution of "declare" for "make" in the war-powers clause, with the intent of leaving the executive the power to repel sudden attacks while reserving to the legislature the power to commit the nation to war. Madison's later correspondence states the restraint intuition directly: that the constitution supposes the executive to be the branch most prone to war and accordingly vested the war question in the legislature.

The restraint intuition is genuine, and the project carries it forward. But the Western tradition's recovery of the restraint principle is recent (the constitutional war-powers clause is two-and-a-half centuries old, against war-restraint traditions millennia older) and partial (the clause restrains the formal declaration of war while leaving the war power substantially intact through the mechanisms Part Two and Part Three develop). The document's engagement of the Western tradition is therefore the engagement of a partial recovery that the older traditions carry more deeply, and the partiality is the analytical point: the constitutional war-powers clause has failed, across the modern record, to restrain the war power it was designed to restrain.

### 2.2 The record of failure: the escape of the war power from its constraints

The modern constitutional record is in large part a record of the war power escaping the constraints the war-powers clause was designed to impose. The United States has formally declared war eleven times in five conflicts, the last in 1942, while conducting hundreds of military actions, interventions, and sustained conflicts without a declaration of war. Arthur Schlesinger Jr.'s *The Imperial Presidency* (1973) supplies the foundational analytical apparatus on the long accretion of war power in the executive — the transformation of the Commander-in-Chief clause from a command authority into a substantive war-making power, the displacement of the congressional declaration by the executive's claimed inherent authority, and the resulting concentration of the war decision in the office the founding restraint intuition identified as most prone to war. The War Powers Resolution of 1973, passed over President Nixon's veto in direct response to the undeclared escalation in Southeast Asia, attempted to recover the congressional war power by requiring the executive to seek congressional authorization for sustained hostilities and to terminate them absent authorization; the record since 1973 is a record of the Resolution's systematic circumvention, with executives of both parties treating its requirements as advisory and conducting sustained hostilities under claimed inherent authority, United Nations authorizations, and elastic readings of standing authorizations.

The Authorization for Use of Military Force of 2001 is the paradigm of the war power's escape from temporal constraint. Enacted in the week after the September 2001 attacks to authorize force against those responsible, it has been invoked across more than two decades, by four administrations, to justify military action against entities and in territories the enacting Congress did not contemplate, with no sunset, no required reauthorization, and no temporal limit. The 2001 AUMF is the constitutional record's clearest demonstration of the structural defect the document's prescriptive architecture addresses: an authorization of force without a sunset condition becomes a permanent grant of war power, detached from the deliberation that authorized it and from the circumstances that occasioned it. The sunset-or-adaptation condition of the four-element decision-rights format is the direct architectural response.

## PART THREE — THE STATES-OF-EXCEPTION PROBLEM: THE EMERGENCY THAT BECOMES PERMANENT

### 3.1 The exception and the norm

The deepest structural problem the war power poses to constitutional order is the problem of the exception: the constitutional provision for emergency — the suspension of ordinary constraints in the face of genuine threat — that becomes the permanent condition rather than the temporary departure. The document engages the states-of-exception literature as diagnostic apparatus while engaging its most prominent theorist critically, because the analytical insight and the normative commitment must be separated. Carl Schmitt's *Political Theology* (1922) supplied the twentieth century's sharpest analysis of the exception — the thesis that sovereignty is defined by the power to decide on the exception, that the one who decides when the ordinary legal order is suspended is the true sovereign, and that the exception reveals the limit of the norm — and the document engages this analysis as a diagnostic of how the war power and the emergency power dissolve constitutional constraint. But Schmitt's analysis was deployed in the service of the authoritarian dissolution of constitutional government, and the document engages the analytical insight against the normative commitment: the exception is precisely the danger the constitutional restraint of the war power must address, not the locus of a sovereignty to be celebrated. The diagnostic value of the exception analysis is its identification of the mechanism; the normative response is the architecture's refusal of the unbounded exception.

Giorgio Agamben's *State of Exception* (2003; English translation 2005) extends the diagnostic at the depth the document requires, specifying how the state of exception has become, in the modern security state, not the temporary suspension of the legal order in a genuine emergency but a permanent paradigm of government — a zone in which the distinction between the rule of law and its suspension is blurred, in which emergency measures enacted as temporary become the permanent infrastructure of governance, and in which the security apparatus operates in a space the ordinary constitutional constraints do not reach. Agamben's analysis of the way the temporary emergency becomes the permanent condition — the way emergency powers, once enacted, are not relinquished but accreted, so that each emergency leaves the baseline of executive and security power higher than before — supplies the precise diagnostic the sunset-or-adaptation condition addresses.

### 3.2 The ratchet and the sunset

The structural defect the exception analysis identifies is the ratchet: emergency powers accrete in one direction, expanding in each emergency and contracting little or not at all afterward, so that the constitutional baseline drifts steadily toward concentrated and unaccountable power. The ratchet is not a failure of any particular emergency response but a structural property of emergency power that lacks an automatic expiration: because relinquishing power requires an affirmative act against the interest of those who hold it, and because the next emergency always arrives before the relinquishment, the powers persist and accrete. The 2001 AUMF, the surveillance authorities enacted in its wake, and the permanent security apparatus they authorized are the contemporary instance of the ratchet the exception analysis predicts.

The architectural response is the sunset-or-adaptation condition applied without exception to every emergency power and every authorization of force: the automatic expiration of the grant unless affirmatively renewed through the full deliberative process that authorized it. The sunset condition inverts the ratchet's default: instead of requiring an affirmative act to relinquish accreted power (which the ratchet shows does not occur), it requires an affirmative act to retain power (which forces the recurring deliberation the founding restraint intuition demanded). The sunset is not a limitation on the polity's capacity to respond to genuine emergency — the architecture provides for the genuine emergency through the bounded grant — but a limitation on the conversion of the genuine emergency into the permanent exception. This is the single most important war-powers reform the modern constitutional record demands, and it is the architectural form of a wisdom the older war-restraint traditions carry: that the resort to organized violence and the suspension of ordinary restraint must be hard to begin, hard to continue, and structurally disposed to end.

## PART FOUR — THE SECURITY-STATE DIAGNOSTIC AND ITS COUPLING TO THE CAPTURE ARCHITECTURE

### 4.1 The permanent security apparatus

The war power in the modern state is not exercised episodically through discrete decisions to make war; it is institutionalized in a permanent security apparatus — the standing military establishment, the intelligence agencies, the surveillance infrastructure, and the armament industry — that persists across the intervals between wars and shapes the polity's constitutional character in the absence of any declared war. Dwight Eisenhower's farewell address of 1961 supplies, from within the Western tradition and from a figure of unimpeachable military authority, the foundational warning: that the conjunction of an immense military establishment and a large arms industry was new in the American experience, that its total influence — economic, political, even spiritual — was felt in every city and statehouse, and that the potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power existed and would persist, requiring an alert and knowledgeable citizenry to guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence by the military-industrial complex. The military-industrial-complex diagnostic is the Western tradition's clearest recognition that the security apparatus built to protect the constitutional order becomes, through its permanence and its scale, a principal threat to it.

The security-state diagnostic the document develops extends the military-industrial-complex warning across the dimensions the contemporary apparatus occupies: the surveillance state the cognitive-and-informational regime section of `II.02` engages (the surveillance infrastructure that the security justification authorizes and that the technological-critique response of `XII.09`'s public-check band is scale-matched to check); the classification system that removes vast domains of state action from the deliberative and epistemic scrutiny the cognitive commons requires; and the carceral and policing apparatus that the harm-response commitment `XII.07_Harm_Response_Accountability_Repair_v7` engages as the domestic face of the security state's organization of the polity around the anticipation and management of violence.

### 4.2 The security-state—capture coupling

The security state couples to the capture architecture `II.03_Oligarchy_and_Dark_Money_v7` diagnoses through the war economy, and the coupling is among the most severe cross-regime couplings the landscape contains. The armament industry is a concentrated political-economic interest with a structural stake in the continuation of the conditions that justify its scale — the permanent anticipation of threat, the recurring resort to force, and the maintenance of the security apparatus — and it deploys the capture mechanisms `II.03` diagnoses (the financing of political campaigns, the revolving door between the security apparatus and the armament industry, the cultivation of the cognitive-informational substrate toward the anticipation of threat) to maintain those conditions. The security-state—capture coupling is the mechanism by which the war power, nominally an instrument of the polity's defense, becomes an instrument of the concentrated political-economic interest that profits from its exercise, and the architecture's absorption of the war power must operate jointly with its absorption of the capture architecture: the public-trust threshold for private organizations `XII.09`'s public-check band specifies applies with particular force to the armament industry, and the political-economic regime's absorption (Thread D of the capstone) extends to the war economy.

## PART FIVE — THE HAUDENOSAUNEE WAR-AND-CONDOLENCE ARCHITECTURE AT PRIMARY-ARTICULATOR STANDING

### 5.1 The Great Law of Peace as a constitution founded on the ending of war

The Kaianerekowa, the Great Law of Peace of the Haudenosaunee (the Six Nations Iroquois Confederacy), is among the foundational normative texts the multi-traditional canonical commitment carries at co-equal weight (`I.01_Declaration_as_Foundation_v7`), and it stands at primary-articulator standing on the constitutional restraint of war for a reason no Western constitutional text can match: it is a constitution founded explicitly on the ending of war among the nations it federates. The Peacemaker's founding of the Confederacy, as the oral constitutional tradition transmits it, was the transformation of nations locked in cycles of retaliatory violence into a federation bound by a law of peace — the burying of the weapons of war beneath the Tree of Peace, the great white pine whose roots extend in the four directions so that any nation seeking peace may trace them to their source. The Great Law's founding act is the constitutional renunciation of war among the federated nations, and its architecture is organized around the maintenance of that peace across generations. This is constitutional war-restraint not as a clause within a document devoted to other purposes but as the founding purpose of the constitutional order itself.

### 5.2 The condolence architecture and the interruption of the retaliation cycle

The Great Law's most profound contribution to the constitutional restraint of organized violence is the condolence architecture — the constitutional and ceremonial apparatus by which the cycle of grief, retaliation, and renewed grief that drives the war cycle is interrupted and transformed. The Condolence Ceremony, by which the Confederacy responds to the death of a leader and by which grief is addressed before it can become the occasion of renewed violence, embodies a constitutional insight the document carries at primary-articulator standing: that war is driven by the unaddressed grief and the demand for retaliation that violence generates, and that the constitutional interruption of the retaliation cycle requires an architecture for the addressing of grief and the restoration of relationship rather than merely a prohibition on violence. The condolence architecture is the constitutional form of the recognition that the recovery-interference of war (Part One) is overcome not by ending the fighting alone but by addressing the grief and restoring the relationships the fighting destroyed — a recognition the harm-response commitment `XII.07` carries forward in its displacement of the retributive-carceral architecture by a restorative one.

### 5.3 The role of the clan mothers and the deliberative restraint of the war decision

The Great Law distributes the war decision across a deliberative architecture designed to make the resort to war difficult, and it vests in the clan mothers a constitutional role in the restraint of war-making that the document carries at primary-articulator standing: the clan mothers nominate and may remove the leaders, and the constitutional culture vests in those who bear and raise the people a particular authority over the decision that would send them to die. The gendered distribution of the war power — the location of a restraining authority in those whose relation to the continuation of the people is most direct — is a constitutional-design insight the Western tradition has no equivalent of, and the document carries it not as a curiosity but as a primary articulation of how a constitutional order distributes the war decision so as to restrain it. The Great Law's requirement of deliberation and substantial consensus across the federated nations before collective action, the difficulty it deliberately imposes on the resort to force, and the orientation of the whole architecture toward the maintenance of peace across generations together constitute a constitutional war-restraint architecture older, deeper, and more thoroughly integrated than the Western war-powers clause that recovers a fragment of it.

## PART SIX — THE DAOIST AND BUDDHIST RENUNCIATION TRADITIONS

### 6.1 The Daoist reluctance: weapons as instruments of ill omen

The Daoist tradition carries at primary-articulator standing a constitutional wisdom about the restraint of organized violence that the document engages at analytical depth rather than at the list level the seventh-pass capstone (Thread F) flagged as the asymmetry to be deepened. The *Daodejing* (the *Tao Te Ching*, attributed to Laozi, ca. fourth century BCE) states the war-restraint principle directly in its thirty-first chapter: that weapons are instruments of ill omen, abhorred by all things, and that the one who follows the Way does not dwell with them; that the cultivated person honors the left (the side of life) in ordinary times and the right (the side of mourning) only in war, because the conduct of war is to be approached as one approaches a funeral; and that a victory in war is not an occasion for celebration but for the rites of mourning, because to celebrate victory is to delight in the killing of human beings, and the one who delights in killing cannot achieve their purpose in the world. The Daoist contribution is the principle that the resort to arms, even when unavoidable, is to be approached with reluctance, conducted with restraint, and concluded with mourning rather than triumph — a disposition toward organized violence that the modern militarized culture, with its celebration of victory and its glorification of force, exactly inverts.

The thirtieth chapter extends the principle to the conduct of the ruler: that one who assists a ruler by the Way does not seek to dominate the world through force of arms, because the resort to force rebounds — where armies camp, brambles grow; after great wars, bad harvests follow — and that the cultivated commander achieves the necessary result and stops, not daring to use the result to dominate, achieving the result without pride, without boasting, without arrogance, achieving it only because there was no choice, achieving it without domination. The Daoist *wu wei* (non-coercive action) and *ziran* (spontaneous naturalness) that `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v7` carries as method articulators bear directly on the war power: the Daoist constitutional disposition is toward the non-coercive maintenance of order and the deep reluctance to resort to the coercive force that war embodies, and the eco-mimetic method's preference for the cultivation of conditions over the imposition of force finds its sharpest application in the restraint of the war power.

### 6.2 The Buddhist analysis: the renunciation of violence and the karmic propagation of harm

The Buddhist tradition carries at primary-articulator standing an analysis of violence and its renunciation that the document engages at the analytical depth the cognitive-and-informational regime section of `II.02` engages the Abhidhamma consciousness analysis. The first of the five precepts is the undertaking to abstain from the taking of life, and the Buddhist analysis of violence locates its root in the three poisons — greed, hatred, and delusion — whose operation the war cycle embodies and amplifies. The analysis of dependent origination (*paṭiccasamuppāda*) that `II.08` carries through Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* and Joanna Macy's *Mutual Causality in Buddhism and General Systems Theory* bears directly on the war cycle: violence arises in dependence on the conditions that produce it — the grief and the demand for retaliation the Haudenosaunee condolence architecture addresses, the greed and hatred the Buddhist analysis names — and the interruption of the war cycle requires the addressing of those conditions rather than the addition of further violence, which propagates the conditions it claims to resolve. The Buddhist contribution to the constitutional restraint of war is the analysis of violence as karmically self-propagating — as a disturbance that generates the conditions of its own continuation — and the corresponding insight that the cultivation of peace requires the cultivation of the conditions from which violence does not arise, an insight the document carries forward into the non-militarism commitment of Part Ten.

## PART SEVEN — THE ASHOKAN RENUNCIATION AND THE CONFUCIAN REMONSTRANCE TRADITION

### 7.1 Ashoka and the constitutional renunciation of conquest

The Edicts of Ashoka (the rock and pillar edicts of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka, third century BCE) supply the document's clearest historical instance of the constitutional renunciation of war by a sovereign at the height of the capacity to wage it, and the document carries it at primary-articulator standing as a constitutional precedent the Western tradition has no equivalent of. The thirteenth major rock edict records Ashoka's renunciation of war following the conquest of Kalinga, in which the slaughter, death, and deportation of the conquered occasioned in the emperor remorse rather than triumph, and led to his adoption of conquest by *dhamma* (righteousness) in place of conquest by arms as the policy of the state. The Ashokan renunciation is a constitutional act of the first significance: a sovereign with the demonstrated capacity for military conquest renouncing it as state policy and reorganizing the purposes of the state around the welfare of its subjects and the practice of non-violence (*ahiṃsā*) rather than around the extension of dominion by force. The Ashokan precedent stands at primary-articulator standing on the proposition the non-militarism commitment of Part Ten develops: that the renunciation of war as an instrument of state policy is not a utopian aspiration but a historical constitutional act, undertaken by a sovereign at the height of military capacity, and recorded as the founding orientation of a reorganized state.

### 7.2 The Confucian remonstrance and the just-war restraint

The Confucian tradition carries at primary-articulator standing — at the analytical depth the capstone Thread F flagged for deepening — a constitutional wisdom about the restraint of the ruler's resort to war that operates through the remonstrance tradition `XII.01` and the broader Confucian canon carry. Mencius (Mengzi, fourth century BCE) develops the sharpest Confucian critique of war for dominion: the condemnation of the rulers who waged war for territory as those who lead the land to devour human flesh, the doctrine that the true king does not need to make war because the people of all states will turn to a ruler who governs benevolently, and the corresponding analysis that the resort to war for conquest is both a moral failure and a practical one, because it forfeits the popular allegiance on which durable rule depends. The Confucian *li* (ritual propriety) that the project carries as method articulator bears on the war power through the ritual restraint of the resort to force and the location of the ruler's legitimacy in benevolent governance rather than in military success. The Confucian remonstrance tradition — the duty of the minister to remonstrate against the ruler's errors, including the error of unjust war — supplies a constitutional mechanism of restraint internal to the structure of governance: the institutionalized obligation to speak against the resort to war, which the deliberative-restraint architecture of Part Eleven carries forward.

## PART EIGHT — THE ISLAMIC JURIDICAL RESTRAINT TRADITION

### 8.1 The juridical constraints on the resort to and conduct of war

The Islamic juridical tradition carries at primary-articulator standing — at the analytical depth the capstone Thread F flagged for deepening across the Eastern and Islamic traditions — a developed body of law on the restraint of both the resort to war and its conduct, and the document engages it as a primary articulation of the constitutional restraint of organized violence rather than as comparative supplement. The Islamic juridical tradition develops detailed constraints on the conduct of war that anticipate by centuries the principles the modern law of armed conflict recovers: the prohibition on the killing of non-combatants — women, children, the aged, the infirm, and those not engaged in fighting; the prohibition on the destruction of crops, livestock, and the means of sustenance; the prohibition on the destruction of places of worship and the killing of those devoted to religious life; the requirement of proportionality and the prohibition on mutilation and treachery; and the obligation to honor agreements and to accept offers of peace. The first caliph's instructions to his armies, as the juridical tradition transmits them, codify the non-combatant immunity and the prohibition on the destruction of the means of life as binding constraints on the conduct of war.

### 8.2 The restraint orientation and the priority of peace

The Islamic juridical tradition carries, alongside the constraints on the conduct of war, a restraint orientation toward its resort: the doctrine that the resort to war is bounded by just cause and right authority, that aggression is prohibited and the resort to force restrained to the defense against aggression, and that the obligation to incline toward peace where the adversary inclines toward it is a binding constraint on the continuation of war. The *waqf* tradition `XIV.01` and `XII.01` carry as an Islamic constitutional institution — the dedication of resources in perpetual trust for the common welfare — bears on the document's political-economic dimension: the dedication of the polity's resources to the cultivation of the common welfare rather than to the war economy is the political-economic form of the restraint orientation, and the security-state—capture coupling of Part Four is the contemporary inversion of the *waqf* principle, with the polity's resources captured by the war economy rather than dedicated in trust to the common welfare. The Islamic juridical restraint tradition stands at primary-articulator standing on the proposition that the resort to and conduct of organized violence are matters of binding law rather than of unconstrained sovereign discretion — the proposition the states-of-exception problem of Part Three identifies the modern security state as having abandoned.

## PART NINE — THE AFRICAN RESTORATIVE TRADITIONS AND THE MODERN WAR-ABOLITION TRADITION

### 9.1 The African palaver and restorative traditions

The African traditions carry at primary-articulator standing — addressing in part the broader non-Western deepening this document and the parallel revisions undertake — a constitutional wisdom about the resolution of conflict without the resort to destructive violence that the document engages through the palaver tradition and the restorative-justice orientation the *ubuntu* relational ontology grounds. The palaver tradition — the institutionalized practice of extended deliberation, the airing of grievance, and the negotiation of resolution through talk rather than force — embodies a constitutional preference for the deliberative resolution of conflict that the war-restraint architecture of Part Eleven carries forward. The *ubuntu* relational ontology (the principle that a person is a person through other persons, that the self is constituted in relation rather than prior to it) grounds a restorative rather than a retributive orientation toward harm and conflict: where the retributive orientation answers violence with violence and so propagates the war cycle the Buddhist and Haudenosaunee analyses diagnose, the *ubuntu*-grounded restorative orientation answers harm with the restoration of the relationship harm has damaged, an orientation the harm-response commitment `XII.07` carries at primary-articulator standing and that the document extends to the war power: the restoration of relationship across the divisions war creates, rather than the perpetuation of enmity, as the constitutional orientation toward the aftermath of conflict.

### 9.2 The Gandhian and modern war-abolition traditions

The Gandhian tradition of *ahiṃsā* (non-violence) and *satyagraha* (truth-force, the disciplined practice of non-violent resistance) carries at primary-articulator standing the modern articulation of the proposition that organized violence is not the only or the most effective instrument of political transformation, and that the disciplined practice of non-violence is both a moral commitment and a political method. Mohandas Gandhi's development of *satyagraha* as a method of political struggle that refuses the resort to violence even against violent oppression supplies the document's bridge between the ancient renunciation traditions (Ashokan, Buddhist, Daoist) and the modern abolition-of-war tradition. The modern war-abolition tradition — the peace-research canon, the analysis of war as an institution that can be abolished as other institutions (slavery, dueling) have been abolished rather than as a permanent feature of the human condition, and the feminist anti-militarist analysis that links the militarized state to the broader structures of domination — carries forward the proposition the non-militarism commitment of Part Ten develops: that the reorganization of the polity away from the anticipation and conduct of organized violence is a constitutional project rather than a utopian fantasy. The document engages these traditions as the modern recovery and articulation of a wisdom the older traditions carry, consistent with the arc's general structure in which the Western and modern canons are recent and partial recovery of what the Indigenous and Eastern traditions have long carried.

## PART TEN — THE NON-MILITARISM COMMITMENT AND THE DEMILITARIZATION QUESTION

### 10.1 Non-militarism as constitutional commitment

The non-militarism commitment the document develops is not pacifism in the sense of the absolute refusal of all defensive force — the architecture provides for the genuine defense against aggression through the bounded grant, and the war-restraint traditions the document carries are traditions of restraint, reluctance, and renunciation rather than uniformly of absolute non-resistance. The non-militarism commitment is the constitutional reorganization of the polity away from the militarized condition Part Four diagnoses: away from the permanent security apparatus that becomes a principal threat to the constitutional order, away from the war economy that captures the polity's resources and couples to the capture architecture, away from the militarized culture that subordinates the cultural plurality the biocultural formation requires, and away from the states-of-exception ratchet that converts each emergency into a permanent expansion of concentrated power. Non-militarism is the constitutional commitment to the cultivation of the conditions from which organized violence does not arise — the Buddhist insight carried into constitutional form — and to the maintenance of the polity's defensive capacity within bounds that prevent its transformation into the permanent militarized condition.

Several constitutional orders supply partial precedents the document engages as recent and partial recovery: the constitutional renunciation of war as a sovereign right in the post-war Japanese constitution's ninth article; the constitutional commitments to neutrality and demilitarization various polities maintain; and the abolition of the standing military in the constitutional order of Costa Rica, which redirected the resources of the military establishment toward education and health and supplies a contemporary instance of the Ashokan reorganization of state purposes away from military dominion and toward the common welfare. These precedents are partial and contested, and the document engages them as instances rather than as models, consistent with the cultivation discipline's refusal of the imported blueprint.

### 10.2 The demilitarization question and the disturbance-adapted caution

The demilitarization question — how a polity reorganizes away from the militarized condition without exposing itself to the aggression the security apparatus was built to deter — is a genuine question the document does not resolve by fiat, and the disturbance-adapted orientation supplies the appropriate caution: the reorganization away from militarism is itself a cultivation undertaken across time and under a disturbance landscape that includes the aggression of other militarized polities, and the architecture must absorb the disturbance the demilitarization process itself encounters. The eco-mimetic method's preference for the cultivation of conditions over the imposition of force, the disturbance-adapted orientation's recognition that the architecture operates under a standing disturbance landscape rather than in a presumed peace, and the cultivation discipline's refusal of the imposed blueprint together specify the demilitarization not as a single act of disarmament but as a cultivated reorganization across the intergenerational horizon, conducted in coordination with the broader cultivation of the conditions — the addressing of grievance through the deliberative and restorative architectures, the dedication of resources to the common welfare rather than the war economy, the cultivation of the cultural plurality against the militarized unity — from which organized violence does not arise. The document carries the demilitarization question forward as cultivation work rather than resolving it, consistent with the arc's general orientation.

## PART ELEVEN — THE WAR-POWERS-AND-SECURITY-STATE CULTIVATION CONDITIONS THE ARCHITECTURE MUST ENABLE

### 11.1 The bounded decision rights the architecture must specify

The document specifies what war-powers-and-security-state architecture the constitutional order must enable, leaving the integrated decision-rights specification to `XII.09_Bounded_Decision_Rights_Across_Nested_Scales_v7` and the synthesis to `XIII.01_Decision_Rights_Synthesis_v7`. The cultivation conditions operate across the scale bands the bounded-decision-rights architecture specifies.

At the foundational band (biosphere-and-ecozone scale), the architecture must specify the resort to war as a bounded decision right whose scale-of-decision is matched to its scale-of-consequence: because the war power, and pre-eminently the nuclear war power, couples a decision at the scale of a single office to a consequence at the planetary scale across the full intergenerational horizon (Part One), the foundational band must vest the resort-to-war decision in the deliberation of the many at the scale the consequence reaches, rather than in the executive at the scale the founding restraint intuition identified as most prone to war (Part Two). The four-element format applies: the scope is the resort to organized force; the scale is biosphere-and-ecozone, because the war power's consequence reaches that scale; the feedback condition is the deliberative authorization and the diverse-epistemic-standing scrutiny that the wartime secrecy apparatus must not be permitted to suspend; and the sunset-or-adaptation condition is the automatic expiration of every authorization of force and every emergency power, the single most important war-powers reform the modern record demands (Part Three).

At the public-check band (ecosystem-and-habitat scale), the architecture must specify the security apparatus and the war economy as terrain subject to the scale-matched public check: the technological-critique response on weapons systems `XII.09`'s public-check band carries (the check on the development and deployment of weapons systems, surveillance technology, and the autonomous-weapons frontier before deployment forecloses deliberation), the public-trust threshold for private organizations applied to the armament industry (Part Four), and the deliberative and epistemic scrutiny of the classification system and the surveillance apparatus that the cognitive-and-informational regime's absorption requires.

At the cognitive-and-intergenerational band (organism-and-organ-system-and-temporal scale), the architecture must carry the war power's intergenerational consequence through the seventh-generation criterion `XII.09`'s intergenerational band carries: the decision to make war, to maintain the security apparatus, and to organize the polity around the anticipation of violence binds the generations who inherit its consequences — the biological and physical-environmental afterlife of war and its weapons (Part One), the militarized culture and the war economy transmitted across generations, and the intergenerational harm the rectification commitment `XII.04_Intergenerational_Rectification_v7` addresses. The intergenerational criterion is the architectural form of the war-restraint traditions' orientation toward the maintenance of peace across generations (the Haudenosaunee), the karmic propagation of violence (the Buddhist), and the reorganization of state purposes toward the welfare of the generations (the Ashokan).

### 11.2 The deliberative-and-restorative restraint architecture

Beyond the bounded decision rights, the architecture must enable the deliberative-and-restorative restraint architecture the war-restraint traditions carry: the institutionalized difficulty of the resort to force the Haudenosaunee deliberative architecture and the Confucian remonstrance tradition supply (Part Five, Part Seven); the addressing of grievance and the restoration of relationship the Haudenosaunee condolence architecture, the *ubuntu* restorative orientation, and the harm-response commitment `XII.07` carry (Part Five, Part Nine); the dedication of the polity's resources to the common welfare rather than the war economy the Islamic *waqf* principle and the Costa Rican and Ashokan reorganizations supply (Part Eight, Part Ten); and the cultivation of the conditions from which organized violence does not arise the Buddhist and Gandhian traditions carry (Part Six, Part Nine). The restraint architecture is not a prohibition that the first emergency overrides but a cultivated disposition — grown rather than built — toward the reluctance, restraint, and renunciation of organized violence that the war-restraint traditions carry at primary-articulator standing and that the Western war-powers tradition recovers only partially.

### 11.3 What survives and what does not under the war-restraint framing

What survives the war-restraint framing is the genuine defensive capacity the disturbance-adapted orientation requires under a standing disturbance landscape that includes the aggression of militarized polities (Part Ten), bounded by the four-element decision-rights format and the deliberative-and-restorative restraint architecture. What does not survive is the war power escaped from its constraints (Part Two), the emergency converted into the permanent exception (Part Three), the permanent security apparatus that becomes a principal threat to the constitutional order (Part Four), the war economy captured by the concentrated interest that profits from it (Part Four), and the militarized culture that subordinates the biocultural formation's cultural plurality and orients the polity toward the destruction of itself and others (Part One). The war power, the security state, and the commitment to non-militarism are constitutional terrain because they are the terrain on which the biocultural formation either cultivates itself across generations or organizes itself toward its own and others' destruction, and the war-restraint traditions the project carries at primary-articulator standing supply the deepest constitutional wisdom the arc engages on the restraint of the capacity for that destruction.
