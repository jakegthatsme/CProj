## Opening — The Document Under the Sixth-Pass Architecture

This document is the Part VII anchor of the sixth-pass constitutional project: multi-eyed seeing as constitutional method. The project rests on a load-bearing triad — biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation. The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the triad's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery. "Grown, not built" is the colloquial summary phrase.

Multi-eyed seeing is the methodological foundation of the entire arc. It bears on the analytical authority, the citation practice, and the conceptual architecture of every document. The Marshall family's Two-Eyed Seeing remains the methodological core; the multi-eyed extension honors rather than displaces the bilateral framework. The Eastern epistemic traditions are integrated at the primary-articulator standing the document accords to the Indigenous tradition through the Marshall family. And the renovation-versus-decolonial question is re-engaged under the cultivation framing — the document's most consequential analytical move and the point on which the project's answer to the decolonial critique turns.

The self-reflexive question the project carries — whether the multilateral commitment domesticates underrepresented traditions or permits genuine co-constitution — is sharpened rather than dissolved under the cultivation framing. Cultivation does not domesticate, because cultivation does not synthesize. What the methodological discipline maintains is the set of conditions under which traditions continue to grow alongside one another in their distinctive registers, not a single architectural register the discipline produces. The test of the commitment is whether those conditions are maintained across the project's downstream work; the discipline operates as an ongoing commitment rather than a resolved problem. Document I.01 (Part I) is the foundational-texts integration the methodological commitment grounds; Document II.01 (Part II) is the polity-as-biocultural-formation thesis the commitment serves methodologically.

## Part One — From Two-Eyed Seeing to Multi-Eyed Seeing: The Conceptual Development

The Marshall family's Two-Eyed Seeing — Etuaptmumk — is the foundational primary-articulator engagement at Indigenous constitutional-methodological standing. Mi'kmaw Elders Albert Marshall and Murdena Marshall, together with Cheryl Bartlett, articulated the framework in their 2012 essay "Two-Eyed Seeing and Other Lessons Learned within a Co-Learning Journey of Bringing Together Indigenous and Mainstream Knowledges and Ways of Knowing" in the *Journal of Environmental Studies and Sciences*, building on their longer collaborative work through the Integrative Science — Toqwa'tu'kl Kjijitaqnn — program at Cape Breton University begun in the 1990s. Etuaptmumk, the Mi'kmaw word the Marshalls translate as "the gift of multiple perspectives," names the discipline of seeing with one eye through the strengths of Indigenous ways of knowing and with the other eye through the strengths of Western ways of knowing, and using both together to produce analytical engagement neither tradition alone can produce.

The priority of the Marshalls' articulation matters to the project's continuing commitment. Two-Eyed Seeing is a Mi'kmaw articulation engaged with Western scientific traditions on Mi'kmaw analytical terms; it is not Western multidisciplinarity extended to include Indigenous knowledge as one input among several. The commitment under the Marshalls' articulation is that Indigenous ways of knowing carry epistemic standing co-equal with Western ways of knowing, and that the engagement maintains the integrity of each tradition rather than absorbing one into the other.

The multi-eyed extension the project carries — the simultaneous engagement of multiple analytical traditions whose authority the project recognizes as co-equal — is an extension of the Marshalls' framework rather than a displacement. It follows the bilateral commitment outward into the multi-traditional canonical inheritance the project commits to: Western constitutional-democratic; Indigenous constitutional in its many nations; Black-radical; dalit and South Asian anti-caste; disability-justice; neurodivergent; feminist and Black-feminist; Latinx and Chicanx; Asian-American; decolonial-theory; climate-justice; African; Islamic; Latin American and Andean; Caribbean; Pacific Islander; Jewish. Each tradition stands at primary-articulator standing in its own analytical register. The self-reflexive question — whether the multilateral extension domesticates the underrepresented traditions or permits genuine co-constitution — is part of the analytical work the commitment carries. The cultivation framing's answer is operational rather than declarative: cultivation, unlike synthesis-into-architecture, does not domesticate, and the test of the commitment is whether the conditions for the traditions' continued growth are maintained across the project's downstream work.

## Part Two — The Indigenous Research-Methodology Tradition

The Indigenous research-methodology tradition stands at primary-articulator constitutional-methodological standing. Linda Tuhiwai Smith's (Ngāti Awa, Ngāti Porou) *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (1999, second edition 2021) articulates the foundational commitment: a critique of Western research methodologies' historical operation against Indigenous peoples, and an articulation of Indigenous research methodologies as primary-articulator standing rather than as alternatives evaluated against Western standards. Smith establishes that Indigenous research methodologies are constituted through Indigenous epistemic, ontological, and ethical commitments — not as methodological techniques but as engagements with Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and acting in the world.

Margaret Kovach's (Plains Cree and Saulteaux) *Indigenous Methodologies: Characteristics, Conversations, and Contexts* (2009, second edition 2021) articulates Plains Cree and Saulteaux methodological commitments through story, ceremony, and relational accountability as research methodology. Shawn Wilson's (Opaskwayak Cree) *Research Is Ceremony: Indigenous Research Methods* (2008) articulates the Cree commitment that research is ceremony — that the practice of research carries the same constitutive relational, ethical, and ontological commitments as ceremony itself. Bagele Chilisa's *Indigenous Research Methodologies* (2012, second edition 2020) articulates African and broader Indigenous research methodologies through Setswana and Southern African commitments. Eve Tuck and K. Wayne Yang's *Toward What Justice?* (2018) and their earlier "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" (*Decolonization: Indigeneity, Education and Society*, 2012) hold that decolonization is a structural commitment rather than a metaphorical orientation. Vine Deloria Jr.'s (Standing Rock Sioux) *Custer Died for Your Sins: An Indian Manifesto* (1969) and *Red Earth, White Lies: Native Americans and the Myth of Scientific Fact* (1995) engage Indigenous methodological commitments against the operations of Western scientific knowledge production. Devon Mihesuah (Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma) and Angela Cavender Wilson (Wahpetonwan Dakota), in their edited *Indigenizing the Academy: Transforming Scholarship and Empowering Communities* (2004), articulate the commitments of Indigenizing the academy.

Under the cultivation framing, the Indigenous research-methodology tradition is read as a primary articulation of cultivation-condition methodology — the conditions under which research that engages Indigenous peoples and the more-than-human world is conducted under Indigenous epistemic and ethical commitments rather than under Western frameworks evaluated against Indigenous knowledge. The tradition's commitments — relational accountability; ceremony as constitutive engagement; story and oral tradition as epistemic engagement; community-based research conducted by and with Indigenous communities; Indigenous languages as epistemic-ontological commitment; land as constitutive relation rather than research site — are foundational cultivation-condition specifications the project's broader methodological discipline operates under. The Western qualitative-research literature is recent and partial recovery of what this tradition articulates foundationally.

## Part Three — Eastern Epistemic Traditions at Primary-Articulator Standing

The Eastern epistemic traditions stand at primary-articulator constitutional-methodological standing alongside the Indigenous research-methodology tradition. They are placed here, immediately following the Indigenous tradition, so that the parallel standing is structural and not merely asserted. They are not supplementary to an Indigenous-and-Western frame; they are foundational primary articulators of epistemic commitments in their own analytical registers.

### The Confucian Epistemic Tradition

The Confucian epistemic tradition runs through the *Analects*' commitments on *zhi* — knowing — and the rectification of names, engaged through Bryan Van Norden's and Edward Slingerland's translations and commentary; through the *Doctrine of the Mean*, the *Zhongyong*, on *cheng* — sincerity — as an epistemic-moral commitment, engaged through Roger Ames and David Hall's *Focusing the Familiar* (2001); through Wang Yangming's *Instructions for Practical Living* on *liangzhi* — innate moral knowing — and the unity of knowledge and action, *zhi xing he yi*; and through Zhu Xi on *gewu*, the investigation of things, and the Cheng brothers' *li*-and-*qi* epistemic-ontological architecture. The contemporary engagement runs through Tu Weiming's *Confucian Thought: Selfhood as Creative Transformation* (1985), Wm. Theodore de Bary's *Asian Values and Human Rights* (1998), and Roger Ames and David Hall's *Thinking Through Confucius* (1987) and *Thinking from the Han* (1998). The Confucian commitment integrates knowing and action — Wang Yangming's claim that knowledge which does not issue in action is not knowledge — and treats moral cultivation as constitutive of epistemic adequacy. The Western pragmatist tradition through Peirce, James, and Dewey is recent and partial recovery of this substrate.

### The Daoist Epistemic Tradition

The Daoist epistemic tradition runs through the *Daodejing* and the *Zhuangzi* on *wu wei* knowing and the perspectival epistemology of the *Zhuangzi*'s "Equality of Things" chapter, the *Qiwulun*, engaged through Brook Ziporyn's *Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings* (2009) and Philip J. Ivanhoe's *The Daodejing of Laozi* (2002). The Daoist commitment to perspectival epistemology — that knowing operates from perspectives rather than from a view-from-nowhere — anticipates by more than two millennia the engagement with situated and standpoint epistemology that Western feminist epistemology articulates as a recent commitment. The Daoist critique of fixed-concept thinking, and the commitment to the contingency of conceptual frameworks, is foundational primary-articulator substrate that the Western late-twentieth-century engagement with conceptual-scheme dependence partially recovers.

### The Buddhist Madhyamaka and Yogacara Traditions

The Buddhist Madhyamaka tradition founded by Nagarjuna (circa 150–250 CE) articulates the two-truths doctrine — conventional truth and ultimate truth — and the analysis of emptiness, *śūnyatā*, as a foundational epistemic-ontological commitment, engaged through Jay L. Garfield's *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way* (1995) and *Engaging Buddhism: Why It Matters to Philosophy* (2015). The two-truths doctrine holds that engagement with the world operates through the conventional categorizations that organize ordinary cognition while remaining aware that those categorizations are themselves constituted relationally and lack independent self-existence. The methodological commitment is a foundational engagement with the constituted character of analytical categories: the categories through which engagement operates are themselves under analytical scrutiny rather than fixed in advance. The Yogacara tradition founded by Asanga (circa 300–370 CE) and Vasubandhu (circa 316–396 CE) extends the engagement into the analytical psychology of cognition — the eight-consciousness doctrine, the *ālaya-vijñāna* or storehouse consciousness, and the analysis of how the world appears to cognition — a foundational substrate the contemporary Western cognitive-science tradition partially recovers. The Chan and Zen epistemic discipline through Huineng's *Platform Sutra* and Dōgen's *Shōbōgenzō*, particularly the *Genjōkōan*, articulates non-dual cognition and sustained attention as an epistemic discipline.

### The Classical Indian Darshana Traditions

The classical Indian *darshana* schools — Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, and Vedanta — and their distinctive *pramāṇa*, or epistemic-authority, theories provide foundational engagement at primary-articulator standing. The Nyaya tradition's account of perception, *pratyakṣa*; inference, *anumāna*; comparison, *upamāna*; and verbal testimony, *śabda*, as epistemic categories; the Mimamsa tradition's textual hermeneutics; and the Vedanta tradition's engagement with the relationship between phenomenal cognition and ultimate reality together constitute a foundational engagement with epistemic categories at scholarly depth. The contemporary engagement runs through B. K. Matilal's *Perception: An Essay on Classical Indian Theories of Knowledge* (1986) and *The Character of Logic in India* (1998), and through J. N. Mohanty's *Classical Indian Philosophy* (2000).

### The I Ching and Pattern-Recognition Epistemology

The *I Ching*, the *Yijing* or Book of Changes — engaged through Richard Wilhelm and Cary Baynes's translation (1950) and Hellmut Wilhelm's *Heaven, Earth, and Man in the Book of Changes* (1977) — articulates pattern recognition as an epistemic discipline. Its commitment is a foundational engagement with the discipline of recognizing pattern under conditions of change: the world is always in process, and the cultivation of pattern-recognition discipline is a foundational methodological commitment for engagement with that process.

### The Kyoto School Twentieth-Century Articulation

The Kyoto School's engagement with the Eastern philosophical traditions in dialogue with Western philosophy — Nishida Kitarō's *An Inquiry into the Good* (Japanese original 1911), Nishitani Keiji's *Religion and Nothingness*, and Tanabe Hajime's metanoetic philosophy — extends the engagement into the twentieth-century philosophical conversation. The Kyoto School is engaged here as a contemporary articulation of the Eastern foundational traditions rather than as a foundational text in the sense the classical and Madhyamaka traditions constitute.

The Eastern epistemic traditions are foundational primary articulators of epistemic commitments — perspectival knowing, the constituted character of analytical categories, knowing-and-action unity, pattern-recognition discipline, and the analytical psychology of cognition — that the Western critical-epistemology canon partially recovers. The methodological discipline of multi-eyed seeing engages the Eastern traditions at primary-articulator standing, not as comparative supplement to an Indigenous-and-Western frame.

## Part Four — Standpoint Epistemology and Epistemic Plurality

The standpoint-epistemology tradition stands at primary-articulator methodological standing in the multi-traditional canonical inheritance. It runs through Sandra Harding's *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge? Thinking from Women's Lives* (1991) and the strong-objectivity commitment Harding articulated; through Dorothy E. Smith's *The Everyday World as Problematic: A Feminist Sociology* (1987) and *The Conceptual Practices of Power* (1990) on the everyday-world standpoint; through Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* (1990, revised 2000) on the intersectional Black-feminist standpoint; through Donna Haraway's "Situated Knowledges" (*Feminist Studies*, 1988); through Nancy Hartsock's foundational work on the feminist standpoint; and through Alison Wylie's engagement with standpoint as an analytical resource in the feminist philosophy of science.

The standpoint commitment is that knowledge is constituted from positions in social relations rather than from a view-from-nowhere, and that the positions of marginalized and oppressed peoples carry epistemic resources that dominant-position knowledge production systematically misses or distorts. The commitment is not relativism — the standpoint tradition explicitly distinguishes itself from relativism — but an engagement with the constitutive role of social-relational position in knowledge production. Under the cultivation framing, the standpoint canon is read as a cultivation-condition specification at the epistemic level: standpoint plurality is the cultivation substrate of epistemic adequacy at scale. A polity that takes the dominant-position view as the polity's view systematically misses the epistemic resources the marginalized-position standpoints carry; standpoint plurality is constitutive of the cultivation conditions for epistemic adequacy rather than supplementary to a single-standpoint epistemic engagement.

## Part Five — Epistemic Injustice and Epistemic Resistance

The epistemic-injustice literature engages the systematic operations through which standpoint-plural epistemic resources are distorted, marginalized, or rendered invisible. Miranda Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing* (2007) articulated the distinction between testimonial injustice — the credibility deficit suffered by speakers from marginalized standpoints — and hermeneutical injustice — the lack of collective interpretive resources for marginalized-standpoint experiences. José Medina's *The Epistemology of Resistance* (2013) extended the engagement to the epistemic-resistance commitments through which marginalized standpoints engage and resist that injustice. Kristie Dotson's "Tracking Epistemic Violence, Tracking Practices of Silencing" (*Hypatia*, 2011) and "Conceptualizing Epistemic Oppression" (*Social Epistemology*, 2014) engage epistemic oppression; Gaile Pohlhaus's "Relational Knowing and Epistemic Injustice" (*Hypatia*, 2012) engages willful hermeneutical ignorance; and Rebecca Tsosie (of Yaqui descent) engages Indigenous epistemic injustice and Indigenous standpoint resources.

Under the cultivation framing, the epistemic-injustice literature is read as a diagnostic of cultivation-condition failure at the epistemic level: the conditions under which standpoint-plural epistemic resources contribute to the polity's epistemic adequacy are systematically violated through the operations of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice. This diagnostic gives the methodological commitment its empirical-political stakes — the discipline of multi-eyed seeing operates against the operations of epistemic injustice the dominant epistemic-political economy produces.

## Part Six — Decolonial Theory and the Pluriverse

The decolonial-theory canon stands at primary-articulator standing. It runs through Walter D. Mignolo's *The Darker Side of Western Modernity: Global Futures, Decolonial Options* (2011) on the coloniality of being, knowledge, and power; through Mignolo and Catherine E. Walsh's *On Decoloniality* (2018); through Aníbal Quijano's "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America" (*Nepantla*, 2000); through Catherine Walsh's corpus on decolonial constitutionalism; through Arturo Escobar's *Designs for the Pluriverse: Radical Interdependence, Autonomy, and the Making of Worlds* (2018); through María Lugones's "The Coloniality of Gender" (2008); through Boaventura de Sousa Santos's *Epistemologies of the South: Justice Against Epistemicide* (2014); and through Linda Martín Alcoff's *The Future of Whiteness* (2015).

The decolonial commitment is that the operations of coloniality — across being, knowledge, power, and gender — continue to organize contemporary political-philosophical, epistemic, and constitutional engagement, and that a decolonial response requires engagement with the constitutive operations of coloniality rather than incremental reform within colonial frameworks. The decolonial critique of synthesis — the claim that integrative-synthetic engagements with a multi-traditional inheritance risk reproducing coloniality by absorbing the underrepresented traditions into a single architectural register the synthesizer controls — is the central critique the project's renovation-versus-decolonial engagement addresses in Part Eight. The decolonial canon is preserved here at primary-articulator standing; the cultivation re-frame is integrated through Part Eight.

## Part Seven — The Neurodivergent and Disability-Justice Epistemic Contribution

The neurodivergent and disability-justice traditions stand at primary-articulator epistemic-methodological standing. The neurodivergent engagement runs through Steve Silberman's *NeuroTribes: The Legacy of Autism and the Future of Neurodiversity* (2015) on neurodiversity as an epistemic contribution; through Devon Price's *Unmasking Autism: Discovering the New Faces of Neurodiversity* (2022); through Robert Chapman on neurodivergent personhood; through Damian Milton's "The Double Empathy Problem" (*Disability and Society*, 2012) on the bilateral character of communicative-empathic engagement; and through the autistic-self-advocacy tradition, including the Autistic Self Advocacy Network. The disability-justice tradition engaged at primary-articulator standing in Document II.02 is methodologically constitutive here: Patty Berne and the Sins Invalid corpus on the ten principles of disability justice; Mia Mingus on access intimacy; Eli Clare's *Brilliant Imperfection* (2017); Alison Kafer's *Feminist, Queer, Crip* (2013); Sami Schalk's *Bodyminds Reimagined* (2018); Margaret Price's *Mad at School* (2011); and Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha's *Care Work: Dreaming Disability Justice* (2018). The Mad-studies tradition through China Mills's *Decolonizing Global Mental Health* (2014), Brendan Stone, the Asylum Magazine corpus, and Bonnie Burstow's *Psychiatry and the Business of Madness* (2015) extends the engagement.

Under the cultivation framing, the neurodivergent and disability-justice traditions are read as a primary articulation of cultivation-condition methodology at the organism-and-organ-system scale of the nested-scales reference frame: cognitive plurality and embodied integrity are constitutive cultivation conditions of the polity's epistemic substrate rather than supplementary considerations to an abstract-deliberative epistemic engagement. Document XII.05 (Part XII — the neurodivergent polis) operationalizes the cognitive-plurality commitment as bounded decision rights at organism-and-organ-system scales; the methodological commitment in this document anchors the broader epistemic-methodological engagement.

## Part Eight — The Renovation-Versus-Decolonial Question Re-Engaged Under Cultivation Framing

The renovation-versus-decolonial question is the most consequential analytical engagement the document carries. The decolonial critique — that the project's move from a Western frame to a multi-traditional canonical commitment risks being itself a Western synthesis that absorbs the traditions it claims to honor into a single architectural register the synthesizer controls — is the strongest analytical challenge the project faces. The sixth-pass framing's move is to replace the renovation framing the project carried in earlier passes with the cultivation framing, and to specify the analytical case for the cultivation framing's answer to the critique.

### The Decolonial Critique on Its Strongest Terms

The decolonial critique runs through Mignolo's engagement with the operations of coloniality of being, knowledge, and power in *The Darker Side of Western Modernity* (2011) and *On Decoloniality* (2018, with Walsh); through Quijano on the coloniality of power; through Lugones on the coloniality of gender; through Santos's *Epistemologies of the South* (2014); through Tuck and Yang's claim that decolonization is not a metaphor (2012); and through Alcoff's engagement with whiteness and decolonial commitments in *The Future of Whiteness* (2015).

On its strongest terms, the critique runs as follows. Integrative engagements with a multi-traditional inheritance, however well-intentioned, operate within the analytical apparatus of Western modernity — its categories, its philosophical frameworks, its political-economic conditions, its epistemic-institutional infrastructure. The integration of underrepresented traditions into such projects, even when conducted with respect, risks reproducing coloniality by translating the underrepresented traditions into the Western apparatus the project operates within; the traditions are thereby reduced to data points, sources, or resources for the Western project rather than maintained as autonomous traditions on their own analytical ground. A decolonial response requires engagement with the operations of coloniality at the constitutive level — not incremental reform within colonial frameworks but engagement with the structural-political-epistemic conditions under which the colonial frameworks operate. The critique is serious, and the project takes it seriously. Any engagement that claims to honor the multi-traditional canonical inheritance must engage the critique on its strongest terms and provide a genuine answer rather than dismissing it or treating it as a problem solved by good intentions.

### Renovation as Design-Imposition; Cultivation as the Alternative

The analytical move of the sixth-pass framing is to specify the difference between renovation framing and cultivation framing. Renovation framing holds that the constitutional architecture is a designed institutional fixture revisable through engineered amendment to produce a more adequate architecture. It operates within Anglo-American design-imposition assumptions the multi-traditional foundational inheritance does not share. Under renovation framing, the move from a Western frame to a multi-traditional canonical commitment risks operating as synthesis-into-architecture: the underrepresented traditions are read as offering resources for the renovated Western architecture, with the risk that the integration reproduces precisely the synthesis-as-appropriation the decolonial critique identifies.

Cultivation framing operates within different assumptions. The polity is cultivated, not designed; the conditions under which it grows are specified through the multi-traditional foundational inheritance the polity engages; the polity's growth into adequate honor of its inheritance is intergenerational cultivation work rather than a one-shot architectural revision; and the cultivation conditions are themselves diverse across the inheritance and are not to be synthesized into a single architectural specification. Cultivation, unlike renovation, does not synthesize-into-architecture. It maintains the conditions under which the traditions continue to grow alongside one another in their distinctive registers; it does not produce a single architectural specification the traditions must conform to.

### The Cultivation Framing's Answer

The answer to the decolonial critique under the cultivation framing operates at four registers. First, the analytical content of the cultivation framing is supplied by the Indigenous and Eastern primary articulators' own specifications of cultivation — by the Daoist *wu wei*, the Buddhist dependent origination, the Indigenous land-and-people-as-constitutive-relation, the Confucian cultivation of virtue across nested scales, the Andean *sumak kawsay*, the Pacific Islander *Vā*, the African Ubuntu, the Māori *whakapapa*, the Haudenosaunee seventh-generation criterion — not by Western recovery of these articulations. The Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery; the Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the primary articulators of cultivation framing itself. The analytical authority of the framing is supplied by the primary articulators, not by Western synthesis of them.

Second, the cultivation framing's methodological operation maintains the integrity of each tradition's primary articulations in its distinctive register. The traditions are not translated into a single architectural register; they are engaged at their own analytical depth, with their own primary articulators, in their own scholarly traditions. The operational test is whether the project's downstream work maintains the integrity of the primary articulators across the analytical arc — an operational test, not a declarative one.

Third, the cultivation framing's temporal commitment is intergenerational rather than synthesis-completing. The polity is cultivated across generations through engagement with the multi-traditional foundational inheritance; the work is not completed by any one analytical pass but continues across the temporal horizon the cultivation operates over, through the operational commitments the project specifies in Document XII.04, Document XIII.01, and Document XIV.01.

Fourth, the decolonial critique is preserved as a live methodological discipline throughout the project's downstream work. The cultivation framing does not foreclose the critique; it specifies the analytical terrain on which the critique continues to operate. The test is whether the downstream work maintains the conditions under which traditions continue to grow alongside one another in their distinctive registers — and the decolonial discipline is constitutive of the engagement with that test.

### The Critique's Strongest Counterposition Engaged Analytically

The strongest counterposition the cultivation framing must address is that even cultivation framing operates from within a Western analytical apparatus and so risks absorbing the traditions it claims to honor. The answer is that the analytical content of cultivation framing is supplied by the Indigenous and Eastern primary articulators' own specifications of cultivation; the Western apparatus is the apparatus within which the project's engagement is conducted, but the content is not generated from within the Western apparatus alone. The philosophical authority of the framing is rooted in the primary articulators' own specifications; the Western recovery canon is read as recovery rather than as foundation.

The counterposition's strongest form — that this move itself operates within the Western apparatus and so cannot escape coloniality — is engaged with the recognition that no project conducted within the contemporary academic-institutional infrastructure can claim to escape coloniality entirely. The project operates with awareness of its situated character. The commitment is not the claim that the project escapes coloniality but the operational commitment to engage the multi-traditional inheritance under conditions that honor the primary articulators, and the test of that commitment is whether the project's downstream work honors them across the analytical arc. The cultivation framing does not resolve the decolonial critique into completed argumentation; it specifies the terrain on which the critique operates as an ongoing methodological commitment.

## Part Ten — Closing Integration: Multi-Eyed Seeing as Intergenerational Cultivation Methodology

The multi-eyed-seeing commitment is constitutive of the project's analytical work, and it is itself intergenerational cultivation work. The discipline operates across generations as an ongoing commitment rather than as a methodology completed in one analytical pass and specifiable in advance of operation. The Marshall family's Two-Eyed Seeing remains the methodological core; the multi-eyed extension honors rather than displaces the bilateral framework; and the Indigenous, Eastern, African, Islamic, Latin American, Caribbean, Pacific Islander, Jewish, dalit, Black-radical, disability-justice, neurodivergent, and feminist traditions each engage in their distinctive analytical registers.

The cultivation conditions of the methodological discipline include the maintenance of primary-articulator standing for the Indigenous and Eastern traditions across the project's downstream work; the discipline of refusing absorption and synthesis-into-architecture; the discipline of holding the decolonial critique as a live methodological commitment rather than a resolved problem; the discipline of attending to the neurodivergent-epistemic and embodied-knowing contributions at the organism-and-organ-system scale; and the discipline of maintaining the temporal orientation under which the cultivation work continues across generations rather than completing in any one analytical pass.

The operational stakes are constitutive. The project's commitment to multi-eyed seeing under the cultivation framing is the commitment that the constitutional architecture is cultivated, not designed; that the conditions under which the polity grows are maintained rather than specified in advance; and that the multi-traditional canonical inheritance is the substrate the polity is cultivated through across the intergenerational time-horizon Document XII.04 operationalizes and Documents XIII.01 and XIV.01 integrate. The cultivation work proceeds.
