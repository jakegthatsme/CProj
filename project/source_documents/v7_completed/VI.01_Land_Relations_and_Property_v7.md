# VI.01 — Land Relations and Property as Cultivation

## Part VI — The Material Substrate

*New sixth-pass document. Working designation in the planning record: Document EE / "Land." This document is the leading document of Part VI, the foundational property-relation substrate on which the food-and-soil, energy, materials, and built-environment documents of Part VI rest.*

*The sixth-pass constitutional project rests on a load-bearing triad. Biocultural governance is the constitutional ontology — the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a biocultural formation, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled at constitutive depth rather than at the level of correlation. Eco-mimetic policy-making is the operational method — ecological, complex-adaptive-systems-theoretic, biomimetic, and disturbance-ecology concepts inspire how laws are passed, written, and tested at constitutional and statutory scale. Disturbance-adapted implementation is the temporal orientation — intergenerational architecture cultivated to absorb, metabolize, and recover from a plural disturbance landscape comprising five named regimes (cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic) and their interactions. The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the principle's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery of framings these traditions have long carried. "Grown, not built" is the project's colloquial summary phrase.*

Land relations are the paradigm case of a biocultural formation. Where the ontology document of Part III specifies the polity as a complex adaptive system in which biological substrate and cultural plurality are coupled, land is the terrain on which the coupling actually happens — the particular watershed in which a particular people develops a particular foodway, the bioregion whose seasons organize a calendar of ceremony and labor, the planetary surface whose continued habitability conditions the continuance of every polity that stands upon it. A constitutional order that treats land as an alienable, commodified stock of resource to be allocated has, in the terms this document will develop, mistaken the substrate of the polity's life for an inventory of inputs to the polity's economy. The error is not peripheral. It is the error from which the rest of Part VI's material-substrate documents inherit their diagnostic burden, and it is the error this document is written to name, to trace to its historical installation, and to specify the constitutional alternative to.

The document carries no designated sixth-pass new thread; it is not a thread carrier. It operates under the fourteen cross-cutting threads of the arc, foregrounding biocultural governance, the nested-scales reference frame, the intergenerational temporal orientation, and the multi-traditional canon held at co-equal weight without synthesis. Its analytical work proceeds in four phases across nine threads. The first phase establishes land relations as cultivation terrain across the nested scales of the reference frame the ontology document specifies. The second conducts the diagnostic of the property-as-engineered-bundle-of-rights framing as a design-imposition. The third — the document's load-bearing center, drafted at its deepest analytical depth — engages the primary-articulator land traditions in their own registers: the Indigenous land-relations tradition above all, then the white-possessive critique of the colonial transformation, then the Land Back articulation, then the commons tradition, the agrarian and bioregional traditions, and the Black agrarian tradition. The fourth specifies what a constitutional architecture must cultivate for land relations to be grown rather than engineered, and bridges to the decision-rights architecture of Part XII and the synthesis of Part XIII.

The document assumes the constitutional ontology and the nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01 and does not re-derive them. It assumes the foundational-normative inheritance of Document I.01, in which land-and-resource sovereignty was specified at Indigenous primary-articulator standing through the Kaianerekowa, the Uluru Statement from the Heart, the Andean rights-of-nature constitutions, and the Treaty of Waitangi as read under *Matike Mai Aotearoa*. It conducts its engagement with the Indigenous, commons, and Land Back traditions under the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01. It does not perform the food-and-soil analysis of Document VI.02, the materials analysis of Document VI.04, or the built-environment analysis of Document VI.05; it specifies the land-relation substrate those documents draw upon. And it does not specify the bounded decision rights themselves or the cultivation protocol that integrates them; those are the work of Part XII and Part XIII, which this document points forward to.

# Part One — Land Relations as Cultivation Terrain Across Nested Scales

## Thread A — The Cross-Scale Frame

The dominant policy vocabulary of land treats it as a stock. Land is acreage; acreage is a quantity; the quantity is allocated among uses — residential, agricultural, industrial, conservation, extractive — and the allocation is adjusted at the margin as prices and preferences shift. The vocabulary is so deeply settled that its assumptions read as neutral description rather than as a particular and contestable framing. But the stock vocabulary carries three assumptions that the cultivation framing of this project rejects. It assumes that land is fungible, that one acre is exchangeable for another acre of equivalent classification, so that what matters about a parcel is its quantity and its grade rather than its particularity. It assumes that land is passive, an inert surface on which uses are placed, rather than a living formation with its own dynamics, memory, and tendencies. And it assumes that land relations occur at a single scale — the scale of the parcel, the holding, the jurisdiction's zoning map — so that the constitutional question of land reduces to who holds which parcels and under what terms of transfer.

The cultivation framing begins from a different account. Land is not a stock but a terrain — the ground on which a biocultural formation grows. To say that the polity is a biocultural formation, as Document III.01 specifies, is to say that its biological substrate and its cultural plurality are coupled; land is where the coupling is located. A foodway is a relation between a people and a soil; a ceremonial calendar is a relation between a people and a bioregion's seasons; a watershed's health is at once an ecological fact and the condition of the cultural practices the watershed sustains. Land is the substrate of the coupling, and a constitutional architecture responsible for the polity as a biocultural formation is therefore responsible for land relations as a constitutional matter rather than as a sectoral-policy matter delegated to a land-use bureau.

Land relations operate across the nested-scales reference frame that Document III.01 articulates as a constitutional category. The reference frame specifies spatial scales — biosphere, ecozone, ecosystem, habitat, population, organism — and temporal analogs — geological time, ecological-succession time, generational time, lifetime, decade, decision-cycle time — and specifies that the constitutional architecture matches decision rights to the scales at which the relevant dynamics operate, rather than reducing all decisions to two or three administrative tiers. This document does not re-derive the reference frame; it specifies how land relations differ in kind across three of its scales, and why a constitutional architecture adequate to land must address all three rather than collapsing land onto the single scale of the parcel.

At the ecosystem scale, land relations are relations to a particular place — this watershed, this forest, this prairie, this stretch of coastline. The ecosystem scale is the scale of the particular: a specific assemblage of soils, waters, plants, animals, and the human communities whose practices are entangled with them, operating on the generational temporal horizon over which a watershed's hydrology or a forest's succession unfolds. Land relations at this scale are irreducibly local; they cannot be specified in the abstract because what it means to relate well to a place depends on the place. The knowledge that sustains an ecosystem-scale land relation — which slopes erode, when the salmon return, how the fire regime works, which plants indicate which conditions — is knowledge of this place, accumulated across the generations of a community's residence in it. Document III.01, drawing on Daniel Wildcat's (Yuchi) account of Indigenous Knowledge of Place, specifies that this knowledge is not an inventory extractable from the place but is the place's relationship with the community across time. The ecosystem scale is where that account is located.

At the ecozonic scale, land relations are relations to a biome and a bioregion — the boreal forest, the temperate grassland, the coastal-marine zone, the arid interior, the tropical forest. The ecozonic scale operates on the ecological-succession temporal horizon, across centuries to millennia, and at spatial extents that cross political boundaries: a bioregion is defined by its watershed, its climate, its characteristic vegetation and fauna, and its long-resident human cultures, not by the surveyor's line. Land relations at this scale concern the continuance of the ecozone's characteristic dynamics — the fire regime of a grassland, the flood pulse of a river basin, the migration corridors that knit a biome together. They cannot be cultivated parcel by parcel, because the dynamics they concern cross every parcel boundary; they require coordination among the communities and polities that share the ecozone. Contemporary constitutional architectures, as Document III.01's analysis of scale-mismatch specifies, operate principally at the national and sub-national administrative scales, and the consequence is cumulative drift between administrative land decisions and ecozonic requirements. The ecozonic scale is where the bioregional tradition, engaged in Thread G, does its analytical work.

At the biospheric scale, land relations are relations to the planetary surface itself — to land as the thin living skin of a single biosphere whose soils, forests, wetlands, and ice together regulate the climate, the carbon and nitrogen cycles, and the conditions of habitability for every polity. The biospheric scale operates on the geological temporal horizon. Land relations at this scale concern commitments that no single polity can secure alone and that no generation can discharge within its own lifetime: the maintenance of the planet's forest cover, the integrity of its soils, the stability of the systems that the disturbance regimes of Part II diagnose as under stress. Land at the biospheric scale is the substrate that all five disturbance regimes act upon — the physical-environmental regime most directly, but the others through it, since cultural, biological, cognitive-informational, and political-economic disturbances all ultimately register in the condition of the land that carries the polity. The biospheric scale is where the seven-generations principle the Haudenosaunee tradition articulates, carried into the project through Document III.01 and Document I.01, exerts its full weight: a land relation adequate to the biospheric scale is one that holds the planetary substrate in trust across a temporal horizon longer than any deciding generation will witness.

A constitutional architecture must address all three scales because land relations are different in kind at each, and an architecture that addresses only one produces characteristic failures at the others. An architecture that operates only at the ecosystem scale — that secures good relations to particular places but provides no coordination across the bioregion or commitment at the biosphere — cannot prevent the cumulative ecozonic and biospheric drift that the sum of locally reasonable parcel decisions produces. An architecture that operates only at the biospheric scale — that legislates planetary targets without scale-matched institutions beneath them — produces the legibility imposition that James C. Scott's work, engaged in Thread F, identifies as the characteristic failure of the high-modernist state: targets specified from above, imposed on places whose particular knowledge the targets cannot see. The nested-scales frame is the alternative to both. It specifies land relations as cultivated at every scale at once, with decision rights matched to the scale at which each kind of land dynamic operates, and with the constitutional architecture responsible for the cross-scale coordination that no single scale can supply.

This is what it means to treat land as cultivation terrain rather than as resource stock. The stock framing flattens land to a single scale and a single relation — the parcel, and its allocation. The cultivation framing holds land at three scales and as a living formation whose dynamics the polity participates in rather than administers. The diagnostic of the next thread specifies how the property concept that organizes the contemporary constitutional order installed the flattening, and what a property concept adequate to the cultivation framing would retain and discard.

# Part Two — The Property-as-Engineered-Bundle-of-Rights Diagnostic

## Thread B — Property as a Design-Imposition Framing

The contemporary constitutional order organizes land through a particular concept of property, and the concept has a history. It is the conceit of the concept that it has none — that property is a natural fact, that to own a thing is simply to stand in the relation that any rational agent stands in to what is theirs, and that the legal apparatus of title, transfer, and exclusion merely records a relation given prior to law. The diagnostic of this thread, conducted through three works of the Euro-American property-theory canon, specifies that the contemporary property concept is not a natural fact but a design-imposition framing: a particular construction, assembled at a particular historical moment for particular purposes, that abstracts land from relation to it and renders it commodifiable, and that has come to read as natural precisely because the construction succeeded.

C. B. Macpherson's *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism: Hobbes to Locke* (Oxford University Press, 1962) supplies the genealogy. Macpherson's analysis specifies that seventeenth-century English political theory — across Hobbes, the Levellers, Harrington, and Locke — shares a set of assumptions he names possessive individualism: the individual is essentially the proprietor of his own person and capacities, owing nothing to society for them; freedom is a function of possession; human society is a series of market relations among proprietors. The assumptions are not argued for in the texts Macpherson examines; they are the unexamined ground on which the arguments stand. And the assumptions do specific work in Locke's account of property in the fifth chapter of the *Second Treatise of Government*. Locke's account begins from the individual's property in his own person, derives property in things from the mixing of the person's labor with them, and applies the derivation first of all to land: the man who encloses, tills, and improves a parcel makes it his property by mixing his labor with it. The account is bounded, in its initial statement, by two provisos — that one leave "enough, and as good" for others, and that one not let what one takes spoil. But Locke then specifies that the invention of money, by storing value in an imperishable form, lifts the spoilage limit, and that the greater productivity of enclosed land satisfies the sufficiency proviso even as enclosure proceeds without limit. The provisos, having been stated, are dissolved. What remains is a theory in which land becomes private property through individual improvement, accumulates without limit, and is held by a proprietor who, in Macpherson's phrase, owes nothing to society for what he holds. Macpherson's analytical point is that this theory is a historically located construction — assembled in seventeenth-century England, during the enclosure of the English commons and the early formation of a capitalist agrarian economy — and that its appearance as a natural account of property is itself part of what the construction accomplished. The possessive-individualist concept of land as the improvable, accumulable, alienable property of an individual abstracted from social and ecological relation is, on Macpherson's showing, a design-imposition: a frame imposed on land at a datable moment, in the service of a particular political economy.

Margaret Jane Radin's *Reinterpreting Property* (University of Chicago Press, 1993) supplies the internal critique — the demonstration, from within the property-theory tradition, that the concept of property as uniformly fungible and uniformly alienable does not survive contact with how property actually figures in human lives. Radin's central distinction is between fungible and personal property. Fungible property is property held purely instrumentally, for its exchange value, fully substitutable by its monetary equivalent — the investor's portfolio, the speculator's holdings. Personal property is property bound up with personhood, property whose loss cannot be made good by its market price because the thing has become constitutive of the person who holds it — the home one has lived in, the wedding ring, the land a family has worked across generations. Radin's argument is that the law of property, and the constitutional protection of property, cannot coherently treat the two as a single category, and that a property regime which assimilates all property to the fungible model — which presumes that any holding is adequately compensated by its market value — has mistaken one kind of property relation for the whole of property. The cultivation framing of this project carries Radin's distinction further than her own argument takes it. Where Radin specifies that some property is bound up with personhood, the land traditions engaged in Part Three specify that land relation is bound up with personhood as such — that the relation to a particular place is constitutive of the person and the community in a way the fungible model cannot register at all. Radin's work is the point at which the property-theory canon, pressing on its own concept, gestures past commodification toward a relation it does not have the apparatus to name. The diagnostic credits the gesture and marks where the canon's own resources run out.

Carol M. Rose's *Property and Persuasion: Essays on the History, Theory, and Rhetoric of Ownership* (Westview Press, 1994) supplies the demonstration that property is convention rather than nature. Rose's essays specify that a property regime is a system of persuasion — that property depends on narrative, rhetoric, and convention, on the telling of persuasive stories about who holds what and why. Her analysis of the first-possession doctrine shows that to acquire property by being the first to possess a thing requires "acts of possession" that communicate a claim to a relevant audience — that possession is not a brute physical fact but a statement, addressed to a community, that the community must be persuaded to recognize. Her analysis of the commons shows that whether a resource is treated as open to all, held in common by a defined group, or enclosed as private property is a matter of the stories a society finds persuasive, not a matter dictated by the resource itself. Rose's point, for this diagnostic, is decisive: if property is a system of persuasion and convention, then the contemporary property regime is one persuasive system among the possible ones, and its appearance as natural fact is the measure of how thoroughly the persuasion has succeeded. A convention that has been naturalized can be re-examined; a convention that is still seen as a convention can be deliberately revised. Rose's work returns property to the status of convention, and in doing so returns it to the domain of constitutional choice.

Taken together, the three works specify the design-imposition framing the diagnostic names. The framing's signature device is the bundle-of-rights metaphor. On the bundle-of-rights account, which became the orthodox analytical picture of property in twentieth-century Anglo-American legal theory, property is not a relation between a person and a thing but a bundle of separable, distinct rights — the right to use, the right to exclude, the right to transfer, the right to the income, the right to the capital, the right to security, and others — each of which is a separable "stick" that can be held, divided, sold, leased, or mortgaged independently of the others. The bundle metaphor has a genuine analytical use: it captures that ownership is divisible, that a tenancy, an easement, a mortgage, and a mineral lease can coexist on a single parcel. But the metaphor, taken as the concept of property as such, performs the design-imposition. It pictures property as an engineered fixture — an assembly of standardized, interchangeable components, designed to be disassembled and recombined. It abstracts the rights from the particular land: a "right to exclude" is the same stick whether the parcel is a desert or a wetland, a sacred site or a parking lot, because the bundle is defined by its components and not by what it is a bundle in. And it builds alienability into the definition: a stick that could not be sold, leased, or mortgaged would not, on the bundle account, be a full stick of property at all. The bundle metaphor thus renders land into a commodity by construction. It makes land an engineered object whose value is the sum of its tradeable rights, abstracted from the place the rights attach to and from any relation a community might stand in to that place.

The diagnostic's analytical move is now to ask what survives of the property concept under the cultivation framing and what does not. The move is not abolition. A constitutional order must answer the questions property answers — who may use a place, who may decide about it, what security of relation a person or community has in the place where their life is located — and the cultivation framing does not pretend those questions away. What survives is a reconstructed concept built around three commitments. First, stewardship obligation: a land relation under the cultivation framing carries, as a condition of the relation itself, an obligation to the land's continuance and to those who will hold the relation after — the obligation is constitutive of the relation, not an external regulatory limit grudgingly placed upon an otherwise absolute right. Second, scale-matched use rights: the right to use, decide about, and have security in a place, specified at the scale at which the relevant land dynamics operate, so that an ecosystem-scale relation, an ecozonic coordination, and a biospheric commitment are each a recognized form of land holding rather than all being collapsed onto the parcel. Third, security of relation to place: the protection of a person's and a community's continued, reliable relation to the particular place where their biocultural life is located — the commitment Radin's category of personal property gestures toward, generalized from the exceptional case to the ordinary condition of land relation. What does not survive is the design-imposition core: the abstraction of the right from the particular land, the presumption of alienability as definitional, and the bundle as an engineered fixture of interchangeable, tradeable sticks. The cultivation framing retains property's work — the ordering of relations to place — and discards property's commodity construction. The threads of Part Three specify the relation to place that the reconstructed concept must be adequate to, beginning with the tradition that articulates it most fully.

# Part Three — Indigenous Land-Relations as Primary Articulation

## Thread C — Land as Relation, Gift, Kin, and Grounded Normativity

The Indigenous land-relations tradition is the primary articulator of what land is. This is the document's central and load-bearing claim, and it must be stated precisely, because the strongest risk this document faces is the absorption of the tradition into the property-theory frame of the preceding thread — the rendering of "land as relation" as a values-supplement or a spiritual gloss decorating an analysis whose real apparatus remains Western property theory. The relation is the reverse. The Euro-American property canon, on the diagnostic of Thread B, is the design-imposition tradition under examination — a historically located construction that abstracted land from relation. The Indigenous land-relations tradition is the tradition that articulates the land relation the construction abstracted away, and it articulates it not as a critique mounted against property theory from property theory's own terrain but as the prior and standing account of what land is, carried by peoples whose relation to particular lands precedes the property concept by millennia and has continued through and beyond its imposition. The tradition is engaged here under the multi-eyed-seeing discipline of Document VII.01: each scholar in the tradition's own analytical register, the registers not collapsed into one, and the engagement conducted by a document that does not claim the standing of the tradition's own articulators but reads what they specify at the constitutional scale this project operates. This thread draws on but does not duplicate the primary-articulator engagement of Document III.01, which engaged the broader Indigenous-ecological tradition for the constitutional ontology; here the engagement is specific to land relation and property.

Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi), in *Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants* (Milkweed Editions, 2013), articulates land as gift. The articulation is precise and it is not metaphorical. In the analytical apparatus Kimmerer writes from, the land gives — the maple gives sugar, the strawberry gives fruit, the sweetgrass gives itself to the braider's hands — and what is given is a gift in the full sense the word carries: it establishes a relation, it carries obligation, and it is degraded if it is converted into a transaction. Kimmerer specifies the difference through the contrast between a gift economy and a commodity economy. In a commodity economy, a thing is acquired by purchase; the transaction is complete when payment is made; the buyer owes the seller nothing further and stands in no continuing relation to the thing's origin. In a gift economy, a thing received as a gift carries an ongoing obligation — to gratitude, to reciprocity, to the care of the giver and of the relation the gift establishes. Kimmerer's central claim about land is that land is properly understood within the second frame and not the first: the relation between a people and the land that feeds them is a gift relation, carrying the obligations gift relations carry, and the conversion of land into a commodity is not a neutral change of legal form but the severance of the obligations that the gift relation constitutes. She specifies the discipline of the gift relation as the Honorable Harvest — the protocol of taking only what is given, never taking the first or the last, taking only what one needs, using what one takes with respect, sharing it, and giving back — and she specifies its opposite through the Anishinaabe figure of the Windigo, the being of insatiable consumption that takes without limit and reciprocity, the embodiment of the commodity logic when it is loosed upon the land. Kimmerer's land-as-gift is the direct articulation of what the bundle-of-rights concept abstracts away: a bundle of alienable sticks carries no obligation of gratitude, no protocol of reciprocity, no relation to a giver, because it has been constructed precisely to be free of all of these.

Vine Deloria Jr. (Standing Rock Sioux), in *God Is Red: A Native View of Religion* (1973), articulates land as the ground of relation in a register Kimmerer's does not occupy: the register of the fundamental orientation of a people's existence. Deloria's central analytical contrast is between spatial and temporal orientations. Christianity and the political traditions formed within its orbit are, on Deloria's account, temporally oriented: their sacred narrative is a history, a sequence of events unfolding in time toward an end, and their relation to place is comparatively thin, because the divine is disclosed in history rather than in geography. Indigenous traditions, Deloria specifies, are spatially oriented: the sacred is disclosed in particular places, the people's foundational narratives are emplaced in a specific geography, and the relation to land is therefore not one relation among a people's relations but the ground of the people's existence as a people. Deloria's well-known formulation — that American Indians hold their lands, their places, as having the highest possible meaning, and that all their statements are made with that reference point in mind — specifies a land relation that cannot be rendered as property at all, because property is precisely the relation that holds the particular place to be substitutable by its monetary value, and a place that is the ground of a people's existence has no monetary equivalent. Deloria's analysis bears directly on the diagnostic of Thread B. The removal of a people from its land, on Deloria's account, is not a transfer of an asset; it is an attack on the people's existence as a people, because it severs the spatial ground on which the people's foundational orientation rests. The property frame cannot register this, because the property frame has no category for a place that is constitutive rather than instrumental. Deloria's land-as-ground-of-religion specifies the magnitude of what the property frame's abstraction conceals.

Glen Sean Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene), in *Red Skin, White Masks: Rejecting the Colonial Politics of Recognition* (University of Minnesota Press, 2014), articulates grounded normativity, and supplies the thread with its sharpest analytical instrument. Grounded normativity, in Coulthard's specification, is the ethical framework that emerges from a people's sustained, place-based relationship with the land — the modes of thought, the obligations, the protocols of right relation that are generated by and carried within the practices of living with a particular territory across generations. Grounded normativity is not a philosophy applied to land from outside; it is normativity that the land relation itself produces. Coulthard specifies that for the Dene the land is understood as a field of reciprocal relations and obligations — not a stock of resources, not even, in the first instance, a homeland in the territorial sense, but a system of relationships that carries ethical content. And Coulthard mounts, from this ground, the critique that gives his book its title: the critique of the politics of recognition. The liberal-multicultural settler state, Coulthard argues, offers Indigenous peoples recognition — a measured accommodation within the existing constitutional and property order, a seat at the table, a degree of self-administration, a negotiated settlement of claims. But recognition, Coulthard specifies, is offered on the settler state's terms and within the settler state's frame, and a people that accepts recognition on those terms is drawn into reproducing the very order that dispossessed it: the property order, the resource-extraction economy, the constitutional structure that holds the land to be ultimately the state's to allocate. Coulthard's alternative is resurgence grounded in grounded normativity — the rebuilding of the land relation and its associated forms of knowledge and obligation from within the tradition, rather than the negotiation of a better position within the settler frame. The analytical force of Coulthard's account for this document is twofold. It specifies, with precision, that the land relation carries its own normativity — that the question of land is not a question of allocating an ethically neutral resource but a question that the land relation itself answers. And it issues a warning the document's Thread I must honor: a constitutional architecture that offers the Indigenous land traditions recognition within the existing property frame has not engaged them at primary-articulator standing; it has performed the politics of recognition that Coulthard's analysis identifies as the continuation of dispossession by accommodative means.

Leanne Betasamosake Simpson (Michi Saagiig Nishnaabeg), in *As We Have Always Done: Indigenous Freedom Through Radical Resistance* (University of Minnesota Press, 2017), articulates land as pedagogy, and carries the tradition's account forward from grounded normativity to resurgence. Simpson's specification of land as pedagogy is that the land teaches — that Nishnaabeg intelligence, the knowledge and the ethical formation that constitute a Nishnaabeg person, is generated through engaged, reciprocal practice with the land, and that the land is therefore not the setting in which education happens but the source and the substance of it. To learn, in this account, is to enter into relation with a place and to be formed by the relation. Simpson's broader argument concerns Indigenous resurgence — the regeneration of Indigenous life, governance, and knowledge from within Indigenous practices, what she names Nishnaabewin, the totality of Nishnaabeg ways of being. And Simpson specifies a politics of refusal: a refusal of the terms of recognition, consonant with Coulthard's, but framed by Simpson as generative — a refusal that is not merely a saying-no to the settler frame but a turning toward the rebuilding of Indigenous nationhood, grounded in land relations, on Indigenous terms. Simpson's contribution to this thread is the specification that the land relation is not static heritage to be preserved but a living practice that generates, in each generation, the people who carry it. This bears on the cultivation framing directly. A land relation is cultivated, in Simpson's account, in the most literal sense: it is grown, generation by generation, through the practices that bring each new generation into relation with the land — and a constitutional architecture that severs those practices, or that permits the land to be alienated out from under them, has not merely transferred an asset but has cut the pedagogy through which a people continues to exist.

Winona LaDuke (Anishinaabe), in *All Our Relations: Native Struggles for Land and Life* (South End Press, 1999) and *Recovering the Sacred: The Power of Naming and Claiming* (South End Press, 2005), articulates the land relation in the register of the contemporary struggle to sustain and recover it. *All Our Relations* documents, across a series of Native environmental struggles — the Seminole, the Northern Cheyenne, the Anishinaabeg of White Earth, the Inupiat, the Native Hawaiians, and others — the land-based economies through which Native communities have sustained themselves, and the assault those economies have faced from extraction, contamination, and dispossession. LaDuke specifies that a land-based economy is not a quaint survival but a functioning alternative political economy: a wild-rice economy, a subsistence-hunting economy, a sustainable-forestry economy, each of which holds the land in a relation of use-with-continuance rather than use-with-exhaustion. *Recovering the Sacred* documents the work of recovery — the repatriation of sacred objects and ancestral remains, the reclamation of sacred sites, the recovery of names, of seed varieties, of the land itself through the buy-back of alienated reservation acreage. LaDuke's contribution to the thread is the specification that the Indigenous land relation is not only a prior account to be acknowledged but a living and contested project — that the relation has been attacked and is being rebuilt, parcel by parcel, seed by seed, site by site, and that the rebuilding is a present political reality and not a historical memory. LaDuke's work supplies the bridge from this thread to Thread E, where the Land Back articulation specifies the recovery as a constitutional question.

What the Indigenous land-relations tradition articulates that the Euro-American property canon does not can now be stated as the thread's central finding. The tradition specifies that land is kin and relative rather than object — that the proper category for the maple, the river, the territory is the category of relation that one stands in to a relative, with the obligations a relative carries, and not the category of the thing one owns. It specifies that relation to land is constitutive of personhood and of law — that the person is formed through the land relation, as Simpson's land-as-pedagogy specifies, and that the normativity which orders a people's life is generated by the land relation, as Coulthard's grounded normativity specifies. And it specifies — this is the point that the diagnostic of Thread B most needs and that the document must not soften — that these are not metaphors. When Kimmerer says the land gives gifts, when Deloria says the land is the ground of a people's existence, when Coulthard says the land is a field of reciprocal obligation, when Simpson says the land teaches, they are not decorating a property analysis with evocative language; they are stating the literal terms of a land-relation tradition that is different from, and prior to, the property tradition. The property tradition's account of land as an alienable bundle of rights is itself, on the multi-eyed-seeing discipline, one tradition's account — a recent and particular one, as Thread B's genealogy specifies. The Indigenous land-relations tradition is another, older, and on the cultivation framing more adequate account. The document holds the two as distinct traditions and does not synthesize them; what it specifies is that the constitutional architecture of Part XII and Part XIII must be built so that the Indigenous land relation stands at primary-articulator standing within it — not recognized within the property frame, in Coulthard's precise sense of recognition, but carried as a co-equal and prior account of what land is.

# Part Three-bis — Eastern, Islamic, and African Land-Relation Traditions at Primary-Articulator Standing

## Thread C-bis — Land as Trust, Triadic Relation, and Ancestral Commons

Thread C carried the Indigenous land-as-relation tradition at primary-articulator standing against the property frame. The land-as-relation commitment is, however, articulated at primary-articulator standing across the Eastern, Islamic, and African traditions as well, and the document engages them here in their own registers rather than as comparative supplement, because the property frame's displacement of the land relation is global and its recovery draws on more than one tradition's primary articulation.

The Confucian and Daoist traditions articulate the land relation through participation rather than possession. The Confucian triad of *tiān* (heaven), *dì* (earth), and *rén* (the human) locates the human not above the earth as its proprietor but between heaven and earth as the participant whose cultivation completes their relation, and the agrarian Confucian ethic accordingly reads the land as the substrate of an obligation discharged through care across generations rather than as an asset disposed through title. The Daoist tradition reads the land as the living order of the Dao — the practice of *fēngshuǐ* as a relational reading of the land's energies, and the *Daodejing*'s insistence that the cultivated person follows the land's own patterns rather than imposing on them — which carries the *wu wei* and *ziran* commitments `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v7` engages into the land relation directly: the land is not inert matter to be engineered but a living order to be followed.

The Islamic tradition carries a developed land-as-trust jurisprudence at primary-articulator standing. The earth belongs to God, and the human holds it as *khalīfa* (vicegerent and steward) under *amāna* (trust), so that the land relation is a relation of accountable stewardship rather than absolute dominion. The tradition institutionalizes the commitment through concrete commons institutions: the *ḥimā* (the protected reserve held in common and withdrawn from private appropriation, classically for pasture, water, and habitat), the *ḥarīm* (the inviolable protective zone around water sources, wells, and watercourses within which development is prohibited), and *iḥyāʾ al-mawāt* (the revival of dead land, which conditions any claim to land on its actual use and care rather than on title or speculation). The *ḥimā* and *ḥarīm* are among the oldest continuously documented commons-conservation institutions, and they stand at primary-articulator standing alongside the Western commons tradition Thread F engages, articulating land held and governed in common as a binding religious-legal institution rather than as a residual exception to private property.

The African traditions articulate the land relation as an ancestral and intergenerational commons. The customary land jurisprudence of West Africa carries the formulation, recorded in the colonial-era land commissions and transmitted in the customary-tenure tradition, that land belongs to a community of which many are dead, few are living, and countless members are yet unborn — the land held not by the present occupants as owners but by the lineage across the full intergenerational span, with the living holding it in trust for the ancestors and the unborn. The *ubuntu* relational ontology grounds the land relation in the same relational personhood it grounds the economy and health in: the land is the substrate of the relations through which persons are constituted as persons, and its alienation through the property frame is the alienation of the relational substrate itself. The African ancestral-commons land relation carries the intergenerational obligation the seventh-generation criterion `XII.09_Bounded_Decision_Rights_Across_Nested_Scales_v7` operationalizes, articulated in the register of lineage and ancestry rather than of generational accounting.

These traditions differ from the Indigenous land relations of Thread C and from one another in their cosmologies and their juridical forms, and the cultivation discipline holds the differences in their own registers rather than dissolving them into a single non-Western land ethic. What they share, and what they articulate at primary-articulator standing against the property frame, is the refusal of land-as-possession: land as trust, as triadic participation, as ancestral commons — the land relation the property frame displaced and the constitutional architecture must recover.

# Part Four — The White-Possessive Logic and the Colonial Transformation of Land Relation

## Thread D — The Datable History of the Property Frame

The diagnostic of Thread B specified that the property-as-engineered-bundle framing is a design-imposition — a construction rather than a natural fact. This thread specifies the historical event of the construction: the colonial transformation of land relation, through which the property frame was installed across the lands of North America and the broader settler-colonial world, and through which it acquired the appearance of naturalness by displacing and overwriting the land relations that preceded it. The thread engages two works — one on the logic of the imposition, one on its concrete historical operation — and its analytical purpose is to establish that the property frame has a datable history, and that a frame with a datable history is a frame that constitutional choice can revise.

Aileen Moreton-Robinson (Goenpul), in *The White Possessive: Property, Power, and Indigenous Sovereignty* (University of Minnesota Press, 2015), specifies the logic. Moreton-Robinson's central concept is the white possessive: the possessive logic through which settler-colonial sovereignty constitutes itself. The settler nation-state, she specifies, is founded on a possession that it must continually assert and naturalize — the possession of the land — and the assertion operates as a logic that runs through the state's law, its political discourse, and the subjectivity of its citizens. The settler state constitutes the land as ownable, as territory available to be possessed, and constitutes the settler subject as the proper owner — the one whose belonging in the place takes the form of ownership. Moreton-Robinson specifies that this possessive logic is racialized: the capacity to own, to be the proper proprietor, is bound up with whiteness, and the property relation is therefore not a race-neutral legal form but a form through which a racial order is constituted and maintained. Her analysis draws the property relation and the racial order into a single frame. And she specifies the alternative the possessive logic is constructed to deny: Indigenous sovereignty, which she articulates as an ontological relation — a belonging *to* the land, an existence constituted by the relation to country — rather than a possession *of* the land. The white possessive constitutes land as a thing that can be owned; Indigenous sovereignty, in Moreton-Robinson's articulation, is a relation in which the people belong to the land rather than the land to the people. The settler state cannot acknowledge the second without unsettling the first, and so the possessive logic operates, continually, to render Indigenous sovereignty invisible or to translate it into a form — a claim, a title, a negotiable interest — that the property frame can absorb. Moreton-Robinson's account specifies, with precision, why the property frame resists the Indigenous land relation of Thread C: not as a matter of oversight, but because the property frame is the form in which the settler state's foundational possession is held, and the Indigenous land relation names what that possession displaced.

William Cronon, in *Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England* (Hill and Wang, 1983), specifies the concrete historical operation of the transformation in one well-documented case. Cronon's ecological history of colonial New England traces the transformation of the land itself — its forests, soils, animal populations, and waterways — as the consequence of a transformation in land relations. The Indigenous peoples of New England, Cronon specifies, held land through a system of usufruct: the right to use a place for a particular purpose in a particular season — this clearing for planting, this stretch of river for fishing, this woodland for hunting — with the rights distributed among groups, varying by season, and not amounting to the exclusive, permanent, alienable ownership of a bounded parcel. The land was not, in the Indigenous system, a thing that anyone owned in the colonists' sense; it was a field of overlapping use rights organized by the seasonal round. The colonists brought a different system: fixed boundaries, exclusive and permanent title, the parcel as a bounded thing that one person owned entirely and could sell. Cronon specifies that the colonial system did not merely coexist with the Indigenous one; it overwrote it, and the overwriting transformed the land. Fixed boundaries and exclusive ownership brought fencing, permanent agriculture, and the introduced livestock that compacted soils and required ever more cleared land. The commodification of land — the treatment of a parcel as an asset to be improved and sold — brought the deforestation that fed the timber and potash trades and the soil exhaustion that followed the market's demand for cash crops. Cronon's summary judgment is that the colonists became, in his phrase, a people of plenty who were a people of waste: the property system that made land an alienable commodity made its exhaustion rational, because a commodity that can be sold need not be sustained by the one who holds it. Cronon's case study makes Thread B's diagnostic concrete and dated. The property-as-engineered-bundle frame did not arrive in New England as a natural fact; it arrived as the colonists' system, displacing an Indigenous system of usufruct, and the date of its arrival and the mechanism of its imposition are matters of historical record.

The thread's analytical conclusion follows from the two works together. The property frame is not natural, universal, or original. It is a particular construction — Macpherson's genealogy locates its theoretical assembly in seventeenth-century England — imposed, through the colonial transformation Cronon documents and the possessive logic Moreton-Robinson analyzes, on lands that were held, before the imposition, through entirely different relations. The frame's appearance of naturalness is an artifact of the imposition's success: a system that has overwritten its predecessor and erased the memory of the overwriting will read, to those formed within it, as simply how things are. The cultivation framing's recovery of the property frame's history is therefore not an antiquarian exercise. It is the precondition of constitutional choice. A frame understood as natural cannot be deliberately revised, because there is understood to be nothing to revise — only reality to conform to. A frame understood as a datable, particular, imposed construction can be revised, because it is understood to be a construction, and what one construction installed another can be cultivated to replace. Thread D establishes that the property frame is of the second kind. Thread E specifies the constitutional question that the recovery of this history places on the present.

# Part Five — Land Back as the Contemporary Constitutional Question

## Thread E — Jurisdiction, Consent, and the Live Commitment

The colonial transformation of land relation that Thread D dated is not a closed historical episode. The dispossession it installed is, in the analysis of the Land Back articulation engaged in this thread, an ongoing condition — a structure that continues to operate, continues to govern who holds jurisdiction over land, and continues, therefore, to pose a question to any constitutional architecture that proposes to order land relations honestly. This thread engages the Land Back articulation as that question. Its purpose is to specify that Land Back is a present constitutional question — a matter of jurisdiction to be answered — and not a historical grievance to be acknowledged, and the document must hold the distinction throughout, because the characteristic failure mode here is precisely the conversion of a live jurisdictional question into a settled historical wrong eligible for commemoration.

The *Land Back: A Yellowhead Institute Red Paper* (2019), produced by the Yellowhead Institute, a First Nations–led research centre, specifies Land Back as the reclamation of jurisdiction. The Red Paper's analytical core is the distinction between two ways a settler state can relate to Indigenous land rights. The first is consultation: a procedure in which the state, having reserved to itself the ultimate authority to decide, consults Indigenous nations about decisions affecting their lands — solicits their views, accommodates them where convenient, and proceeds. The second is consent: a relation in which Indigenous nations hold the authority to grant or withhold agreement to what happens on their lands, so that a decision cannot proceed over their refusal. The Red Paper specifies that the contemporary settler-state framework, for all its language of reconciliation, operates overwhelmingly through the first relation and not the second — that consultation, as institutionalized, is a procedure for managing Indigenous objection rather than for recognizing Indigenous authority, and that it leaves the jurisdictional question, the question of who ultimately decides, answered in the state's favor. Land Back, on the Red Paper's articulation, is the demand that the jurisdictional question be reopened and answered differently: that decision-making authority over land — jurisdiction, in the full sense — be returned to Indigenous nations. The Red Paper is precise that Land Back is not reducible to the transfer of title to particular parcels, though it includes that; it is the return of the authority to decide, the recovery of Indigenous jurisdiction as a standing constitutional fact. This is what makes Land Back a constitutional question rather than a property question. A property question asks who holds which parcels; a constitutional question asks who holds the authority to decide about land, at what scale, under what relation of consent. Land Back asks the second.

Nick Estes (Lower Brule Sioux), in *Our History Is the Future: Standing Rock Versus the Dakota Access Pipeline, and the Long Tradition of Indigenous Resistance* (Verso, 2019), specifies the depth of the history the question stands on and the temporal frame within which Indigenous nations hold it. Estes's account of the Standing Rock struggle against the Dakota Access Pipeline — the struggle of the Oceti Sakowin, the people of the Seven Council Fires, and their allies, conducted under the watchword Mni Wiconi, water is life — places the 2016 confrontation within a continuous history that Estes traces back through the twentieth-century flooding of Oceti Sakowin lands by the Pick-Sloan dam projects, through the nineteenth-century treaties and their violation, through the wars of the Plains, to a relation to the Mni Sose, the Missouri River, that long precedes the United States. Estes's titular claim — that the history is the future — specifies the temporal frame within which the Land Back question is held. The Indigenous resistance at Standing Rock was not a reaction to a discrete 2016 grievance; it was, in Estes's account, the most recent expression of a resistance carried continuously across generations, conducted by people who understand themselves as bound to ancestors and to the not-yet-born, as the caretakers of a relation that runs from a deep past into a far future. Estes's contribution to the thread is the specification that the Land Back question is held by Indigenous nations on an intergenerational temporal horizon — the same horizon the seven-generations principle articulates, carried into this project through Document I.01 and Document III.01 — and that a constitutional architecture which proposes to engage the question on the horizon of a single electoral cycle, or to treat it as an account to be settled and closed, has mismatched the temporal scale of its response to the temporal scale of the question.

What Land Back asks of a constitutional architecture can now be specified, and it is considerable. It asks that the jurisdictional question be treated as open — that the constitutional order not presume, as its unexamined foundation, that ultimate authority over all land rests with the settler state and its property system. It asks that the relation of consent, not consultation, govern decisions about Indigenous lands. It asks that the return of jurisdiction be understood as a standing constitutional commitment held on an intergenerational horizon, not as a finite program of claims settlement with a completion date. And it asks all of this of the cultivation framing specifically, because the cultivation framing's central claim — that land relations are grown under conditions the constitutional architecture cultivates rather than engineered by design-imposition — is empty with respect to Indigenous land if the conditions it cultivates leave the colonial jurisdictional settlement in place. A cultivation framing that grew everything except the answer to the Land Back question would be cultivating land relations on land whose foundational dispossession it had declined to examine. Thread I therefore carries the Land Back question forward as one of the four land-relation conditions a constitutional architecture must cultivate, and carries it forward as a live jurisdictional commitment — a question the architecture holds open and is built to answer — rather than as a historical wrong the architecture acknowledges and moves past. The document records the distinction as a constitutional commitment and not as a sentiment: Land Back is engaged here as the contemporary constitutional question of land, and the cultivation framing answers to it.

# Part Six — The Commons Tradition

## Thread F — Land Held and Governed in Common

The property-as-engineered-bundle framing of Thread B presents a constrained menu: land is either private property, held by an individual proprietor, or it is state property, held by the government. The two options exhaust the field; what is not owned by a private proprietor must, on the framing's own logic, be owned and managed by the state, since unowned land is presumed to be land subject to the ruin that follows from the absence of an owner. The commons tradition engaged in this thread is the demonstration that the menu is false — that there is a third form, land held and governed in common by a defined community through its own institutions, and that this third form is not a transitional defect awaiting resolution into private or state ownership but a durable, functional, and historically widespread way of holding land. The thread engages three works, and it engages them with a tension held open rather than resolved, because the commons tradition and the Indigenous land-relations tradition of Thread C are distinct and at points genuinely opposed.

Elinor Ostrom, in *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* (Cambridge University Press, 1990), supplies the empirical refutation of the framing's central premise. The premise is the tragedy of the commons — the proposition, given its influential modern statement in Garrett Hardin's 1968 essay, that a resource held in common will inevitably be destroyed, because each user has an incentive to take as much as possible while the costs of depletion are shared by all. Ostrom's work, drawing on decades of empirical study of common-pool-resource institutions across the world — the high mountain meadows and forests of the Swiss and Japanese villages, the *huerta* irrigation systems of Spain, the irrigation institutions of the Philippines, inshore fisheries — demonstrates that the proposition is empirically false as a general claim. Communities have, in fact, governed common-pool resources sustainably across centuries, and Ostrom specifies the conditions under which they succeed. Her design principles for long-enduring common-pool-resource institutions name those conditions: clearly defined boundaries around the resource and the community of users; congruence between the institution's rules and local conditions; collective-choice arrangements through which those affected by the rules participate in making them; monitoring by accountable actors; graduated sanctions for violations; accessible conflict-resolution mechanisms; recognition by external authorities of the community's right to organize; and, for larger systems, nested enterprises in which smaller institutions are organized within larger ones. Ostrom's contribution to the thread, and to the cultivation framing, is twofold. She establishes that the commons is a real and durable third form of land holding, dissolving the false menu of Thread B's framing. And her design principles are themselves an articulation of scale-matched, polycentric governance — institutions matched to the scale of the resource, nested within larger institutions for larger-scale resources — which the cultivation framing carries forward, and which Document III.01's nested-scales reference frame already engages Ostrom's polycentricity to specify.

Peter Linebaugh, in *The Magna Carta Manifesto: Liberties and Commons for All* (University of California Press, 2008), supplies the commons tradition's suppressed constitutional history. Linebaugh's recovery centers on a document that has been all but erased from the constitutional memory of the English-descended legal order: the Charter of the Forest of 1217, the companion charter to Magna Carta. Where Magna Carta has been carried forward as the foundational charter of liberties, the Charter of the Forest, Linebaugh specifies, was the charter of the commons — the document that secured to commoners the rights of subsistence in the forest: estovers, the right to gather wood; pannage, the right to pasture pigs; the rights of grazing, of turbary, of the gathering that the common land sustained. Linebaugh's central analytical move is to specify commoning as a verb — as a practice, an activity, a way of living with land that a community continually carries out, rather than a static legal status. The commons, on Linebaugh's account, is not a category of land but a set of practices through which a community sustains its life from land it does not own as private property and does not hold as the state. And Linebaugh traces the long history of the suppression of commoning — the centuries of enclosure through which the English commons were converted into private property, the commoners dispossessed, and the Charter of the Forest forgotten. The recovery of that history is, for this document, the recovery of constitutional memory: the demonstration that the English legal tradition itself, the tradition from which the property-as-engineered-bundle frame descends, once carried a charter of the commons co-equal with its charter of liberties, and that the disappearance of the one and the survival of the other is itself a result of the design-imposition Thread B diagnoses.

James C. Scott, in *Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed* (Yale University Press, 1998), supplies the warning that the commons tradition carries to the cultivation framing. Scott's analysis concerns legibility — the project, central to the modern state, of rendering society legible: making it measurable, mappable, countable, and therefore governable from a center. Scott specifies that customary land tenure, with its overlapping, season-varying, community-specific use rights, is precisely the kind of complex local arrangement that the legibility project cannot tolerate, because it cannot be read from the center. The state's response, across the modern period, has been the cadastral map and the freehold title: the simplification of the dense web of customary tenure into the standardized, surveyed, individually owned parcel — which is legible, taxable, and governable, and which has destroyed, in the simplification, the customary commons that the legibility could not see. Scott's concept of *mētis* — the practical, local, experiential knowledge that customary arrangements embody and that the legible scheme cannot capture — names what is lost. The warning the commons tradition carries through Scott is therefore aimed at the cultivation framing itself. A constitutional architecture that proposed to cultivate land relations from the center, by specifying legible categories and imposing them downward, would be repeating the legibility imposition, and would destroy in the imposition the *mētis* that ecosystem-scale land relations depend on. The commons tradition recovers the third form of land holding; Scott's warning specifies that the third form cannot be installed from above without ceasing to be itself.

The tension that this thread holds open, rather than resolving, is the tension between the commons tradition and the Indigenous land-relations tradition of Thread C. The two are not the same analysis, and the document must not synthesize them. The commons tradition, as Linebaugh's history makes clear, has a specifically English genealogy — the forest, the manor, the village, the long English struggle of commoners against enclosure — and it operates within the broad terms of a debate about how land is held and governed: privately, by the state, or in common. The Indigenous land-relations tradition does not operate within those terms. As Thread C specified, that tradition articulates land as kin and relation, and its account is not a third position in a debate about forms of holding but a different account of what land is — one that the commons-versus-private-versus-state frame does not contain. To present the Indigenous land relation as a variety of commons would be to absorb it into a frame it stands outside of; it would be the synthesis-into-architecture this document's plan and the project's standing commitments forbid. The commons tradition is engaged here in its own register and at its own value: it dissolves the false menu, it recovers a suppressed constitutional history, and it carries Scott's warning. The Indigenous land-relations tradition is engaged in Thread C in its register and at its value. The two are distinct primary articulations, and Thread I will carry forward what each supplies — common right as a recognized form of land holding, from the commons tradition; the Land Back jurisdictional question and the kin-relation account of land, from the Indigenous tradition — without collapsing either into the other.

# Part Seven — The Agrarian and Bioregional Traditions

## Thread G — The Land Ethic, Reinhabitation, and Their Critical Limits

The agrarian and bioregional traditions engaged in this thread articulate an ethic of relation to land that arises from within the Euro-American settler world itself — from the farmer, the conservationist, the back-to-the-land reinhabitant — and that gestures, often powerfully, past the property-as-commodity frame. The thread engages the tradition for what it genuinely contributes to the cultivation framing. But it engages it critically, and the criticism is not incidental. The agrarian and bioregional traditions have at points carried a romanticism, a settler innocence, and an exclusionary politics of place, and a document that engaged them uncritically would let the agrarian register soften the Land Back question of Thread E and the dispossession history of Threads D and H. The thread therefore engages the tradition's contribution and its limit together.

Aldo Leopold, in *A Sand County Almanac, and Sketches Here and There* (Oxford University Press, 1949), articulates the land ethic. Leopold's central move is the enlargement of the moral community. Ethics, Leopold specifies, has historically extended moral standing to an ever-wider circle — from kin to tribe to the whole of humanity — and the land ethic is the next enlargement: the extension of the community to include soils, waters, plants, and animals, which Leopold names, collectively, the land. The land ethic, in Leopold's formulation, changes the role of the human from conqueror of the land community to plain member and citizen of it. And it supplies a criterion: a thing is right, Leopold writes, when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community, and wrong when it tends otherwise. The contribution to the cultivation framing is direct. Leopold's land-as-community is a frontal rejection of land-as-commodity, and his criterion specifies the obligation — to the integrity and continuance of the biotic community — that Thread B identified as surviving, under the name of stewardship obligation, into the reconstructed property concept. But the limit must be stated alongside the contribution. Leopold's land ethic is articulated as though the land he writes of — the Wisconsin sand county, the Southwest he worked in as a forester — were land whose human history began with its settlement, and the Indigenous peoples whose relation to those same lands long preceded Leopold's, and whose removal made the sand-county farm available to be bought and abandoned, are largely absent from his account. The land ethic enlarges the community to include soils and waters and animals; it does not, in Leopold's text, reckon with the prior peoples whose dispossession is the condition of the settler's presence on the land the ethic concerns.

Wendell Berry, in *The Unsettling of America: Culture and Agriculture* (Sierra Club Books, 1977) and *The Gift of Good Land: Further Essays Cultural and Agricultural* (North Point Press, 1981), articulates agrarianism as a culture of husbandry. Berry's analysis in *The Unsettling of America* specifies the displacement of the small farmer by an industrial agriculture organized around the standard of efficiency, and contrasts two standards: the standard of the exploiter, which is efficiency, and the standard of the nurturer, which is care. The nurturer — the husbandman, the good farmer — holds land in a relation of care, attending to its long-term health, its soil, its capacity to sustain a family and a community across generations; the exploiter holds land in a relation of extraction, organized around the maximization of short-term yield. Berry's concept of membership specifies that the good farmer is a member of the land community and of the human community the land sustains, bound by the obligations membership carries. And in *The Gift of Good Land*, Berry develops, from the Biblical phrase, the proposition that land is a gift held in trust — that the people did not make the land and cannot own it absolutely, and that their tenure is conditional on good use. Berry's contribution to the cultivation framing is the articulation of land tenure as conditional and care-bound — tenure held in trust, on condition of husbandry, rather than held as an absolute and unconditioned right. This is close kin to the stewardship obligation of Thread B's reconstructed concept. But Berry's agrarianism, too, carries a limit the document must mark. The agrarian ideal of the rooted family farm, the settled household on its own good land, can read the history of how that land became available — the Indigenous dispossession, the displacement Cronon's New England documents — as background rather than as the very subject the agrarian ethic ought to reckon with, and the agrarian register's nostalgia for a settled rural past can soften, if it is not checked, the live and unsettled jurisdictional question that Thread E specifies.

Peter Berg, in the anthology *Reinhabiting a Separate Country: A Bioregional Anthology of Northern California* (Planet Drum Foundation, 1978), and Kirkpatrick Sale, in *Dwellers in the Land: The Bioregional Vision* (Sierra Club Books, 1985), articulate bioregionalism. The bioregional vision specifies the bioregion — the region defined by its watershed, its landforms, its characteristic life, and its long-resident cultures — as the proper unit of human dwelling and self-governance, and it specifies reinhabitation as the practice through which people learn to live-in-place: to know their watershed, to live within its limits, to govern themselves at its scale. Bioregionalism's contribution to the cultivation framing is the articulation of the ecozonic scale, in this document's terms — the specification that human communities should organize their land relations and their governance at the scale of the bioregion rather than at the scale of the surveyor's jurisdiction, which is the ecozonic scale of Thread A given as a practical program. But bioregionalism carries the thread's sharpest critical problem in its very vocabulary. To call for the reinhabitation of a land is to imply a prior inhabitation that has lapsed and is now to be resumed by the reinhabitant — and the lands the bioregional movement called on settler people to reinhabit were, and are, inhabited: by the Indigenous peoples whose continuous relation to those bioregions Thread C articulates. A reinhabitation that does not reckon with the peoples who never ceased to inhabit the land enacts, in its language, exactly the erasure that Moreton-Robinson's white-possessive analysis names — the constitution of the land as available to be possessed, or in this case re-possessed, by the settler. The document credits bioregionalism's articulation of the ecozonic scale and marks, without softening it, the settler innocence the reinhabitation framing can carry.

Henry George, in *Progress and Poverty* (1879), articulates the land-value response to the capture of land rent. George's analysis begins from the paradox his title names: that material progress, across the nineteenth century, was accompanied by deepening poverty, that the advance of wealth and the advance of want went together. George's explanation locates the cause in land. As a society's progress raises the value of land, the gain is captured by those who own land, in the form of rent; the rent is not produced by the landowner's labor or enterprise but by the presence and progress of the whole community, and yet it accrues privately to the owner. George's remedy is the taxation of land value — the recovery, for the community, of the rent that the community's own presence creates. George's contribution to the cultivation framing is the specification that the value of land is socially and ecologically produced rather than created by the holder, and that a land relation which permits the private capture of socially produced land value installs, structurally, the concentration of wealth and the dispossession of the landless. This is a distinct contribution from Leopold's, Berry's, or the bioregionalists': it concerns the political economy of land rent rather than the ethic of land care, and it bears on the currency-and-political-economy architecture that other documents of the arc specify. But George's frame, too, remains within the property tradition — it proposes to tax land value, not to reconceive what land is — and the document engages it as a contribution to the reconstructed property concept's political economy rather than as an articulation of the land relation that Thread C supplies.

The thread's critical conclusion holds the contributions and the limits together. The agrarian and bioregional traditions contribute genuinely to the cultivation framing: Leopold's land-as-community and his criterion of integrity, Berry's tenure-in-trust and his standard of care, the bioregionalists' articulation of the ecozonic scale, George's analysis of socially produced land rent. The cultivation framing carries these forward. But the traditions are settler traditions, and they have at points carried the romanticism, the settler innocence, and the exclusionary politics of place that the thread has marked at each engagement. The discipline the document holds is that the agrarian register must not be permitted to soften the harder threads that surround it. The land Leopold loved and Berry farmed and the bioregionalists called settlers home to is land whose availability was produced by the dispossession Thread D dated and the enslavement-and-sharecropping history Thread H will specify, and the jurisdictional question Thread E raises remains open over all of it. The agrarian and bioregional traditions are engaged here as partial recovery — settler traditions reaching, with real insight and real limits, toward a land relation that the Indigenous tradition of Thread C articulates more fully and more truly — and the document holds them at exactly that standing.

# Part Eight — The Black Agrarian Tradition

## Thread H — Land, Freedom, and Repair

The Black agrarian tradition engaged in this thread stands at primary-articulator standing in this document. It articulates a land relation that neither the white agrarian tradition of Thread G nor the Indigenous land-relations tradition of Thread C articulates, because it is the land relation formed under a specific history: the history of enslavement, of the dispossession that followed emancipation, and of the long Black struggle for land as the material basis of freedom. The thread engages two works, and it engages them as the articulation of land relation under that history rather than as a supplement to the agrarian threads that precede it.

Monica M. White, in *Freedom Farmers: Agricultural Resistance and the Black Freedom Movement* (University of North Carolina Press, 2018), articulates the Black agrarian tradition as a tradition of resistance. White's analytical intervention is directed against a settled reading of Black agricultural history — the reading in which the land, for Black Americans, figures only as the site of oppression: the plantation, the sharecropper's debt, the cotton field worked under coercion, the land one fled in the Great Migration. That history is real, and White does not deny it. But she specifies that it is not the whole, and that the part it omits is the part that matters most for a land-relations document: the long tradition in which Black communities used agriculture and landholding as instruments of resistance, self-determination, and freedom. White traces this tradition from the Reconstruction-era demand for land — the demand that the formerly enslaved be given the material basis of freedom, the forty acres that would make emancipation more than nominal — through the Black agricultural cooperatives of the twentieth century, through the Federation of Southern Cooperatives, through Fannie Lou Hamer's Freedom Farm Cooperative in Mississippi, which White treats as a central case: a cooperative founded so that Black families driven off plantation land for registering to vote would have land of their own, food of their own, and the political independence that material self-sufficiency makes possible. White's concept of collective agency specifies the land relation the tradition articulates: land held and worked collectively as the foundation of a community's capacity to act for itself, to feed itself, to be politically independent of those who would coerce it through its dependence. The Black agrarian tradition, in White's articulation, is a tradition in which the relation to land is a relation of freedom-making — and this is a distinct articulation, because it specifies land relation as the answer to a specific unfreedom, the unfreedom of a people whose ancestors were themselves held as property and whose post-emancipation dispossession kept the substance of freedom out of reach.

Leah Penniman, in *Farming While Black: Soul Fire Farm's Practical Guide to Liberation on the Land* (Chelsea Green Publishing, 2018), articulates the Black agrarian tradition as recovery and as healing. Penniman's book is at once a practical manual of regenerative farming and a recovery of the African and African-diasporic agrarian inheritance — the specification, against the erasure that has attributed sustainable and regenerative agriculture to a white lineage, that many of its practices have deep roots in African agronomy and in the knowledge that enslaved Africans carried, against every effort to destroy it, into the diaspora. Penniman recovers the figure of the ancestor who, on the Middle Passage, braided seeds into her hair — carrying the means of life and the knowledge of cultivation through the passage into bondage — as the founding image of a diasporic agrarian inheritance that survived the attempt to extinguish it. And Penniman articulates land as a site of healing: the specification that for Black communities, whose relation to land was forged in the trauma of the plantation and the sharecropper's cabin, the reclamation of land as a place of one's own cultivation, dignity, and connection to ancestors is itself a practice of healing from that trauma. Penniman's work also documents the dispossession that the thread must name: the catastrophic decline of Black landholding across the twentieth century, from a peak at which Black farmers held a meaningful share of American farmland to the near-elimination of Black farm ownership — a dispossession effected through discriminatory lending, through the denial of credit and disaster relief, through the legal vulnerabilities of heirs' property, and through outright violence. Penniman's articulation specifies, alongside White's, that the Black land relation is a relation under active dispossession and active recovery, and that repair — the return of land, the recovery of the agrarian inheritance, the reparation of a specific and documented theft — is integral to it.

What the Black agrarian tradition articulates that the surrounding threads do not can now be specified. The white agrarian tradition of Thread G articulates an ethic of land care from the position of the settler whose tenure, however care-bound, rests on land made available by dispossession. The Indigenous land-relations tradition of Thread C articulates land as kin and as the ground of a people's existence, held in a relation continuous from before the colonial transformation. The Black agrarian tradition articulates neither of these. It articulates the land relation of a people brought to this land in bondage, themselves once held as property under the very property system Thread B diagnoses; a people for whom land was first the instrument of their unfreedom and then, in the tradition White and Penniman recover, the sought-after material basis of a freedom repeatedly promised and repeatedly withheld; a people whose twentieth-century landholding was built against every obstacle and then stripped away through a documented dispossession of its own. The Black agrarian land relation is a relation of freedom-making and of repair, formed under enslavement and its long afterlife, and it is irreducible to the other traditions the document engages. The thread holds it at primary-articulator standing, distinct, and carries its specification — land as the material basis of freedom, repair as integral to the land relation — into the cultivation specification of Thread I.

# Part Nine — The Cultivation Specification and the Bridge to the Decision-Rights Architecture

## Thread I — What the Constitutional Architecture Must Cultivate

The eight threads preceding have established the document's analytical ground. Land is cultivation terrain operating across nested scales, not a stock of resource (Thread A). The property-as-engineered-bundle framing is a design-imposition that abstracts land from relation and renders it commodity (Thread B). The Indigenous land-relations tradition articulates, as the prior and on the cultivation framing more adequate account, land as kin, gift, and the ground of grounded normativity (Thread C). The property frame was installed through a datable colonial transformation and is sustained by a possessive logic, which means it is a construction that constitutional choice can revise (Thread D). The Land Back articulation poses the return of land jurisdiction as a live contemporary constitutional question (Thread E). The commons tradition establishes land held and governed in common as a durable third form, and carries Scott's warning against the legibility imposition (Thread F). The agrarian and bioregional traditions contribute, with marked limits, an ethic of land care and an articulation of the ecozonic scale (Thread G). The Black agrarian tradition articulates land as the material basis of freedom and repair as integral to the land relation (Thread H). This closing thread specifies what the constitutional architecture must cultivate for land relations to be grown rather than engineered, and bridges to the decision-rights architecture of Part XII and the synthesis of Part XIII. It is a specification, not a summary, and it does not perform the work that the documents it points to are written to perform.

The cultivation framing's specification of land relation must be guarded, at the outset, against a slip-back that would betray it. The document has argued at length against property-as-engineered-bundle. The symmetrical error — the design-imposition slip-back in mirror-image form — would be to engineer, in advance, the abolition of property, or to specify a single new land-relation architecture and impose it downward with the same design-imposition logic the document has diagnosed. That would not be cultivation; it would be design-imposition with a different content. The cultivation framing does not specify either absolute property or its abolition. It specifies conditions — scale-matched, revisable, stewardship-bound conditions under which land relations are grown, plurally and locally, by the communities that hold them — and it leaves the grown relations themselves to be grown. What follows is a specification of conditions, in that precise sense.

The first condition is scale-matched land jurisdiction. The constitutional architecture must hold land relations at the ecosystem, ecozonic, and biospheric scales of the nested-scales reference frame, with decision rights over land matched to the scale at which the relevant land dynamics operate, rather than collapsing all land decisions onto the parcel and the administrative jurisdiction. This means that an ecosystem-scale land relation — a community's relation to its particular watershed or forest — is a recognized constitutional form, governed by the community whose *mētis*, in Scott's term, the relation depends on. It means that ecozonic land coordination — the coordination among communities and polities sharing a bioregion, for the continuance of the bioregion's characteristic dynamics — is a recognized constitutional form, matched to a scale that crosses administrative boundaries. And it means that biospheric land commitment — the holding of the planetary land substrate in trust across the geological and seven-generations horizons — is a recognized constitutional commitment, exerting its weight on every scale beneath it. Scale-matched jurisdiction is the structural form the nested-scales frame of Thread A takes when it is carried into the decision-rights architecture, and it is the alternative both to the parcel-collapsed property frame and to the centralized legibility imposition Scott warns against.

The second condition is the Land Back question as a live commitment. The constitutional architecture must hold the return of land jurisdiction to Indigenous nations as a standing, open, intergenerational constitutional commitment — engaged under the relation of consent rather than consultation that the Yellowhead Red Paper specifies, and on the intergenerational temporal horizon that Estes specifies — and not as a historical grievance to be acknowledged and closed. The cultivation framing answers to the Land Back question; an architecture that grew land relations on land whose foundational jurisdictional dispossession it had declined to reopen would, in Coulthard's precise sense, be performing the politics of recognition rather than engaging the Indigenous land tradition at primary-articulator standing. The condition is that the jurisdictional question be held open as a constitutional commitment the architecture is built to answer.

The third condition is common right as a recognized form of land holding. The constitutional architecture must recognize land held and governed in common — by a defined community, through its own scale-matched and polycentric institutions, on the model the commons tradition of Thread F recovers — as a standing form of land holding, co-equal with and not transitional to private or state property. This dissolves, at the constitutional level, the false menu that the property-as-engineered-bundle framing presents, and it does so without absorbing the Indigenous land relation into the commons frame: common right is one recognized form, the Indigenous land relation is engaged on its own distinct terms, and the architecture holds both rather than collapsing either.

The fourth condition is stewardship obligation as constitutive of any land relation. The constitutional architecture must specify that every land relation it recognizes — private, common, Indigenous, public — carries, as a condition of the relation itself and not as an external regulatory limit, an obligation to the land's continuance and to those who will hold the relation after. This is the commitment that Thread B identified as surviving into the reconstructed property concept, that Leopold's criterion of integrity and Berry's tenure-in-trust articulate from the agrarian side, and that the Indigenous tradition's reciprocity and grounded normativity articulate at greater depth. Stewardship obligation as constitutive means that there is no land relation, under the cultivation framing, that is the absolute and unconditioned dominion the bundle-of-rights frame presents; obligation is built into the relation, not placed grudgingly upon it.

These four conditions are what this document specifies and what it hands forward. The document does not specify the bounded decision rights that would instantiate them, and it does not perform the integration that would assemble them into a working cultivation protocol; those are the work of the documents that follow, and Thread I names them precisely so that the work is correctly located. Document XII.09, on foundational decision rights, is where land-and-resource sovereignty — articulated at Indigenous primary-articulator standing in the foundational-normative inheritance of Document I.01 — is specified at constitutional weight as a bounded decision right; the scale-matched land jurisdiction and the Land Back commitment that this thread specifies as conditions are what Document XII.09 is being asked to instantiate as rights. Document XII.09's provisioning scale band, on provisioning decision rights, is where land as the provisioning substrate — the land that feeds, houses, and sustains the polity — is instantiated; the land-relation substrate this document specifies is what Document XII.09's provisioning scale band draws upon when it specifies the decision rights over provisioning, and the four conditions condition that specification. Document XIII.01, the decision-rights synthesis, is being asked to integrate the land-relation conditions specified here with the conditions specified across the rest of the arc into the cultivation protocol; this document names what XIII.01 must integrate from the land side and does not perform the integration. And Document XIV.01, the capstone synthesis, reads this document as the foundational-substrate document of Part VI — the document that specifies what land is, on which the food-and-soil analysis of Document VI.02, the energy analysis of Document VI.03, the materials analysis of Document VI.04, and the built-environment analysis of Document VI.05 each rest, since a foodway, an energy system, a materials economy, and a built environment are each a particular way of standing in relation to land, and the land relation is what this document has specified.

Land relations are the foundational substrate of the material life of the polity. A constitutional order that holds land as an alienable, commodified stock has mistaken the substrate of its biocultural life for an inventory of inputs, and the rest of Part VI inherits, from that mistake, the diagnostic burden its documents carry. The cultivation framing's alternative is the one this document has specified: land as cultivation terrain, held across nested scales, related to rather than owned in the design-imposition sense, with the Indigenous land-relations tradition at primary-articulator standing, the Land Back question held open as a live constitutional commitment, common right recognized as a standing form, and stewardship obligation constitutive of every land relation. What the constitutional architecture cultivates, on this specification, is not a land-relation blueprint but the conditions under which good land relations are grown — plurally, locally, across the scales and the generations — by the communities and the peoples whose lives are located in particular places. Grown, not built: of no part of the constitutional order is the project's summary phrase more exactly true than of the polity's relation to its land.
