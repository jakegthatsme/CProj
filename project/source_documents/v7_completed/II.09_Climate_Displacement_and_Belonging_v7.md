# Part One — Climate Displacement as a Cross-Regime Disturbance Category

## Thread A — The Six-Phenomenon Category, Documentary Archive, and Cross-Regime Character

Climate displacement is not a single phenomenon admitting a single diagnostic. It is a category of at least six distinct phenomena, each with its own dynamics, temporal signature, and population geography, unified by the cross-regime character through which each phenomenon operates and by the polity-formation question each phenomenon forces. The category must be held open at the analytical level the document carries, because the architectural response a polity cultivates to absorb the category must absorb the category and not any one phenomenon read as paradigmatic. The constitutional architecture the rest of the arc specifies cannot be calibrated against the dynamics of any single climate-displacement phenomenon — calibrated against drought displacement it will under-prepare for the threshold dynamics of livability-collapse heat-zone displacement; calibrated against coastal sea-level-rise displacement it will under-prepare for the acute-event recurrence dynamics of fire-regime-shift wildland-urban-interface displacement; calibrated against any of these it will under-prepare for the political-violence-amplification dynamics through which climate operates as a threat multiplier rather than a sole cause. Thread A's analytical work is to specify the six phenomena at the resolution the architectural specification requires, to identify the cross-regime character through which the category operates, and to engage the documentary archive that records the category critically rather than treating the archive as neutral data the architecture builds upon.

The first phenomenon is drought-induced agricultural-system collapse and the displacement it drives. The dynamics are slow-onset, cumulative, and frequently compounding. A region's agricultural production depends on the coupling between precipitation regimes, soil-moisture conditions, the genetic and management profile of the crops planted, the irrigation infrastructure available, the political-economic conditions under which producers operate, and the food-system architectures through which production reaches consumers. When the precipitation regime shifts — through declining seasonal rainfall, through shifting rainy-season timing, through increasing variability of rainfall events — the coupling breaks down, the agricultural production declines, and the producers, the agricultural laborers, and the populations that depend on the agricultural economy of the region are forced into successive choices about whether to remain, to migrate within the region, or to migrate beyond it. The Sahel and the Central American Dry Corridor supply the documented contemporary cases. The Sahel — the semi-arid band stretching across West and Central Africa from Senegal through Mauritania, Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, and Chad — has experienced declining rainfall, declining soil productivity, and accelerating displacement across the past four decades. The Central American Dry Corridor — running through Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, and Nicaragua — has experienced parallel dynamics, with substantial displacement of small-farm households toward urban centers within the region and, where the displaced households have lacked the conditions for urban absorption, onward toward the United States border. The drought-displacement dynamics in both regions are not adequately analyzed at the level of the proximate physical-environmental disturbance alone; they are constituted through the cross-regime coupling Thread A specifies, with political-economic conditions, food-system architectures, and population geographies all bearing on whether a given drought regime produces displacement at the scale and direction the contemporary record documents.

The second phenomenon is sea-level rise and coastal displacement. The dynamics combine slow-onset background change — the steady rise of mean sea level driven by thermal expansion of the oceans and by glacial-and-ice-sheet melting — with acute-event punctuation through storm surge, tidal flooding, and infrastructure failure. The low-lying deltas of South and Southeast Asia (the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna delta across Bangladesh and eastern India; the Mekong delta in Vietnam; the Irrawaddy delta in Myanmar) and the atoll states of the Pacific (Kiribati, Tuvalu, the Marshall Islands) and the Indian Ocean (the Maldives) constitute the populations most directly exposed. The dynamics differ across the cases. For the delta populations the coupling is between sea-level rise and the long-developed political-economic conditions of densely populated agricultural and fishing economies operating on land that is, in places, only a meter or two above mean sea level; the question is when and at what rate the land becomes uninhabitable, and how the populations affected are absorbed by the broader political-economic architectures of their respective states and regions. For the atoll states the dynamics threaten the territorial existence of the polity itself; the populations face the prospect not of relocation within a continuing national territory but of statelessness in a form Hannah Arendt's analysis (engaged in Thread C) did not anticipate but which her diagnostic framework illuminates with unusual force.

The third phenomenon is fire-regime shift and wildland-urban-interface displacement. The dynamics are acute-event, recurring, and amplifying. Fire regimes — the typical frequency, intensity, season, and spatial extent of fire in a given ecological community — have shifted across the past several decades in regions where the combination of rising temperatures, drying fuels, vegetation-community shifts driven by climate change, fire-suppression legacies that have accumulated fuel loads beyond the levels the prior regimes generated, and expansion of human settlement into fire-prone landscapes have together produced fire-regime conditions outside the historical envelope. The western North American record and the Mediterranean record across Europe, North Africa, and the Levant supply the documented contemporary cases. The displacement dynamics are partly acute (the immediate evacuation of populations from the path of fire and the post-fire displacement of populations whose homes have been destroyed) and partly cumulative (the gradual depopulation of zones rendered repeatedly fire-exposed, the inability of insurance and credit markets to operate in those zones, the resulting failure of the political-economic conditions for continued habitation). The wildland-urban-interface dynamics interact at substantial depth with the political-economic regime Document II.02's political-economic-regime section engages: the populations most exposed to fire are not randomly distributed across the political-economic landscape, and the post-fire recovery dynamics operate through political-economic architectures that systematically advantage some populations and disadvantage others.

The fourth phenomenon is water-scarcity displacement. The dynamics are slow-onset, basin-scale, and tightly coupled to political-economic conditions. The contemporary cases include the Colorado River basin across the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico, the Indus basin across Pakistan and India, the basins of the Aral Sea catchment across Central Asia, the Tigris-Euphrates basin across Turkey, Syria, and Iraq, and the basins of the eastern Mediterranean and southwest Asia more broadly. Water scarcity operates as a displacement driver through the breakdown of the agricultural, industrial, and urban systems that depend on water at the scales and reliabilities the prior basin regimes supplied. The political-economic dimension is constitutive: water-scarcity dynamics are mediated by the legal architectures of water allocation, the political architectures of transboundary water governance, and the political-economic architectures of which uses are prioritized when allocations are insufficient to meet aggregate demand. The displacement that follows is the displacement of populations whose continued habitation depended on water allocations the political-economic architecture has now removed or reduced.

The fifth phenomenon is livability-collapse heat-zone displacement. The dynamics are slow-onset background change with threshold-crossing dynamics that produce qualitative shifts in habitability when crossed. The wet-bulb temperature literature — the analytical work that specifies the conditions under which the combination of ambient air temperature and humidity exceeds the human body's capacity to dissipate metabolic heat through evaporative cooling, beyond which sustained exposure produces hyperthermia and, beyond longer exposures, death — supplies the analytical apparatus through which the threshold is specified. The wet-bulb temperature of 35°C has been identified in the climate-physiology literature as the threshold beyond which prolonged outdoor exposure becomes physiologically incompatible with continued human life for healthy adults; lower wet-bulb thresholds become incompatible for elderly, infant, ill, and otherwise vulnerable populations. The contemporary record documents wet-bulb conditions approaching or briefly exceeding the 35°C threshold in parts of the Persian Gulf coast, the Indus valley, and the eastern coast of India during the most extreme heat events of the past decade. The displacement dynamics, when the threshold begins to be crossed with sufficient regularity that habitation becomes physiologically infeasible, will be qualitatively different from the displacement dynamics of the other phenomena in the category: they will operate on populations whose continued habitation in their current locations has become biologically impossible, not merely economically or ecologically untenable.

The sixth phenomenon is political-violence climate-amplification displacement. The dynamics are climate-as-threat-multiplier rather than climate-as-sole-cause. Climate disturbance does not produce political violence in regions where political-economic, cultural, and historical conditions do not already supply the substrate for conflict; in regions where those conditions do supply the substrate, climate disturbance amplifies the conflict dynamics by intensifying resource competition, by stressing political-economic architectures already operating near their failure thresholds, and by providing precipitating conditions for the activation of conflict architectures already in place. The Syrian case has been engaged extensively in the literature as a worked example of these dynamics — the 2006–2010 drought, the displacement of agricultural populations toward urban centers, the political-economic and political-cultural conditions under which the urban absorption was inadequate, and the subsequent civil-conflict and refugee dynamics that have displaced millions of Syrians across the past decade and a half. The Sudanese case, the Yemeni case, and the broader pattern of climate-amplified conflict across the Sahel and the Horn of Africa supply additional empirical material. The analytical commitment Thread A maintains is that the climate-displacement dynamics of this phenomenon are not adequately analyzed as either purely climate-driven or purely conflict-driven; they are constituted through the cross-regime coupling between physical-environmental disturbance (Document II.02's physical-environmental-regime section), political-economic disturbance (Document II.02's political-economic-regime section), and the historical and cultural conditions through which conflict architectures are built and maintained.

The documentary archive through which the category is recorded operates principally through the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre's *Global Report on Internal Displacement* (published annually since 1998 by the Norwegian Refugee Council-affiliated IDMC) and the UNHCR climate-displacement and climate-refugee documentation (across a developing UNHCR corpus on climate-related movements and the UN High Commissioner for Refugees' broader engagement with the category). The archive supplies the principal contemporary empirical record of how the category's phenomena operate at scale, and it supplies the analytical categories — internally displaced person, refugee, climate refugee, displaced person, person of concern — through which the contemporary international system records and responds to the displacement. The archive is engaged here critically rather than as neutral data. The categories the IDMC and UNHCR use are themselves the product of the borders-and-sovereignty architecture Thread B engages: they distinguish internally displaced persons from refugees on the basis of whether an international border has been crossed; they distinguish refugees from migrants on the basis of whether the displacement is forced or voluntary; they distinguish climate displaced persons from refugees on the basis of whether the 1951 Refugee Convention definition applies. These distinctions are not analytical primitives that the architecture of climate-displacement response should be built around; they are juridical categories produced by the specific historical-political conditions of the post-1945 international refugee regime, and the architectural response to the category Thread A specifies cannot be calibrated to the juridical categories without inheriting the limitations the categories embed.

The cross-regime character of the category operates across all five disturbance regimes Documents II.02 through II.02's political-economic-regime section specify. The proximate driver is physical-environmental disturbance (Document II.02's physical-environmental-regime section): the changing precipitation regimes, the rising seas, the shifting fire regimes, the heat-livability thresholds. The structure determining who is displaced and who absorbs the cost is the political-economic regime (Document II.02's political-economic-regime section): the wealth-and-power distributions through which exposure is allocated, the political architectures through which response is organized or refused, the infrastructure architectures through which adaptation capacity is built or withheld. The regime through which the displacement is racialized and caste-sorted is the cultural disturbance regime (Document II.02): the populations most exposed are not randomly distributed across the cultural-disturbance axes the document engages, and the contemporary architecture of who counts as a sympathetic displaced person and who counts as a threatening migrant is constituted through caste, racial, religious, and embodied-exclusion dynamics that operate continuously on the displaced. The biological disturbance regime (Document II.02's biological-regime section) couples through food-system and disease dynamics: the displaced are exposed to nutritional deficits, to infectious disease pressures, and to the broader pathologies of acute and chronic displacement, with the dynamics interacting at depth with the political-economic and cultural regimes. And the cognitive-and-informational regime (Document II.02's cognitive-and-informational-regime section) bears through displacement-related disinformation: the "invasion" and "replacement" narratives, the manufactured racial-threat framings, the disinformation cascades through which political consent for border violence against the displaced is manufactured. Thread D engages the cognitive-informational dimension at the necropolitics-and-political-economy intersection where the disinformation operates; the cross-regime interactions Document II.08 engages at depth supply the analytical apparatus through which all five regime couplings operate, and Thread A cites Document II.08 for the interaction mechanics without re-deriving them.

# Part Two — The Borders-and-Sovereignty Diagnostic

## Thread B — The Case Against Borders-as-Engineered-Fixture Under the Cultivation Framing

The contemporary border-and-sovereignty architecture operates as a design-imposition fixture in the precise sense Document III.01 specifies the design-imposition framing: borders are treated as engineered, fixed, and prior to the polity rather than as cultivated, revisable, and scale-matched conditions of a biocultural formation. The architecture treats the border as if it were a settled feature of the world the polity inhabits, a feature the polity may fortify, monitor, or selectively open but whose existence and basic character are not themselves matters the polity may reconsider. Under the cultivation framing the document operates within, the border is a condition the polity cultivates — a condition with a specific genealogy, a specific contemporary architecture, and a range of possible alternative cultivations the polity may grow toward. The cultivation framing's analytical move on the border is not to deny that boundaries exist or that they have functions, but to refuse the design-imposition framing's claim that the boundary's specific contemporary character is prior to the polity rather than a product of the polity's choices and the cultivation conditions the polity has maintained or failed to maintain. Thread B builds the analytical case for this re-framing through the contemporary borders-and-sovereignty canon.

Wendy Brown's *Walled States, Waning Sovereignty* (Zone Books, 2010) is the principal contemporary analytical engagement with the proliferation of border walls and the political-philosophical question of what the proliferation signifies. Brown's analytical work proceeds from the empirical observation that the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries have seen a substantial increase in the construction of physical border walls between sovereign states — at the United States-Mexico border, at the Israeli border with the West Bank, at the borders of the European Union, at the India-Bangladesh border, and at numerous other sites — at the same period when the political-philosophical category of state sovereignty has been substantially eroded by the dynamics of globalized capital, transnational corporate power, transnational political-religious movements, and the broader conditions of late modernity. The analytical move Brown specifies is that the wall is not evidence of waxing sovereignty but of waning sovereignty: the wall is theatrical compensation for the state's declining capacity to control the flows — of capital, of information, of populations, of political-religious movements, of environmental conditions — that constitute its contemporary operating environment. The wall operates principally at the symbolic register, as a performance of sovereign control in the absence of the actual capacity for the control performed; it operates secondarily at the material register, as architecture that produces specific violence against the populations that attempt to cross it without producing the controlled flow it advertises.

Brown's analysis is foundational for Thread B's diagnostic because it specifies the contemporary border-as-fixture as a symptom of architectural failure rather than as the natural condition of sovereign states. The wall is not the expression of a polity confident in its membership and its capacity to cultivate the conditions under which its membership operates; it is the expression of a polity in which the architecture of membership has failed to develop the conditions under which population movement can be absorbed without theatrical violence. The cultivation framing recovers this analytical commitment by specifying the border as something a polity grows the conditions for, that operates as one cultivated and revisable feature of a biocultural formation among others, rather than as the prior fixture against which the polity defines itself. The wall, on Brown's analysis and on the cultivation framing's extension, is not the border at its strongest but at its weakest: a polity that requires walls to enact its membership is a polity that has failed to cultivate the conditions under which membership could operate without them.

Reece Jones's *Violent Borders: Refugees and the Right to Move* (Verso, 2016) and *White Borders: The History of Race and Immigration in the United States from Chinese Exclusion to the Border Wall* (Beacon Press, 2021) supply the analytical apparatus through which the contemporary border is read as a site of state violence and through which the racial genealogy of the United States border regime in particular is traced. The analytical move Jones specifies in *Violent Borders* is that the contemporary border regime kills people, in numbers that constitute one of the principal contemporary forms of state-administered violence against unarmed populations, and that the killings are not accidental features of an otherwise humane architecture but the architecture's substantive operation. Jones documents the empirical record of deaths at borders worldwide — the Mediterranean crossings between North Africa and Europe, the Sonoran Desert crossings between Mexico and the United States, the Bay of Bengal crossings, the southern African crossings, and the others — with the analytical finding that the cumulative annual death toll across the global border regime exceeds the death toll of most contemporary armed conflicts and is approached only by the most severe contemporary wars. The right to move that Jones articulates is not a right that the contemporary international regime recognizes; it is a right the analytical apparatus of the document advocates for as a foundational rights commitment the contemporary regime has failed to articulate.

Jones's *White Borders* extends the analytical apparatus into the racial genealogy of the United States border regime. The United States border with Mexico, in its contemporary form as a heavily militarized line of fences, walls, sensors, drones, surveillance towers, and patrolling agents, is not the natural expression of a long-existing line between two nations; it is the product of a specific historical-political genealogy that runs through the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 (the first federal law to bar immigration on explicitly racial grounds), through the National Origins Act of 1924 (which institutionalized a racial-quota system designed to preserve the demographic composition of the United States as it had been established in earlier waves of European immigration), through the Bracero Program and its termination, through the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (which formally removed the racial-origins quotas but which was followed almost immediately by the Hart-Celler Act's caps on Western Hemisphere immigration and by the subsequent Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 that began the contemporary militarization), through Operation Gatekeeper and Operation Hold the Line and the broader 1990s border-enforcement build-up, and through the post-2001 incorporation of border enforcement into the national-security architecture under the Department of Homeland Security. The contemporary border regime is the cumulative product of more than a century of racial-political choices, each of which shaped the architecture in specific ways and each of which the contemporary architecture continues to operate under. Jones's analytical apparatus supplies Thread B with the genealogical depth the cultivation re-framing requires: the border-as-fixture is not the natural condition the polity has always operated under but a specific cultivated condition the polity has built through specific historical choices, and the cultivation framing's move toward a different cultivation of the border condition operates against the genealogy Jones documents rather than against an undifferentiated naturalized fixture.

Catherine Dauvergne's *Making People Illegal: What Globalization Means for Migration and Law* (Cambridge University Press, 2008) and *The New Politics of Immigration and the End of Settler Societies* (Cambridge University Press, 2016) supply the analytical apparatus for understanding the contemporary juridical production of illegality as a category of human existence. The analytical move Dauvergne specifies in *Making People Illegal* is that the legal category of the illegal migrant is not a long-existing juridical category that has continuously applied to populations who cross borders without authorization; it is a contemporary category produced by the specific interaction between the dynamics of globalization and the responses sovereign states have made to those dynamics through immigration law. As capital, information, goods, and certain populations (the wealthy, the professionally qualified, the holders of investor visas and skilled-worker permits) have been made increasingly free to cross borders, other populations (the unskilled, the displaced, the poor, the racially marginalized) have been increasingly criminalized for crossing the same borders; the asymmetry is the substantive content of contemporary immigration law, and the production of illegality is the principal architectural mechanism through which the asymmetry operates. Dauvergne's analytical apparatus supplies Thread B with the recognition that the population the border regime treats as its principal threat is a population the border regime itself has produced through the juridical operations of immigration law, and that an alternative cultivation of the border condition would operate in part by not producing this population in the first instance.

Dauvergne's *The New Politics of Immigration and the End of Settler Societies* extends the analytical apparatus into the comparative analysis of how the major settler-colonial states (the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand) have responded to contemporary migration dynamics in ways that have substantially undone the founding-period self-understanding of these states as settler societies open to continued immigration. The settler-society self-understanding had operated, across the nineteenth and much of the twentieth centuries, as one of the foundational political identities of these states; immigration was constitutive of the polity, and continued immigration was assumed as a permanent feature of the political-economic operation. The late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries have seen this self-understanding substantially undone, with the settler-society states now operating border regimes that treat continued immigration as a problem to be managed and minimized rather than as constitutive of the polity. The analytical move Thread B carries forward from this analysis is that the contemporary border-as-fixture framing is not merely a denial of the cultivation framing the multi-traditional foundational inheritance articulates; it is also a denial of the settler-society polities' own founding self-understanding, with the denial accomplished through a specific recent political-cultural shift the diagnostic must engage rather than treat as a natural condition.

Étienne Balibar's *Politics and the Other Scene* (Verso, 2002) supplies the analytical apparatus through which the border is read as an institution dispersed throughout the polity rather than located only at the territorial edge. The analytical move Balibar specifies is that the contemporary border is not adequately analyzed as a line at the territorial perimeter; it is an institution that operates at multiple sites throughout the polity — at the workplace where employer-verification systems check work authorization; at the housing market where landlord-verification systems check residence authorization; at the educational institution where enrollment-verification systems check eligibility; at the medical facility where coverage-verification systems sort patients by juridical status; at the financial institution where account-opening systems check identification; at the traffic stop where law-enforcement systems check immigration status; at the airport where security architectures sort passengers by national origin; at numerous other sites the contemporary architecture has elaborated. The border is dispersed throughout the polity, and the population subjected to border enforcement is not principally the population at the territorial line but the population throughout the polity whose juridical status the dispersed border architecture continuously checks. Balibar's analytical apparatus supplies Thread B with the recognition that an alternative cultivation of the border condition must operate not only at the territorial line but throughout the polity, by re-cultivating the conditions under which the dispersed border architecture currently operates and by withdrawing the institutional infrastructure that has been built to sort populations by juridical status across the spaces of contemporary life.

What survives of the border concept under the cultivation framing, and what does not, is the analytical question Thread B closes on. What survives is the recognition that polities have boundaries — that the conditions under which a biocultural formation cultivates itself include conditions about who is a member of the formation and on what terms, conditions about how new members are absorbed and on what timelines, conditions about the relationships between the formation and the formations adjacent to it. The cultivation framing does not deny that boundaries exist or that they have functions; it specifies that the boundaries are themselves cultivated conditions, revisable across the temporal horizons over which cultivation operates, and that the boundaries' specific contemporary architecture is not prior to the polity but a product of the polity's choices. What does not survive of the contemporary border concept is the design-imposition framing's specific cluster of commitments: the framing of borders as fixtures rather than as cultivated conditions; the framing of borders as territorial lines rather than as dispersed institutional architectures; the framing of borders as natural expressions of national sovereignty rather than as theatrical compensation for sovereignty's contemporary weaknesses; the framing of borders as architectures of legitimate state violence rather than as architectures whose violence is itself a symptom of architectural failure. Thread B does not specify the alternative cultivation in this thread — the closing cultivation specification is Thread I — but it specifies what the existing architecture is and what an alternative must not reproduce.

Thread B must not romanticize borderlessness as design-imposition in the opposite direction. The cultivation framing specifies conditions, not the abolition of all boundary; it specifies the revisable, scale-matched, cultivated character of boundary conditions, not their imposition or their abolition by architectural fiat. The mirror-image error — arguing for the abolition of borders as a design solution to the problems the contemporary border architecture produces — would operate within the same design-imposition framing the diagnostic rejects, simply with the architectural specification reversed. The cultivation framing's commitment is that the polity grows the conditions under which population movement can be absorbed and under which membership can be cultivated, and that the architecture must specify those cultivation conditions rather than impose either the wall or its absence. Thread H returns to this analytical commitment at depth; Thread B specifies the negative case (against borders-as-fixture) and leaves the positive cultivation specification to Threads H and I.

## Thread C — Statelessness and the Right-to-Have-Rights Tradition

Hannah Arendt's *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (Harcourt, Brace, 1951) supplies the foundational analytical engagement with statelessness as the constitutional condition that exposes the dependence of human rights on national membership. The analysis Thread C engages is the one Arendt develops in Chapter Nine of the book, "The Decline of the Nation-State and the End of the Rights of Man." Arendt's analytical work in that chapter proceeds from the empirical observation that the interwar period produced, for the first time in modern history, a substantial population of stateless persons — Jews expelled from or fleeing Germany and Austria, Armenians displaced by the genocide, Russians displaced by the Revolution, Spanish republicans displaced by the Civil War, and the broader population of denaturalized and stateless persons the interwar political dynamics generated. The stateless population could not be returned to a state of origin (the state of origin had denaturalized them or had ceased to exist), could not be assimilated into the states where they were physically present (those states had no juridical category that would admit them to membership), and could not be deported to any other state (no other state would receive them). The stateless person occupied a juridical condition for which the international system of nation-states had no place.

The analytical move Arendt specifies in the chapter is that the condition of statelessness exposed the dependence of human rights on national membership in a way that the philosophical tradition of human rights had not anticipated and could not adequately address. The "rights of man" had been articulated, from the French Declaration onward, as rights that belonged to human beings by virtue of their humanity, independent of any political community, antecedent to any state. When the rights came to be tested by the condition of human beings who had been stripped of national membership, the rights proved to be unavailable: the stateless person had no state to invoke the rights against, no political community to make them operational, no institutional infrastructure that would honor them. The "right to have rights" — the right to belong to a political community in which other rights become operative — emerged as the foundational right the human-rights tradition had not articulated. The stateless person, Arendt writes, "could live and die without leaving any trace, without having contributed anything to the common world"; the rights the philosophical tradition had promised to all human beings turned out to require, as their precondition, the membership in a political community the stateless person had been denied.

Arendt's analytical commitment is foundational for Thread C's engagement with the contemporary climate-displacement category. The climate-displaced person frequently does not cross an international border; the displacement is, in the IDMC's juridical category, internal rather than international. The 1951 Refugee Convention definition — a person outside the country of nationality who is unable or unwilling to return because of well-founded fear of persecution on grounds of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion — does not extend to climate displacement. The climate-displaced person who has not crossed an international border is not a refugee under the Convention; the climate-displaced person who has crossed an international border but whose displacement is climate-driven rather than persecution-driven is similarly not a refugee under the Convention. The contemporary juridical architecture for the climate-displaced therefore operates principally through the categories of internally displaced person (without the international-protection regime that applies to refugees), of irregular migrant (with the criminalization and exposure to border violence that contemporary irregular migration carries), or of person fully outside any protection regime. The climate-displaced person is, increasingly, in the juridical condition Arendt diagnosed: the condition in which the philosophical promises of human rights cannot be operationalized because the political community within which they would become operative has not extended membership.

The "right to have rights" is the foundational articulation Thread C carries forward into the document's positive analytical work. Belonging, on Arendt's analytical commitment as Thread C reads it, is not a status the polity confers as one privilege among others; it is the precondition of having any rights at all in operational form. A polity that does not extend membership to a population physically present within it is not preserving its own membership unmuddied; it is producing a population whose rights cannot be operationalized, and the production is itself the substantive content of the polity's relationship to that population. The contemporary climate-displacement category, in its scale and in its trajectory, threatens to generalize the stateless condition Arendt diagnosed — to produce populations, at scales that may reach into the hundreds of millions over the coming century, whose juridical condition is precisely the one in which the philosophical promises of human rights cannot be operationalized because the political community within which they would become operative has not extended membership.

The analytical bridge to the foundational-rights architecture of Document XII.09 is foundational rather than incidental. Document XII.09 specifies the foundational decision rights at biospheric scale that the constitutional architecture must enable, and the right to have rights is the foundational right at that scale: the right to belong to a political community in which other rights become operative. The right is not, on Arendt's analytical commitment, a right to membership in any specific nation-state, nor a right to choose one's nation-state of membership, nor a right to abolish nation-states; it is the foundational right to belong to a political community at all, the right whose violation produces the stateless condition and whose contemporary architectural failure produces the climate-displaced populations the rest of Document II.09 engages. Thread I returns to the bridge at depth; Thread C specifies the foundational articulation.

The analytical commitment Thread C maintains across its engagement with Arendt is that the engagement does not absorb Arendt's analytical apparatus into a contemporary border-and-sovereignty diagnostic she did not herself conduct. Arendt's analytical work in *The Origins of Totalitarianism* engaged the interwar European stateless populations and the dynamics that produced them; the contemporary climate-displacement category operates under different conditions and produces different populations. Thread C engages Arendt's diagnostic for the foundational analytical insight it supplies — that human rights require, as their precondition, the political community within which they become operative — and carries that insight forward into the contemporary analytical work. The engagement is at the level of the foundational right; it is not at the level of the specific political dynamics Arendt diagnosed. The contemporary analytical work has its own dynamics, its own populations, and its own architectural questions, and Thread C engages Arendt as the principal philosophical articulator of the foundational right rather than as the principal analyst of the contemporary dynamics.

## Thread D — Necropolitics, Expulsions, and the Political Economy of Border Infrastructure

The border regime operates as a necropolitical architecture, in the precise sense Achille Mbembe specifies the concept. Mbembe's *Necropolitics* (Duke University Press, 2019), drawing on and extending his earlier *On the Postcolony* (University of California Press, 2001) and the foundational essay "Necropolitics" (*Public Culture*, 2003), develops the analytical apparatus through which the operations of contemporary state power are read at the level of the distribution of exposure to death rather than principally at the level of the regulation of populations through biopolitical management. The analytical move Mbembe specifies in *Necropolitics* is that the principal contemporary architectures of state power operate by deciding which lives are grievable and which are abandonable; by producing zones of abandonment in which populations are left exposed to death-producing conditions without the protections the polity extends to other populations; by sustaining sovereignty through the capacity to expose populations to death rather than principally through the protection of those populations' lives. The necropolitical architecture is, on Mbembe's analytical commitment, the principal contemporary form of sovereign power, and the border regime is one of its principal contemporary sites.

The border regime operates necropolitically through several architectural mechanisms Thread D engages. The first is the spatial production of zones in which exposure to death is the principal condition the populations attempting to cross face. The Sonoran Desert between Sonora and Arizona is the most extensively documented contemporary case: the United States Border Patrol's prevention-through-deterrence strategy, articulated in the 1994 Border Patrol Strategic Plan and operationalized through the buildup of enforcement infrastructure at the urban crossing points of San Diego, El Paso, and other principal cities, deliberately channeled migration flows into the desert spaces between the urbanized crossings on the explicit calculation that the desert's hostility would deter the attempt. The deterrence did not occur; the flows shifted into the desert, and the desert killed the migrants. Jason De León's *The Land of Open Graves: Living and Dying on the Migrant Trail* (University of California Press, 2015) supplies the principal contemporary ethnographic-anthropological account of the desert deaths, with the analytical finding that the Border Patrol strategy's death-producing effects were not unanticipated consequences of an otherwise humane policy but constitutive content of the policy itself. The Mediterranean crossings between North Africa and the European Union supply the parallel contemporary case at the larger scale: the European Union's externalization of border enforcement to Libya, the Italian and Greek coast-guard operations that have systematically failed to rescue migrants in distress at sea, the cumulative annual death toll at the central Mediterranean route that has, across the past decade, exceeded any other contemporary borderscape in absolute terms. The necropolitical architecture of these crossings is not a series of failures of an otherwise humane regime; the failures are the regime's substantive operation.

The second mechanism is the production of zones of abandonment within the territorial space of the receiving states. The populations of irregular migrants who have crossed the border successfully but who operate within the territorial space without juridical status occupy a condition Mbembe's analytical apparatus illuminates: they are present within the polity but excluded from the protections the polity extends to its members, exposed to labor exploitation through the impossibility of recourse to labor protections, exposed to housing exploitation through the impossibility of recourse to tenant protections, exposed to direct violence through the inability to seek police protection without exposure to deportation, exposed to the gradual erosion of physical and mental health through the cumulative effects of these exposures across years and decades. The zones of abandonment are not spatially distinct from the rest of the polity; they are produced within the polity by the juridical architecture that withholds membership from the populations within them. The third mechanism is the production of detention regimes that operate as necropolitical sites at the boundary between the spatial zones and the zones of abandonment. The United States immigration-detention architecture, the European Union's externalized detention sites in Libya and elsewhere, the Australian offshore-detention regime on Nauru and Manus Island — these architectures operate as sites in which the necropolitical character of the border regime is concentrated, with the populations detained exposed to conditions whose cumulative effects on physical and mental health constitute a substantive distribution of exposure to death and to forms of injury approaching it.

Saskia Sassen's *Expulsions: Brutality and Complexity in the Global Economy* (Harvard University Press / Belknap, 2014) supplies the analytical apparatus through which the necropolitical operations of the border regime are read at the systemic political-economic level. The analytical move Sassen specifies in *Expulsions* is that the contemporary global economy operates through systemic expulsions — of populations from the spaces of the global economy through the dynamics of land grabbing, financial dispossession, environmental destruction, debt-driven displacement, and the broader political-economic conditions that have, across the past several decades, produced populations whose continued presence within the economic spaces of the global economy is no longer required by the economic logic the economy operates under. The expulsions are not the same as the historical-economic dynamics through which workers were continuously displaced and re-employed; they are dynamics in which populations are expelled from the economic spaces without being absorbed into new economic spaces, with the consequence that the expelled populations occupy a condition outside the economic dynamics the contemporary global economy operates under. The climate-displaced populations the document engages operate, in many cases, within the systemic dynamics Sassen specifies: they have been expelled from the agricultural, coastal, and habitable spaces in which they previously lived, and the political-economic spaces of the contemporary global economy do not extend the conditions for their absorption into new economic spaces.

Sassen's earlier *Territory, Authority, Rights: From Medieval to Global Assemblages* (Princeton University Press, 2006) supplies the longer historical analytical apparatus through which the contemporary territory-authority-rights assemblage is traced. The analytical work specifies that the contemporary nation-state-based architecture of territory, authority, and rights is a specific historical assemblage that emerged through the long political-economic and political-philosophical developments of the early modern and modern periods; the assemblage is not a natural condition but a specific historical formation, and the contemporary period is one in which the assemblage is being substantially reconfigured by the dynamics of globalization, transnational capital, and the broader conditions Sassen's analytical work engages. The border regime is one of the principal sites at which the reconfiguration is being conducted; the cultivation framing's analytical move on the border is one alternative reconfiguration the historical record of the contemporary period makes legible as an analytical possibility.

Harsha Walia's *Border and Rule: Global Migration, Capitalism, and the Rise of Racist Nationalism* (Haymarket Books, 2021) supplies the analytical apparatus through which the contemporary border regime is read as constitutive of global capitalism rather than as incidental to it. The analytical move Walia specifies is that the border regime is not a regrettable feature of an otherwise unified global capitalist economy; it is the architecture through which the global capitalist economy continues to operate at the contemporary scale. The border regime sorts populations into those whose labor the global economy absorbs at the wage rates and under the protections of the receiving-state labor markets, those whose labor the global economy absorbs at the substantially lower wage rates and under the substantially weaker protections of the irregular-migrant labor markets within the receiving states, and those whose labor the global economy does not absorb at all and whose populations are therefore subject to the necropolitical operations of the border regime at its most violent sites. The sorting is the constitutive content of how the global economy operates at the contemporary scale; the border regime supplies the architectural mechanism through which the sorting is conducted. Walia's analytical apparatus supplies Thread D with the recognition that the border regime cannot be reformed within the existing global political-economic architecture without addressing the political-economic architecture itself, because the border regime is constitutive of the architecture rather than incidental to it.

The political economy of border infrastructure is the substantive content through which the necropolitical and expulsion dynamics are materially built. The contractors who design and construct border walls, fences, surveillance towers, and sensor networks; the detention industry that operates the immigration-detention facilities under contract to receiving-state governments; the surveillance-technology vendors that supply the biometric, drone, and data-analytic systems on which the contemporary border regime depends; the political-action committees and lobbying organizations through which the contractors, detention operators, and technology vendors influence the political dynamics that fund and direct the border regime — these constitute a substantive political-economic infrastructure whose continued profitability depends on the continued expansion and operation of the border regime in approximately its current form. Document II.03's analysis of the capture architecture through which oligarchic political-economic power influences contemporary American political decision-making supplies the analytical apparatus through which the political-economic infrastructure of the border regime is engaged; Thread D cites Document II.03 for the capture architecture and engages the border-specific dimension of the capture as the substantive content through which the necropolitical and expulsion dynamics of the border regime are politically entrenched and materially built.

The cognitive-and-informational dimension of the border regime is the substantive content through which political consent for the necropolitical operations is manufactured. The "invasion" and "replacement" narratives — the framing of migration, and increasingly of climate-driven migration specifically, as an invasion of the receiving polity by populations whose presence threatens the demographic composition, the cultural integrity, or the economic resources of the receiving polity — operate as the principal contemporary framing through which political consent for border violence against the displaced is manufactured. The narratives operate through the cognitive-informational architecture Document II.02's cognitive-and-informational-regime section engages: the algorithmic amplification of racial-threat content through platform-mediated information distribution, the production of disinformation cascades through coordinated political-economic networks, the cumulative effect of these dynamics across years and decades in shaping the cognitive dispositions through which receiving-state populations encounter the displaced and the political dynamics through which border policy is determined. The "great replacement" framing — articulated initially in the contemporary form by Renaud Camus in *Le Grand Remplacement* (2011), elaborated by far-right political movements across Europe and North America, and operationalized in the rhetorical apparatus of political campaigns and political-media organizations across the contemporary period — has become, across the past decade, the principal cognitive-informational framing through which climate-displacement-related disinformation is organized. The framing operates by combining racial-threat narratives with demographic-decline anxieties to produce a cognitive disposition under which the receiving-state population perceives the displaced as a fundamental threat to the polity's continued existence and under which the necropolitical operations of the border regime can be politically sustained as a defensive necessity.

The empirical record of the cognitive-informational dynamics is substantial. The Christchurch mosque attacks of March 15, 2019, in which a far-right terrorist killed fifty-one people in two New Zealand mosques, were conducted under an explicit "great replacement" framing the attacker articulated in his manifesto. The Buffalo supermarket attack of May 14, 2022, in which a far-right terrorist killed ten Black shoppers at a grocery store in a predominantly Black neighborhood of Buffalo, New York, was conducted under the same framing, with the attacker's manifesto explicitly invoking the "great replacement" theory and citing climate-related migration as one of the dynamics the framing identified as constitutive of the threat. The El Paso Walmart attack of August 3, 2019, in which a far-right terrorist killed twenty-three people at a Walmart in a predominantly Latino neighborhood of El Paso, Texas, was conducted under the same framing, with the attacker's manifesto explicitly identifying Hispanic immigration as a constitutive threat to the receiving polity. These attacks are not isolated incidents of individual extremist violence; they are events at the violent tail of a continuous distribution of political-cognitive-informational dynamics through which the displacement-related disinformation operates at scale. The dynamics produce the political conditions under which the necropolitical operations of the border regime can be sustained, and they produce, at the violent tail, the direct attacks on populations the framing identifies as the threat.

Thread D's engagement with the cognitive-informational dimension cross-references Document II.02's cognitive-and-informational-regime section for the disinformation mechanics — the platform-mediated information-distribution architecture, the algorithmic amplification dynamics, the coordinated disinformation production through coordinated political-economic networks — and does not re-derive them. Document II.09's contribution is to specify how the mechanism operates specifically on the climate-displaced: the displacement-related disinformation operates through the same architectural mechanisms the broader disinformation dynamics operate through, with the specific content adapted to the climate-displacement category through the "invasion" and "replacement" framings the contemporary period has elaborated. The cumulative effect on the political conditions under which the border regime operates is to sustain the necropolitical and expulsion dynamics Threads D and Sassen specify; the cumulative effect on the populations targeted by the dynamics is the direct violence the empirical record documents.

The political-economic and cognitive-informational dimensions Thread D engages are constitutive of the contemporary border regime, not contingent features of an otherwise architecturally settled regime. The capture architecture supplies the political-economic conditions under which the regime is funded and built; the disinformation architecture supplies the cognitive-political conditions under which the regime is politically sustained; the necropolitical and expulsion dynamics supply the architectural mechanisms through which the regime operates on the populations it acts upon. An alternative cultivation of the border condition under the framing Thread B specifies must operate against all three dimensions: it must address the capture architecture (the work Documents II.03 and XII.09's public-check scale band engage at depth), the disinformation architecture (the work Documents II.02's cognitive-and-informational-regime section and XII.09's public-check scale band engage at depth), and the necropolitical operations of the regime itself (the work the foundational-rights architecture of Document XII.09 anchors). Thread I returns to the architectural specification at the closing; Thread D specifies the architectural-political content of the contemporary border regime that the alternative cultivation must address.

## Thread E — Indigenous Sovereignty, the Border, and Climate Colonialism

The Indigenous-sovereignty tradition stands at primary-articulator standing in the analytical engagement Document II.09 conducts. The tradition is not engaged as illustrative supplement to the Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon of Threads B and D; it is engaged as the primary articulation of the polity-formation question the diagnostic forces, with the Euro-American canon read as recent and partial recovery of the analytical content the Indigenous-sovereignty tradition articulates from longer and more concrete experience. The engagement is conducted under the methodological discipline Document VII.01 specifies — the multi-eyed-seeing commitment under which Indigenous and Eastern analytical traditions are engaged in their own registers rather than being absorbed into the analytical apparatus of the Western canons.

Audra Simpson (Mohawk) is the principal contemporary articulator of the analytical engagement Thread E carries forward. Her *Mohawk Interruptus: Political Life Across the Borders of Settler States* (Duke University Press, 2014) articulates, at the depth the analytical work requires, the political life of the Kahnawà:ke Mohawk Nation across the contemporary period in which the international border between Canada and the United States runs directly through the territory and political life of the continuing Haudenosaunee Confederacy of which Kahnawà:ke is a member. The analytical move Simpson specifies is that the border is, from the Mohawk political-life vantage Simpson articulates, an instrument of settler-colonial dispossession rather than a natural feature of the geopolitical world. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy preceded the Canadian and the United States polities by centuries; the international border between Canada and the United States was drawn by the Treaty of Paris of 1783 and the Jay Treaty of 1794 directly through Haudenosaunee territory without the consent of the Haudenosaunee; the contemporary border regime operates continuously on Haudenosaunee political life by sorting Haudenosaunee persons into Canadian and United States juridical categories that the Haudenosaunee themselves do not recognize. The analytical apparatus the contemporary Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon operates under takes the border between Canada and the United States as a settled geopolitical feature against which the analytical work proceeds; from the Haudenosaunee political-life vantage Simpson articulates, the border is the contemporary form of the colonial dispossession the Haudenosaunee Confederacy has resisted for more than two centuries and continues to resist.

Refusal is the substantive political and analytical practice Simpson articulates as the Haudenosaunee response to the settler-state's terms of recognition. Refusal, on Simpson's analytical commitment, is not the rejection of political life in favor of withdrawal from it; it is the political-philosophical practice of refusing the settler-state's claim to be the authority through which Haudenosaunee political life is constituted and recognized, while continuing to conduct Haudenosaunee political life through the Haudenosaunee constitutional architecture (the Kaianerekowa, the Great Law of Peace) the Confederacy has carried across the centuries of settler-colonial dispossession. The Kahnawà:ke decisions across the contemporary period — the decisions about membership in the Mohawk Nation, the decisions about who is and is not a member of the political community, the decisions about how the political community engages or refuses to engage with the Canadian and United States polities — operate under Haudenosaunee political authority that the settler-state's claim to be the constituting authority does not undo. Refusal is, on Simpson's analytical commitment, itself a sovereign act: the assertion of Haudenosaunee political authority against the settler-state's claim of paramount authority, conducted not by armed resistance but by the substantive maintenance of Haudenosaunee political institutions and the substantive refusal of the settler-state's authority to constitute Haudenosaunee political life.

Simpson's analytical apparatus supplies Thread E with the analytical content the Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon does not articulate. The Euro-American canon engages the border as a feature of the political-geographical world whose contemporary architecture is a matter for analytical engagement; it does not engage the border as a colonial instrument whose contemporary architecture is the contemporary form of a centuries-long dispossession. The Euro-American canon engages sovereignty as an analytical category whose contemporary erosion (Brown) or constitutive functions (Walia, Mbembe, Sassen) are matters for analytical engagement; it does not engage sovereignty as a contested category between settler states and the Indigenous nations whose political life the settler-state's sovereignty claim continuously contests. The analytical work Simpson conducts operates from inside the Haudenosaunee political life the contemporary border regime acts upon; the Euro-American canon operates from outside that political life, with analytical apparatuses developed within the Euro-American intellectual traditions and applied to the political-philosophical questions the border raises within those traditions. Thread E's engagement with Simpson maintains the analytical priority Simpson's primary-articulator standing requires: Simpson's articulation is engaged as the primary articulation of the analytical content, and the Euro-American canon is engaged as recent and partial recovery of analytical commitments Simpson's tradition has articulated from longer and more concrete experience.

Kyle Powys Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi) is the principal contemporary articulator of the analytical engagement with climate change as colonial continuation. His "Indigenous Climate Change Studies: Indigenizing Futures, Decolonizing the Anthropocene" (*English Language Notes* 55, nos. 1–2, 2017: 153–162) supplies the foundational articulation of the analytical commitment Thread E carries forward, and his broader corpus across the past decade — including "Is It Colonial Déjà Vu? Indigenous Peoples and Climate Injustice" (in *Humanities for the Environment: Integrating Knowledge, Forging New Constellations of Practice*, Routledge, 2017), "Indigenous Science (Fiction) for the Anthropocene: Ancestral Dystopias and Fantasies of Climate Change Crises" (*Environment and Planning E: Nature and Space* 1, no. 1–2, 2018: 224–242), and "Settler Colonialism, Ecology, and Environmental Injustice" (*Environment and Society* 9, 2018: 125–144) — develops the analytical commitment at the depth the climate-displacement diagnostic requires.

The analytical move Whyte specifies is that climate change, as it is being experienced by Indigenous peoples across the contemporary period, is not a novel and separate phenomenon from the longer history of colonial dispossession; it is the contemporary continuation of dispossession dynamics the Indigenous nations of North America have experienced for more than five centuries. The dispossession of Indigenous lands through the original colonial appropriations, the dispossession of Indigenous lifeways through the assimilationist policies of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the dispossession of Indigenous lands through the allotment policies and the boarding-school architectures, the dispossession of Indigenous water through the irrigation-and-dam architectures of the twentieth century, the dispossession of Indigenous food systems through the broader political-economic dynamics of the twentieth century — these are not separate phenomena from the climate-displacement dynamics the contemporary period engages. They are the prior phases of the same long colonial dispossession that climate change is now extending into the contemporary phase. The Indigenous nations of North America, on Whyte's analytical commitment, have already experienced multiple comprehensive dispossessions of the kind the contemporary climate-displacement category threatens to produce at the broader scale; the analytical resources for engaging the contemporary dynamics are present in the Indigenous experience of the prior dispossessions, and the contemporary engagement with climate displacement must operate through engagement with the Indigenous analytical apparatus rather than through the Euro-American climate-change apparatus that has only recently begun to recognize the dynamics the Indigenous nations have engaged for centuries.

The "ancestral dystopias" framing Whyte develops in the 2018 *Environment and Planning E* article supplies the analytical apparatus through which the contemporary climate-future is read against the Indigenous experience of the prior dispossessions. The dystopian futures the contemporary climate-change literature anticipates — populations displaced by environmental change, ecological systems destabilized beyond recognition, political-economic architectures stressed by the cumulative effects of the dynamics — are not futures the Indigenous nations face for the first time. The Indigenous nations have, across the past five centuries, experienced versions of these dynamics already: populations displaced by colonial-environmental change, ecological systems destabilized beyond recognition by the colonial introductions and removals, political-economic architectures destroyed and replaced by colonial impositions. The contemporary climate-future is, from the Indigenous experience Whyte articulates, an ancestral dystopia for the broader contemporary population rather than a novel future the contemporary population faces for the first time. The analytical-political implication is that the Indigenous analytical apparatus, developed across centuries of engagement with the dynamics the broader contemporary population is now beginning to face, supplies analytical resources for the contemporary engagement that the Euro-American climate-change apparatus is recovering only partially and only recently.

The analytical apparatus Thread E carries forward from Whyte is that the analytical separation between climate-displaced populations and colonially-dispossessed populations is a category error of contemporary climate-displacement diagnostic; the two are not separate analytical categories. The colonially-dispossessed Indigenous nations of North America are climate-displaced populations in the most foundational sense — populations whose continued occupation of their lands has been continuously stressed across the centuries of colonial environmental change and whose contemporary occupation is being further stressed by the climate dynamics the colonial political-economic order has produced. The climate-displaced populations the contemporary period is producing at the broader scale — the populations displaced by drought, by sea-level rise, by fire-regime shift, by water scarcity, by livability collapse, by political-violence amplification — are populations entering the dynamics the Indigenous nations have engaged across centuries. The analytical engagement with the contemporary climate-displacement category must therefore proceed not by treating the Indigenous experience as one comparative case among others but by recognizing the Indigenous experience as the primary articulation of the analytical dynamics, with the contemporary expansion of the category as the broader contemporary manifestation of the dynamics the Indigenous nations have engaged from longer experience.

The methodological discipline Document VII.01 specifies is constitutive of how Thread E conducts the engagement. The engagement with Simpson and Whyte operates at primary-articulator standing — with the analytical content of their work engaged in its own analytical register, with tribal affiliation explicitly recorded (Simpson as Mohawk, Whyte as Citizen Potawatomi), with the analytical authority of their work treated as primary rather than supplementary. The engagement does not absorb their work into the Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon of Threads B and D or into the broader political-philosophical apparatus of the document; it operates them as the primary articulation of the analytical content the document carries forward at the polity-formation level. The cross-reference to Document III.01's primary-articulator engagement with the Indigenous-sovereignty tradition is operational: Document III.01 engages the constitutive Indigenous tradition at the constitutional-ontology depth the broader project requires; Document II.09's Thread E engages the contemporary articulation of the analytical content specifically on the polity-formation question the climate-displacement diagnostic forces. The two engagements operate complementarily, not duplicatively, with Thread E drawing on the broader engagement Document III.01 conducts without re-doing the analytical work the broader engagement carries.

The analytical content Thread E carries forward into Threads H and I is foundational. The Indigenous-sovereignty tradition articulates the polity-formation question — how a polity is constituted through its members' relations to one another, to the land, and to the broader-than-human relations the polity operates within — as a question that operates continuously across centuries of disturbance and dispossession, with the polity continuing to operate through the maintenance of its constitutive relations rather than through the structural specification of its institutional architectures. The contemporary climate-displacement diagnostic, on the analytical commitment Thread E carries forward, must specify the conditions under which the broader contemporary polity can cultivate the relations through which it absorbs the displacement dynamics, with the architectural specification operating to enable those relations rather than to specify the architectures within which they will be conducted. Thread H returns to this analytical commitment at depth; Thread E specifies the primary articulation from which the analytical commitment derives.

## Thread F — Belonging from the Borderlands

The Chicana borderlands tradition stands at primary-articulator standing in the analytical engagement Thread F conducts. The tradition is engaged in its own analytical register — the register of lived-experience theorizing, of multilingual analytical work, of integrated literary-philosophical-political articulation, of embodied knowledge production from the specific situated experience of populations whose lives are constituted in and through the borderlands the contemporary border regime produces. The engagement does not absorb the tradition into the Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon of Threads B and D or treat it as lived-experience illustration of analytical content articulated principally in the Euro-American canon; the tradition is engaged as the primary articulation of the analytical content the document carries forward on the question of belonging.

Gloria Anzaldúa is the principal contemporary articulator of the analytical engagement Thread F carries forward. *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza* (Aunt Lute Books, 1987), her foundational text in the Chicana borderlands tradition, articulates the borderland as a lived condition rather than a line. The Chicana feminist scholar engaged in the analytical work — Anzaldúa identified as a Chicana writer-philosopher of the borderlands, with the identity marker analytically constitutive of the work — proceeds from the lived experience of the United States-Mexico border specifically as it has operated across the Rio Grande Valley of South Texas and the broader borderlands of the United States Southwest. The analytical move Anzaldúa specifies is that the border is not adequately analyzed as a line on a map; it is a zone of lived experience, geographically extensive across both sides of the territorial line, in which the populations who live within it operate continuously between the political-juridical-cultural categories of the two states the line nominally separates. The borderland produces, on Anzaldúa's analytical commitment, the *mestiza* consciousness — a consciousness formed in the continuous negotiation of multiple political-juridical-cultural categories, multiple languages, multiple religious-spiritual traditions, multiple historical inheritances — and the *mestiza* consciousness is itself a substantive analytical-political resource rather than a deviation from a clearer single-state-single-culture consciousness the border regime treats as the norm against which the borderland populations are evaluated.

The image Anzaldúa develops of the border as an open wound — *una herida abierta* — articulates the analytical and political content of the border condition the diagnostic engages. The border is not a healed feature of the political-geographical world; it is, for the populations who live in and through it, a continuously open wound, with the contemporary border regime continuously inflicting the violences Threads B and D engage and with the populations of the borderland continuously experiencing the cumulative effects of those violences on their lives, their families, their communities, and their political-philosophical conditions. The borderland populations are not, on Anzaldúa's analytical commitment, marginal to either of the two states the border nominally separates; they are constitutive of both, with the lived conditions of the borderland generating substantive political, cultural, and philosophical resources that the two states have generated in part through the borderland populations' continuous work across the border condition. The analytical apparatus Thread F carries forward from Anzaldúa is that belonging is not a status the polity confers on the borderland populations from the political-juridical centers of either of the two states; belonging is something the borderland populations themselves carry, plural and layered, formed in the continuous lived experience of the borderland and operative across the political-juridical-cultural categories the two states attempt to impose.

The plural and layered character of belonging Anzaldúa specifies is the analytical content Thread F carries forward into the document's positive engagement with the polity-formation question. Belonging, on Anzaldúa's analytical commitment, is not a single category in which a person is either inside or outside; it is a continuous, lived, multi-dimensional condition in which a person may belong to one tradition in one register, to another tradition in another register, to one political community in one dimension of their lives, to another political community in another dimension, to a third community in yet another register, with the layered belonging itself the substantive content of the person's political-cultural-personal existence. The borderland populations carry the plural belonging Anzaldúa articulates as a constitutive feature of their lived condition; the lived condition supplies the analytical apparatus through which the polity-formation diagnostic engages belonging not as a juridical category the architecture confers but as a substantive condition the architecture must cultivate the conditions for.

Aviva Chomsky's *Undocumented: How Immigration Became Illegal* (Beacon Press, 2014) supplies the contemporary analytical engagement with the lived experience of belonging without juridical status in the contemporary United States. The analytical move Chomsky specifies is that the population of undocumented persons in the contemporary United States — estimated across the period of her writing at approximately eleven million persons, with the population stable across the past two decades after the substantial growth of the 1980s and 1990s — operates within the political-economic, political-cultural, and political-philosophical conditions of the United States in nearly all of the substantive registers through which the documented population operates, while being formally excluded from the juridical category that would extend the protections of the constitutional architecture to them. The undocumented are workers in the labor markets, parents in the schools, members of the religious-cultural communities, contributors to the economic-political-cultural life of the polity, and, in many cases, persons who have lived within the United States for decades, who have built their lives within it, and whose belonging to it operates substantively even where the juridical architecture refuses to recognize that belonging.

Chomsky's analytical apparatus traces the historical-political conditions through which the contemporary architecture of "illegality" was constructed. The historical analytical work specifies that the United States operated, across most of its nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, without the juridical category of the illegal migrant in anything resembling its contemporary form. The category was constructed through a series of legislative interventions across the twentieth century — the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the various Acts of the first three decades of the twentieth century that elaborated the architecture, the National Origins Act of 1924, the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 (McCarran-Walter), the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart-Celler), the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, the post-2001 incorporation of immigration enforcement into the national-security architecture — that together built the contemporary juridical architecture under which the undocumented population operates. The analytical apparatus supplies Thread F with the recognition that the contemporary architecture of undocumented existence is not a natural feature of the polity's relationship to migration but a specific cultivated condition the polity has built across the past century-and-a-half through specific historical-political choices.

## Thread G — The Pacific Islander Climate-Displacement Tradition and Legal Precedent

The Pacific Islander climate-displacement tradition stands at primary-articulator standing in the analytical engagement Thread G conducts. The tradition is engaged in its own analytical register — the register of movement-based political-philosophical articulation, of integrated cultural-political-legal work, of substantive engagement with the international juridical architectures through legal precedents the tradition has forced into being. The engagement does not absorb the tradition into the Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon or into the broader climate-displacement diagnostic; it engages the tradition as the primary articulation of the analytical content the document carries forward on the question of belonging-to-place and the right not to be displaced.

The Pacific Climate Warriors are the principal contemporary articulators of the analytical-political content Thread G engages. The Pacific Climate Warriors are a movement of young Pacific Islanders from across the Pacific Island states — Kiribati, Tuvalu, the Marshall Islands, Vanuatu, Fiji, Tonga, Samoa, the Federated States of Micronesia, the Cook Islands, and the broader Pacific Islander diaspora — that emerged across the late 2000s and early 2010s and that has, across the subsequent decade-and-a-half, articulated the Pacific Islander climate-displacement position at the international climate-political conferences, at the international legal-political fora, and across the broader contemporary political-cultural conversation about climate displacement. The movement has been organized in substantial part through 350.org Pacific, the regional affiliate of the international 350.org climate movement, and through the broader Pacific Islander civil-society networks across the region. The movement's principal articulation — "we are not drowning, we are fighting" — is the analytical-political move Thread G carries forward.

The analytical content of the "we are not drowning, we are fighting" articulation is the refusal of the framing of Pacific Islanders as passive climate victims awaiting relocation. The contemporary Euro-American climate-displacement discourse has, across the past two decades, frequently engaged the Pacific Islander populations through the framing of the climate refugee — the population whose continued habitation of their islands is being made impossible by sea-level rise, who must therefore be relocated to other states, who require humanitarian assistance and resettlement programs from the broader international community. The framing operates with substantial sympathetic intent in much of the discourse; it nevertheless operates analytically in a register that the Pacific Climate Warriors have refused. The framing positions the Pacific Islander populations as objects of climate displacement rather than as subjects of political-philosophical analysis and political action; it positions the contemporary climate dynamics as external conditions impinging on the Pacific Islander populations rather than as conditions produced by the political-economic and political-cultural dynamics of the larger contemporary system the Pacific Islander populations are part of; it positions the appropriate response to the conditions as relocation rather than as the substantive engagement with the political-economic conditions producing the climate dynamics; and it positions the Pacific Islander populations as recipients of humanitarian responses rather than as political actors whose articulations the contemporary system must engage on its own terms.

The "we are not drowning, we are fighting" articulation refuses each of these framings. The Pacific Islander populations are subjects of the analytical and political engagement, not objects of it; the climate dynamics are not external conditions but conditions produced by the contemporary political-economic system the Pacific Islander populations are part of and have continuously resisted; the appropriate response to the conditions is not relocation but engagement with the political-economic conditions producing the dynamics, with the engagement conducted by the Pacific Islander populations themselves as political actors rather than as recipients of humanitarian programs designed for them by others. The articulation operates as analytical and political assertion of belonging-to-place — the claim that the Pacific Islander populations belong to the specific islands, atolls, and ancestral territories the climate dynamics threaten to render uninhabitable, that the belonging is constitutive of who the populations are, and that the right not to be displaced from the ancestral territories is prior to any right of relocation the international system might extend in compensation for the displacement.

The articulation of belonging-to-place Thread G engages is the primary articulation of the foundational right Thread C engaged through Arendt. Arendt's "right to have rights" is the right to belong to a political community in which other rights become operative; the Pacific Islander articulation extends the foundational right to its specifically place-based dimension. The right to have rights is, on the Pacific Islander articulation, not principally the right to membership in any abstract political community but the right to continued belonging-to-place — to continued occupation of the ancestral territories that constitute the substantive content of the populations' political-cultural-spiritual existence. The Pacific Islander populations are not, on the articulation, willing to be relocated to other states under the framing that the relocation would satisfy their fundamental political claim; the fundamental claim is the right to continued belonging-to-place, and any architecture that addresses the displacement dynamics only at the level of post-displacement relocation has failed to engage the substantive content of the claim.

The legal precedent the Pacific Islander tradition has forced into being is the analytical-juridical content Thread G engages as the operational extension of the articulation. *Teitiota v. New Zealand*, UN Human Rights Committee, Communication No. 2728/2016, Views adopted 7 January 2020, is the principal contemporary international determination on the juridical question of whether climate conditions can ground a non-refoulement claim under the international human-rights regime. Ioane Teitiota was a national of Kiribati who had migrated to New Zealand and whose application for refugee status under the 1951 Refugee Convention had been rejected by the New Zealand authorities and the New Zealand courts on the ground that the climate conditions in Kiribati did not constitute persecution within the meaning of the Convention. Teitiota brought a communication to the UN Human Rights Committee under the First Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, arguing that New Zealand's refusal of his claim and its expulsion of him to Kiribati violated his right to life under Article 6 of the Covenant on the ground that the climate conditions in Kiribati had made his continued life there incompatible with the right to life. The Human Rights Committee adopted its Views on 7 January 2020.

The Views did not find that Teitiota's particular claim succeeded — the Committee held that the conditions in Kiribati at the time of his removal did not yet rise to the level at which Article 6 was engaged on the specific facts of his case — but the Views established, at the foundational analytical level, that climate conditions can in principle engage the right to life under the Covenant and can therefore engage the non-refoulement obligation that follows from the right to life under the established jurisprudence of the Committee. The Views constitute the first international juridical determination addressing the question, and they constitute the foundational precedent on which subsequent juridical engagement with the question will operate. The precedent does not, in its operational consequences for the particular claimant, supply the comprehensive juridical response to the climate-displacement category Thread A specifies; it supplies the foundational juridical recognition that the category is engaged by the existing international human-rights architecture in principle, and it supplies the analytical-juridical opening through which subsequent juridical work can build the response.

The analytical content Thread G carries forward from the Pacific Climate Warriors and the *Teitiota* precedent is that the polity-formation question the climate-displacement diagnostic forces is not only the question of how a receiving polity absorbs the displaced; it is also the question of how the international architecture honors belonging-to-place and the right not to be displaced in the first instance. The two questions are not separable. An architecture that responds to the climate-displacement dynamics only by absorbing the displaced — by extending membership to populations who have been displaced from their ancestral territories, by building the infrastructure for resettlement of displaced populations in receiving polities, by elaborating the juridical architectures through which the displaced are integrated into the receiving polities — has not engaged the foundational claim the Pacific Islander tradition articulates: the claim that the displacement should not occur in the first instance, that the political-economic conditions producing the climate dynamics should be addressed at their source rather than only at the displacement they produce, that the right to continued belonging-to-place is prior to any right of relocation in compensation for its violation. The architecture must operate at both levels: at the level of preventing the displacement through engagement with the political-economic conditions producing the climate dynamics, and at the level of absorbing the displaced when prevention has failed.

The cross-reference to Document XII.09's public-check scale band (the public-check decision rights) is operational for Thread G's analytical content. The public-check architecture Document XII.09's public-check scale band specifies operates at the ecosystem-and-habitat scale at which the climate dynamics producing the Pacific Islander displacement are generated; the architecture must enable the scale-matched public checks on the political-economic decisions whose cumulative effects are producing the dynamics. The Pacific Islander populations whose belonging-to-place is being threatened by the climate dynamics are not the populations whose political-economic decisions are producing the dynamics; the dynamics are produced principally through the political-economic decisions of the larger industrial-economic powers whose carbon emissions, ecological-system impacts, and broader political-economic operations are generating the climate conditions the Pacific Islander populations are subject to. The public-check architecture must enable the populations being subjected to the dynamics to participate in the political-economic decisions that produce them at scales matched to where the dynamics are actually generated; the contemporary architecture, in which the nation-state remains the principal unit of political-economic decision and in which the Pacific Islander populations have minimal influence on the political-economic decisions of the large industrial-economic powers, has failed to enable the scale-matched public checks the dynamics require. Thread I returns to the architectural specification at the closing; Thread G specifies the primary articulation of the analytical content from which the architectural specification draws.

The methodological discipline Document VII.01 specifies is constitutive of how Thread G conducts the engagement. The engagement with the Pacific Climate Warriors and with the *Teitiota* precedent operates at primary-articulator standing — with the analytical content of the movement and of the precedent engaged in its own analytical register, with the political-cultural-spiritual content of the Pacific Islander tradition engaged as constitutive of the analytical work rather than as cultural background to it. # Part Four — The Positive Polity-Formation Question and the Bridge to the Cultivation Architecture

## Thread H — Belonging Cultivated, Not Designed: The Positive Polity-Formation Question

The polity-formation question the diagnostic forces is the question of how a polity forms and re-forms its membership under conditions of large-scale population movement. The diagnostic Threads B, C, and D have built has specified what the contemporary architecture does (operate as design-imposition fixture; produce statelessness; conduct necropolitical and expulsion operations; sustain itself through capture and disinformation architectures); the primary-articulator engagements Threads E, F, and G have conducted have specified the analytical content from which the alternative cultivation draws (Indigenous sovereignty and climate colonialism; lived borderlands and plural belonging; Pacific Islander belonging-to-place and the right not to be displaced). Thread H specifies the positive polity-formation question the diagnostic forces and engages the Western recovery of the polity-formation framings the primary-articulator traditions of Threads E, F, and G have articulated more concretely.

Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri's analytical work supplies the principal contemporary Western recovery of the polity-formation framings Thread H engages. *Multitude: War and Democracy in the Age of Empire* (Penguin Press, 2004) and *Commonwealth* (Harvard University Press, 2009), together with the earlier *Empire* (Harvard University Press, 2000) of which they constitute the second and third volumes of the trilogy, develop the analytical apparatus through which contemporary political composition is engaged at the scale at which the contemporary global political-economic system operates. The analytical move Hardt and Negri specify in *Multitude* is that the contemporary political-economic system has produced a new form of political composition — the multitude — that is not adequately analyzed as the people of the nation-state, as the working class of industrial capitalism, as the citizenry of the liberal-democratic constitutional order, or as any of the other political-compositional categories the prior phases of modern political theory developed. The multitude is plural without being reducible to the people of any nation-state; it is composed of singularities that operate together in common while remaining substantively plural; it operates through the production of the common — the substrate of contemporary social-political-economic life that is produced collectively and that is the principal site of contemporary political-economic struggle. The analytical apparatus of *Empire*, *Multitude*, and *Commonwealth* engages the contemporary global political-economic system at the scale at which it operates and articulates the political-compositional resources for engaging that system at the matching scale.

The analytical commitment to the common in *Commonwealth* extends the *Multitude* engagement into the substantive analysis of the political-economic conditions under which contemporary political composition operates. The common, on Hardt and Negri's analytical commitment, is not the same as the public (the goods and conditions managed by the state) or the private (the goods and conditions held under capitalist property relations); it is the substrate of substantive social-political-economic life that is produced collectively and that is increasingly the principal site of contemporary capitalist accumulation through the enclosure dynamics through which the common is converted into private property and through the broader dynamics through which the contemporary political-economic system operates on and through the common. The multitude is the political-compositional category that operates within and on the common; the political project Hardt and Negri specify is the project of the multitude operating to maintain and expand the common against the enclosure dynamics of contemporary capitalism.

Thread H engages Hardt and Negri's analytical apparatus as a Western recovery of polity-formation framings the Indigenous, borderlands, and Pacific traditions of Threads E, F, and G articulate more concretely. The multitude framing recovers, in the analytical register of late-twentieth and early-twenty-first century Western political philosophy, the analytical content the Indigenous-sovereignty tradition has articulated across centuries of substantive engagement with non-nation-state political composition: the recognition that political life can be conducted through compositional forms that are not the nation-state, that the polity can operate as a plurality of singularities held together by substantive constitutive relations rather than by sovereign-state institutional architectures. The common framing recovers the analytical content the Indigenous tradition has articulated about land and the broader-than-human relations as the constitutive substrate of political life, that the substrate is not adequately analyzed as either public property or private property but as the relations through which the polity is constituted. The Pacific Islander articulation of belonging-to-place specifies the same analytical commitment in the specifically place-based dimension that the abstract-political articulation of the multitude does not capture as concretely; the borderlands articulation of plural and layered belonging specifies the analytical commitment in the specifically lived-experience dimension that the political-philosophical articulation does not capture as concretely.

The analytical engagement with Hardt and Negri operates under the recognition that the recovery is recent and partial relative to the primary articulations. The multitude framing was articulated in *Empire* in 2000, in *Multitude* in 2004, and in *Commonwealth* in 2009, drawing on the Western political-philosophical tradition through Spinoza and the broader contemporary political-philosophical work the authors engage. The Indigenous-sovereignty tradition has articulated the analytical content the multitude framing recovers across centuries of substantive political-philosophical-political work, and continues to articulate it at the level the contemporary primary articulators (Simpson, Whyte, and the broader contemporary Indigenous-political-philosophical corpus) develop. The borderlands tradition has articulated the analytical content the multitude framing recovers across decades of Chicana feminist political-philosophical work, with Anzaldúa's foundational articulation in 1987 and the broader subsequent corpus developing it at the level the contemporary engagement requires. The Pacific Islander tradition has articulated the analytical content across centuries of Pacific Islander political-philosophical-cultural work, with the contemporary climate-displacement articulation developing the specifically place-based dimension of the analytical commitment.

The analytical priority Thread H maintains is that the engagement with Hardt and Negri operates as engagement with a Western recovery of analytical content the primary-articulator traditions articulate at the foundational level the polity-formation question requires. The recovery is not dismissed — Hardt and Negri's analytical work supplies substantive content in its own register, and the engagement is conducted at the level the substantive content warrants — but the recovery is not engaged as the foundational articulation of the analytical content. The foundational articulations are the primary-articulator traditions of Threads E, F, and G; the recovery is engaged as the Western contemporary work that has begun to recover analytical content the primary-articulator traditions have articulated at greater concrete depth across longer historical engagement.

The cultivation framing's answer to the polity-formation question is the analytical content Thread H specifies as the closing analytical move. Belonging, on the cultivation framing's commitment as it is articulated through the engagement with the primary-articulator traditions and the partial Western recovery, is cultivated rather than designed. The polity grows the conditions under which belonging operates — the conditions for plural and layered belonging Anzaldúa specifies, the conditions for belonging-to-place the Pacific Climate Warriors specify, the conditions for the continuing maintenance of the polity's constitutive relations Simpson and Whyte specify, the conditions for the operation of the multitude on the common Hardt and Negri partially recover. The polity does not engineer belonging through the structural specification of who is and is not a member; it cultivates the conditions under which the polity's members carry their belonging in the plural and layered registers belonging operates within, and the architecture's task is to specify and maintain the cultivation conditions rather than to engineer the structural architecture within which belonging will be conducted.

The case against both design-imposition framings is the closing analytical move Thread H makes. The first design-imposition framing the diagnostic must avoid is the borders-as-engineered-fixture framing Thread B specifies — the framing under which the border architecture is taken as fixture and the polity's task is to fortify, monitor, and selectively open the fixture. The framing fails for the reasons Threads B, C, and D specify: it operates within a sovereignty framing the contemporary conditions have substantially eroded; it produces the stateless condition Arendt diagnosed and the contemporary climate-displaced are increasingly subjected to; it sustains the necropolitical and expulsion operations Mbembe and Sassen specify; it operates through the capture and disinformation architectures Document II.03, Walia, and the broader contemporary engagement specify. The second design-imposition framing the diagnostic must avoid is the mirror-image framing under which the border architecture is to be abolished by architectural specification — the framing under which the polity's task is to engineer the absence of borders rather than to engineer their presence in the contemporary form. The mirror framing fails because it operates within the same design-imposition register the first framing operates within; it substitutes one architectural specification for another without engaging the cultivation framing's analytical move toward the cultivated and revisable character of boundary conditions.

The cultivation framing specifies neither the wall nor its absence. It specifies the conditions under which the polity cultivates the membership architecture appropriate to its biocultural-formation character. The conditions are revisable across the temporal horizons over which cultivation operates; the conditions are scale-matched to the dynamics the architecture absorbs; the conditions are themselves cultivated through the polity's ongoing engagement with the multi-traditional foundational inheritance the project commits to. The conditions are not specified once and operationalized as a fixed architecture; they are themselves matter for ongoing cultivation through the operational architecture the rest of the arc specifies. Thread I returns to the closing specification at depth; Thread H specifies the analytical commitment the closing specification operates from.
