# VI.03 — Energy as Cultivation: Soft Paths, Energy Democracy, and Just Transition Across Nested Scales

## Part VI — The Material Substrate

*Fifth-pass designation: Document O. This document is the third document of Part VI, specifying energy production and distribution as scale-matched cultivation.*

Energy is the third of Part VI's material-substrate cultivation terrains, following land relations and the food-and-soil system and preceding the materials and built-environment documents. It is the terrain on which the dominant framing's hold is firmest, because energy more than any other material substrate has been understood, across the industrial era, as a commodity: a quantity extracted from a reservoir, converted in a centralized plant, and distributed as a metered product to consumers who relate to it only as a bill. The framing is so settled that the very vocabulary of energy policy — supply, demand, generation, load, the consumer — encodes it. This document specifies the alternative the cultivation framing requires: energy as cultivation terrain, an architecture of relations among peoples, places, and the flows of sun, wind, water, and stored carbon that sustain a polity's material life, cultivated across nested scales rather than extracted and distributed as commodity.

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad — biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation — articulated in full in Document III.01 and not re-argued here. For energy, the triad bears in a single direction. The energy system is the principal driver of the physical-environmental disturbance regime that Document II.02's physical-environmental-regime section diagnoses, and its transformation is therefore not a sectoral-policy question but a question of whether the polity can cultivate, across the disturbance landscape and the intergenerational horizon, an energy architecture that does not undermine the conditions of its own continuance. The document specifies energy as scale-matched cultivation across the nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, and it carries the Indigenous energy-sovereignty traditions at primary-articulator standing, with the Western soft-energy-path and energy-democracy literatures engaged as recent and partial recovery of what those traditions have long carried.

The document assumes the constitutional ontology and the nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, the systems-science substrate of Document V.01, the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01, and the foundational-normative inheritance of Document I.01. It does not perform the physical-environmental-disturbance diagnostic of Document II.02's physical-environmental-regime section or the political-economic-disturbance diagnostic of Document II.02's political-economic-regime section; it draws on both as diagnostic context, and it does not duplicate the energy political economy that belongs to Document II.02's political-economic-regime section or the currency architecture of Document XII.02. It does not specify energy-technology design or the bounded provisioning decision rights, which are the work of Document XII.09's provisioning scale band; it specifies the cultivation-condition constitutional categories — grid governance, energy ownership, and the just-transition question — that those documents operationalize. The analytical work proceeds in six movements: the soft-energy-path tradition; the energy-density-and-transition-rate critique engaged on its strongest terms; the energy-democracy literature; the Indigenous energy-sovereignty traditions at primary-articulator standing; the just-transition literature; and the constitutional implications specified across the nested scales.

# Part One — The Soft-Energy-Path Tradition

The soft-energy-path tradition engaged in this part is the body of analysis, opened in the late 1970s and developed continuously since, that first specified energy not as a quantity to be supplied but as a service to be cultivated — and that, in doing so, recovered within the Western energy sciences a way of thinking the Indigenous energy-sovereignty traditions of Part Four have long carried.

Amory Lovins's *Soft Energy Paths: Toward a Durable Peace* (1977) is the tradition's founding text, and its central analytical move is a bifurcation. The hard energy path, as Lovins specified it, is the path of centralized, large-scale, capital-intensive supply expansion — ever-larger power stations, fueled by fossil carbon and fission, feeding an ever-larger grid, on the premise that the policy task is to forecast demand and build supply to meet it. The soft energy path is the path of energy services matched, in scale and in quality, to the end uses they actually serve: efficiency first, so that the service is delivered with the least energy throughput; renewable sources appropriate to the site; and generation distributed near the load rather than concentrated in remote megaprojects. Lovins's decisive insight concerned the matching of energy quality to end use. The dominant framing treats energy as a single undifferentiated quantity, so that the question is only how much. Lovins specified that energy services have qualities — that heating a building to a comfortable temperature, running a motor, and smelting a metal are different tasks requiring different grades of energy — and that supplying a low-grade task such as space heating with a high-grade source is a thermodynamic and economic waste, like, in his image, cutting butter with a chainsaw. The soft path begins from the end use, asks what quality and scale of energy the use actually requires, and supplies exactly that. And Lovins argued that the two paths are, at the level of the institutions and capital commitments they require, mutually exclusive: a polity that commits its capital, its grid architecture, and its regulatory institutions to the hard path forecloses the soft, because the soft path's distributed, efficiency-first, end-use-matched architecture cannot be assembled from the components of the hard path's centralized one.

The Rocky Mountain Institute, which Lovins co-founded in 1982, carried the soft-path analysis from critique into operational design, and its central contribution is the practice of integrative design. Conventional engineering design optimizes each component of a system separately; integrative design treats the whole system at once, and Lovins's institute documented, across building, industrial, and vehicle systems, that whole-system design can capture savings unavailable to component optimization — that, in the institute's formulation, large savings can sometimes cost less than small ones, because a deep enough efficiency intervention can eliminate the need for expensive supply equipment entirely and so "tunnel through" the cost barrier that incremental efficiency runs into. Lovins also gave the tradition the concept of the negawatt: a unit of energy service delivered by efficiency rather than by generation, tradeable and investable like a unit of generated power, and on the soft-path analysis the cheapest and fastest energy resource available.

*Natural Capitalism* (1999), by Paul Hawken, Amory Lovins, and L. Hunter Lovins, set the soft-path energy analysis within a broader framework of four principles: radical resource productivity, biomimicry in the design of production systems, a shift from an economy of goods toward an economy of service and flow, and reinvestment in the natural capital on which the whole economy rests. The book's bearing on this document is its specification that energy throughput is not a measure of prosperity but a cost of it — that the goal is the energy service, the warm house and the moved freight and the lit room, and that the throughput is to be minimized rather than maximized. This is the decoupling the soft-path tradition specifies: the decoupling of energy services, which a polity wants more of, from energy throughput, which carries every cost the energy system imposes. The cultivation framing carries the soft-path tradition forward as recovery: it recovered, within the Western energy sciences, that energy is rightly understood as a cultivated service grown to fit the actual shape of a polity's life, not a commodity supplied in ever-greater undifferentiated quantity. What the tradition recovered, the constitutional architecture of Part Six specifies as condition.

# Part Two — The Energy-Density-and-Transition-Rate Critique on Its Strongest Terms

A constitutional document on energy cannot proceed by assembling only the literatures that support its framing. The soft-energy-path tradition and the renewable-pathway literature face a serious and empirically grounded critique, and this part engages that critique on its strongest terms — not to dismiss it, and not to capitulate to it, but because the cultivation-condition specifications the document carries forward must be the ones that survive the engagement.

Vaclav Smil's body of work is the critique's most rigorous statement. *Energy and Civilization: A History* (2017) is a comprehensive account of the role of energy in human societies from foraging to the present, and its constitutional relevance is its demonstration of the sheer scale and the deep entanglement of the modern energy system: every major structure of contemporary material life — its food system, its cities, its transport, its materials — was built by and remains dependent on a flow of energy historically unprecedented in magnitude, the overwhelming majority of it from fossil carbon. *Energy Transitions: Global and National Perspectives* (second edition, 2017) supplies the critique's sharpest empirical edge. Smil's study of the historical record of energy transitions — the transition from biomass to coal, from coal to oil, from oil to natural gas — establishes that these transitions have been slow, typically unfolding across fifty to sixty years or more from a new source's first appearance to its capture of a substantial share of the system, because the energy system is vast, capital-intensive, long-lived, and infrastructurally entrenched. *Numbers Don't Lie* (2020) carries the analysis into accessible form and specifies the energy-density and dependency problems concretely: fossil fuels have high energy density and are dispatchable on demand, while wind and solar have lower power density and are variable; and the material foundations of modern civilization — among them the ammonia that fertilizes the food system, and the steel and cement that build its infrastructure — are at present produced through processes deeply dependent on fossil energy and fossil feedstock. Smil's conclusion is not climate denial and not fatalism. He holds that decarbonization is necessary. His conclusion is that a complete transition of a system this large, this entrenched, and this fast — fast, that is, relative to every historical transition on record — is an undertaking of extraordinary difficulty, and that optimistic timelines that treat it as a straightforward matter of political will and falling technology costs understate the scale, the material constraints, and the time the transition will demand.

The soft-energy-path and renewable-pathway literatures answer Smil, and the answer must be stated with equal care. Mark Jacobson and colleagues' "100% Clean and Renewable Wind, Water, and Sunlight All-Sector Energy Roadmaps for 139 Countries of the World" (2017) is the most ambitious statement of the case that a full transition is technically achievable, mapping a path to entirely wind-, water-, and solar-powered energy systems; the document records, as scholarly honesty requires, that the Jacobson roadmaps have themselves been contested within the modeling literature, with critics challenging particular assumptions about storage, grid integration, and hydropower capacity. The broader literature is more cautious and correspondingly more robust: the International Renewable Energy Agency's renewable-pathway scenarios, the mitigation pathways assessed in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's Sixth Assessment Report, and the country-level studies of the Deep Decarbonization Pathways Project converge on the finding that deep decarbonization is technically and economically feasible, that it requires the rapid scaling of renewable generation together with electrification, storage, grid expansion, and — crucially — large reductions in energy demand through efficiency, and that the principal obstacles are political, institutional, and infrastructural rather than physical.

The document refuses both the dismissive treatment of Smil and the unreflective treatment of the pathway literature, and the analytical work is to specify what cultivation-condition specifications emerge from holding both. Three emerge. The first is that the demand-side reduction the soft-energy-path tradition specifies is the move that most directly answers Smil's scale objection: a smaller energy system is faster and easier to decarbonize than a large one, and every negawatt of avoided throughput is a unit of the daunting buildout Smil describes that need not be built at all. The soft path is not one option alongside the renewables buildout; it is the precondition that makes the buildout tractable. The second is that the transition cannot be constitutionalized as a single engineered megaproject delivered on a fixed schedule. To write a precise transition timeline into the constitutional architecture would be to commit the design-imposition error in the energy domain — to specify, in advance and from the center, a path whose actual rate depends on material, institutional, and place-specific conditions that no central specification can foresee. Smil's empirical caution is, read correctly, an argument for the cultivation framing rather than against it: a transition of this difficulty must be cultivated — pursued as fast as conditions allow, monitored, and adapted — not designed and decreed. The third is that the constitutional architecture must hold the transition as a disturbance-adapted commitment: a standing, intergenerational commitment to decarbonize as rapidly as the cultivated conditions permit, robust to the political reversals and the technological surprises that a transition spanning decades will certainly encounter. What survives the engagement with Smil is not a slower ambition but a differently structured one — demand reduction first, no design-imposed timeline, the commitment held as cultivation across the disturbance landscape.

# Part Three — The Energy-Democracy Literature

The energy-democracy literature engaged in this part shifts the constitutional question from the technical to the institutional: not what the energy system is made of, but who owns it and who governs it. Its central claim is that the ownership and governance architecture of energy generation and distribution is not a downstream policy-instrument choice but a determinant of what kind of energy system, and what kind of polity, results — and the cultivation framing reads that architecture, accordingly, as a cultivation condition rather than as an instrument.

Matthew Burke and Jennie Stephens, in "Political Power and Renewable Energy Futures" (2018), supply the literature's framing analysis: the transition to renewable energy is not politically neutral with respect to power, and renewable technologies can be deployed in ways that concentrate ownership and control in the same centralized utilities and financial actors that hold the fossil system, or in ways that distribute ownership and control across communities. The transition's technical content does not determine which; the institutional architecture does. Jennie Stephens's *Diversifying Power* (2020) carries the analysis into a positive account, specifying that a transition worth making is one that diversifies power in both senses of the word — distributing the energy system's generation across many sites and many owners, and distributing political and economic power along with it — and that the leadership of such a transition has come disproportionately from those whom the fossil system has most harmed. Denise Fairchild and Al Weinrub's edited volume *Energy Democracy: Advancing Equity in Clean Energy Solutions* (2017) gathers the movement's practitioner and analytical accounts and specifies energy democracy as the reclamation of the energy system as a commons governed by and for communities, with particular attention to the racial and economic equity of who owns, who governs, and who benefits.

The literature's empirical spine is the comparative record of community energy. Conrad Kunze and Sören Becker's *Energy Democracy in Europe* (2014) documents the German *Energiewende*'s community-energy archive — the *Bürgerenergie* movement, in which citizen cooperatives, municipalities, and households came to own a large share of the country's renewable generation capacity, enabled by a feed-in-tariff architecture that made small-scale, community-owned generation financially viable. The Danish wind-cooperative tradition, running from the 1970s, is the older and in some respects deeper case: the cooperative ownership of wind turbines by the farmers and households of the surrounding district, which built both the turbines and the broad public consent that carried Danish wind power forward. The energy-justice literature — developed by Benjamin Sovacool, Sanya Carley, David Konisky, and colleagues — supplies the analytical apparatus through which the equity of an energy system is assessed: distributional justice, concerning who bears the system's burdens and who receives its benefits; procedural justice, concerning who participates in the decisions; and recognition justice, concerning whose needs and whose knowledge the system's design acknowledges.

The cultivation framing re-frames the whole literature in a single move. Energy democracy, read through the framing, is not a menu of ownership models from which a polity selects on grounds of efficiency or preference; it is a specification of cultivation conditions at the habitat and population scales of the nested-scales reference frame. The ownership-and-governance architecture of energy — whether generation is owned by communities, cooperatives, municipalities, and households or by remote utilities and financial actors, and whether the grid is governed as a commons or as private infrastructure — is a condition that the constitutional architecture either cultivates or fails to cultivate, and it conditions everything downstream: the distribution of the transition's costs and benefits, the resilience of the system to disturbance, the breadth of the consent that carries the transition, and the capacity of communities to govern the material substrate of their own lives. Part Six carries energy ownership and grid governance forward as two of its three constitutional categories on exactly this basis. But the energy-democracy literature, for all that the cultivation framing draws on it, is itself a recent and partial recovery, and the tradition that articulates energy governance most fully is the one Part Four engages.

# Part Four — Indigenous Energy-Sovereignty Traditions

The Indigenous energy-sovereignty traditions engaged in this part stand at primary-articulator standing. They are the primary articulation of what it means to govern energy as a relation rather than to administer it as a commodity, and the Western energy-democracy literature of Part Three is engaged, against them, as partial recovery. The traditions are engaged here under the multi-eyed-seeing discipline of Document VII.01 and on the land-relation substrate of Document VI.01, because Indigenous energy sovereignty is, at its foundation, a matter of jurisdiction and consent over land and water — the same constitutional question that Document VI.01's engagement with the Land Back articulation specified.

The Indigenous energy-sovereignty tradition is articulated, first, in the sustained record of Indigenous resistance to fossil-energy infrastructure imposed on Indigenous land. The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe's opposition to the Dakota Access Pipeline, and the #NoDAPL movement that gathered at the Oceti Sakowin camps in 2016 under the watchword Mni Wiconi — water is life — specified, in the most widely witnessed Indigenous-led mobilization of the era, that an energy decision is a decision about water, land, treaty jurisdiction, and the conditions of a people's continuance, and that an infrastructure routed through Indigenous territory without Indigenous consent is a continuation of the colonial dispossession that Document VI.01 dated. The Anishinaabe-led opposition to the Line 3 tar-sands pipeline through the treaty territories of northern Minnesota carried the same articulation, and sharpened it: the water protectors specified the pipeline as a threat to *manoomin*, the wild rice that is at once a food, a relative, and a treaty-protected being, and so specified that an energy decision is inseparable from the food-and-water relations that constitute a people. Winona LaDuke (Anishinaabe), in *All Our Relations* (1999) and *Recovering the Sacred* (2005), and through the work of Honor the Earth, the organization she co-founded, articulated the tradition's positive content alongside its resistance: the recovery of Indigenous land-based economies, the development of tribal renewable-energy capacity, and the understanding of energy as a relation that must honor the land, the water, and the generations rather than extract from them.

The tradition is articulated, second, in the contemporary Indigenous transition to community-owned renewable generation — a transition that is itself a reclamation of energy sovereignty. The closure of the Navajo Generating Station, the large coal-fired plant near Page, Arizona, in 2019 — a plant that had supplied power and wage employment while burning coal from Navajo land and drawing on Navajo and Hopi water — left a just-transition challenge that the tradition has met on its own terms: Navajo Power, a Native-led solar-development enterprise, and Native Renewables, which brings solar generation to off-grid Navajo and Hopi households that the centralized grid never reached, together with the Indigenized Energy Initiative and the broader work of the Indigenous Environmental Network, specify a transition in which the renewable system is owned and governed by the Indigenous nations whose land it occupies. This is energy democracy in the energy-democracy literature's sense, but it is more: it is energy sovereignty, the governance of the energy system as an exercise of the nation's jurisdiction over its own territory.

The tradition is articulated, third, in a specification that the Western energy-democracy literature does not reach and that the document must carry precisely: that Indigenous energy sovereignty entails the refusal of non-consensual renewable infrastructure as well as the refusal of fossil infrastructure. The Sami Council's engagement with Arctic energy development includes the contested siting of large wind installations on Sami reindeer-herding lands, and the case of the Fosen wind development in Norway — where the national Supreme Court found that a large wind farm had been built in violation of Sami rights — specifies the point with constitutional clarity: a renewable energy project is not, by virtue of being renewable, consistent with Indigenous sovereignty, and an energy transition that imposes wind, solar, transmission, or mining infrastructure on Indigenous land without Indigenous consent reproduces the dispossession it claims to leave behind. The Pacific Islander energy-and-climate-sovereignty record carries the tradition into the register of the polities most exposed to the physical-environmental disturbance regime: Pacific nations have articulated, simultaneously, a demand for the rapid decarbonization on which their territorial survival depends and a demand that the transition be governed by and for the peoples it is meant to protect. The Indigenous energy-sovereignty tradition specifies, in sum, that energy governance is the governance of a relation among a people, its land, its water, and the generations — that the question is always one of jurisdiction and consent — and the constitutional architecture of Part Six carries that specification as its primary articulation of what grid governance and energy ownership must secure.

# Part Five — The Just-Transition Literature

The just-transition literature engaged in this part addresses the question that the energy transition makes unavoidable: what is owed to the workers and the communities whose livelihoods are bound to the fossil-energy system that the transition must wind down. It is the literature in which the labor movement and the climate movement have met, and its constitutional significance is that it specifies the transition's fairness not as a sentiment but as a condition of the transition's legitimacy and therefore of its political durability.

The tradition's historical articulator is Tony Mazzocchi, the American labor leader of the Oil, Chemical and Atomic Workers union, who through the 1980s and early 1990s specified that workers should not be made to choose between their livelihoods and a habitable environment, and who proposed what he framed as a "Superfund for workers" — a guarantee of income, retraining, and support for workers displaced by environmental protection, modeled on the principle that a society which benefits from winding down a hazardous industry owes a debt to those the winding-down displaces. Mazzocchi's framing gave the tradition its name and its founding commitment. The Just Transition Alliance, formed in 1997 as a coalition of environmental-justice and labor organizations, carried the framing into an organized movement, and the Climate Justice Alliance's Just Transition framework developed its mature and most ambitious form: the transition from what the framework names an extractive economy — organized around the extraction of energy, labor, and wealth — to a regenerative economy, through the managed decline of the fossil sector, the ecological restoration of the lands and communities it has harmed, and the building of community wealth and resilience. The Labor Network for Sustainability and the BlueGreen Alliance carry the labor-environmental coalition in the contemporary United States, and J. Mijin Cha's scholarship supplies the analytical specification of what a just transition requires institutionally — the timing of support relative to displacement, the role of the affected workers and communities in governing the transition, and the distinction between transition measures that are genuine and those that are nominal.

The comparative and critical literature disciplines the concept. Edouard Morena, Dunja Krause, and Dimitris Stevis's edited volume *Just Transitions: Social Justice in the Shift Toward a Low-Carbon World* (2019) specifies that "just transition" is not a single agreed program but a contested band of meanings, running from a narrow interpretation — compensation and retraining for displaced fossil-sector workers, leaving the surrounding political economy intact — to a transformative interpretation, in which the transition is the occasion for a structural change in who owns and governs the economy. Daniel Aldana Cohen's work on the political economy of decarbonization sharpens the critical edge: a transition pursued without attention to its political-economy conditions can produce what amounts to an eco-apartheid, in which the benefits of decarbonization accrue to the already advantaged while its costs fall on the already burdened, and a genuinely just transition therefore requires the redistribution of ownership, security, and political power, not only the deployment of clean technology. The document carries the just-transition literature forward as the specification of its third constitutional category. It does not duplicate the energy political economy that belongs to Document II.02's political-economic-regime section or the currency architecture of Document XII.02; it specifies the just-transition question as a cultivation condition — the condition that the wind-down of the fossil system be governed so that those bound to it are carried through the transition rather than abandoned by it — and Part Six carries it as the third of the three categories the constitutional architecture must hold.

# Part Five-bis — Eastern, Islamic, and African Energy Traditions at Primary-Articulator Standing

The preceding parts carried the soft-energy-path tradition, the energy-democracy literature, the Indigenous energy-sovereignty traditions, and the just-transition canon. The Eastern, Islamic, and African traditions articulate the energy-as-cultivation and energy-as-sufficiency commitment at primary-articulator standing, and the document engages them here in their own registers rather than as comparative supplement.

The Daoist tradition carries the deepest pre-modern articulation of energy as flow rather than as extractable stock. The Daoist cosmology of *qì* — the vital energy whose circulation constitutes the living order — reads energy not as a stored resource to be extracted and consumed but as a flow to be aligned with, and the *wu wei* and *ziran* commitments (`XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v7`) translate directly into an energy ethic: the cultivated energy system works *with* the renewable flows of sun, wind, and water that are the contemporary forms of the circulating *qì*, rather than against the grain of the living order by extracting and combusting the stored carbon of the deep past. The soft-energy-path tradition's preference for flows over stocks (Part One) is the recent and partial recovery of the Daoist energy cosmology, which articulated the flow-not-stock orientation two millennia before the thermodynamic argument for it.

The Buddhist and Gandhian traditions carry the energy-sufficiency commitment. The Buddhist right-livelihood and sufficiency orientation (engaged at the political-economy register in `X.01_Political_Economy_Transformative_Legislation_v7`) bears on energy as the refusal of the maximize-consumption premise that drives the extractive energy configuration: the cultivated energy system is sized to well-being rather than to ever-rising throughput. The Gandhian tradition supplies the decentralization commitment — *swadeshi* (local self-reliance) translated into local, distributed, community-governed energy rather than the centralized extractive grid, and the critique of energy-intensive mass production that the appropriate-technology and soft-path traditions later recovered. The energy-democracy literature's commitment to distributed, community-owned energy (Part Three) is the recovery of the Gandhian decentralization ethic.

The Islamic tradition carries the energy-stewardship commitment through its environmental ethics. The *mīzān* (the balance of creation that the human is obligated not to disturb) and the prohibition on *isrāf* (wasteful excess and squandering) ground an energy ethic of restraint and sufficiency; energy, as part of the created order the human holds under *amāna* (trust) as *khalīfa* (steward), is to be used within the balance rather than extracted and consumed without limit. The African *ubuntu* tradition carries the energy-as-commons commitment: energy as a substrate of the relational personhood through which persons are constituted as persons, governed communally and cooperatively rather than privatized and extracted — the relational ground beneath the energy-democracy and energy-sovereignty commitments the preceding parts engage.

These traditions differ in cosmology from one another and from the Indigenous energy-sovereignty traditions, and the cultivation discipline holds the differences in their own registers. What they articulate at primary-articulator standing against the extractive energy configuration is energy as flow to be aligned with (Daoist), sized to sufficiency (Buddhist), held in local self-reliance (Gandhian), used within the balance under trust (Islamic), and governed as a relational commons (*ubuntu*) — the energy-as-cultivation commitment the soft-path and energy-democracy canons recover recently and partially.

# Part Six — Constitutional Implications Across the Nested Scales

The five preceding parts have engaged the canons and traditions through which energy is to be understood as cultivation. This closing part specifies what follows for a constitutional architecture: the cultivation conditions the energy system requires at each scale of the nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, and the three constitutional categories — grid governance, energy ownership, and the just-transition question — through which those conditions are held. The part specifies cultivation-condition constitutional categories; it does not specify energy-technology design, which the cultivation framing leaves to be grown to fit each place, and it does not specify the bounded decision rights, which are the work of Document XII.09's provisioning scale band.

At the biosphere and ecozone scales, the cultivation condition is that the energy system's aggregate impact on the climate system be held within the bounds of a habitable biosphere across the geological and seven-generations temporal horizons. The energy system is the principal driver of the atmospheric-composition change that Document II.02's physical-environmental-regime section diagnoses, and the biospheric condition is therefore the standing, intergenerational, disturbance-adapted commitment to decarbonization specified in Part Two: a commitment held as cultivation, pursued as rapidly as conditions allow, rather than as a design-imposed timeline. Because the climate system is indivisibly planetary while energy systems are governed by many jurisdictions, the biospheric scale also imposes the condition of cross-jurisdictional decarbonization commitment — the constitutional recognition that no polity can secure a habitable climate alone and that the architecture must carry commitments coordinated across the polities sharing the biosphere.

At the ecosystem scale, the cultivation condition concerns the watershed and the bioregion as units of energy-system architecture. A soft-path energy system, distributed and matched to its sources, is also a system matched to the renewable endowment of its bioregion — the solar, wind, hydro, and geothermal flows that a particular place actually carries — and the ecosystem-scale condition is that the constitutional architecture enable bioregional power systems and bioregional grids, cultivated to the renewable resources of the place rather than imposed as a uniform national template. This is the soft path's end-use matching, carried up from the building to the bioregion.

At the habitat scale, the cultivation condition is grid governance and energy-ownership authority. The habitat scale is the scale at which the energy-democracy literature of Part Three and the Indigenous energy-sovereignty tradition of Part Four locate their central claim: that the ownership of generation and the governance of the grid are conditions that determine what kind of energy system results. The habitat-scale condition is that the constitutional architecture recognize and enable community, cooperative, municipal, and Indigenous ownership of generation, and the governance of the grid as a commons — shared infrastructure governed by and for the communities it serves — rather than as private infrastructure governed for extracted return.

At the population scale, the cultivation condition is the energy-cooperative architecture and the energy-access right. The population scale is where energy democracy is instantiated in the cooperative and community-energy institutions that the German and Danish records of Part Three document, and it is where energy poverty — the inability of households to afford the energy services of warmth, light, and power that a decent life requires — is registered as a constitutional failure. The population-scale condition is that the architecture cultivate community-energy institutions and recognize a right of energy access: the assurance that every household holds reliable access to the energy services its life and health require.

At the organism and organ-system scales, the cultivation condition is embodied energy justice. The energy system reaches the body directly: through the warmth or cold a household can or cannot secure, through the air quality that fossil combustion degrades, and through the dependence of some bodies on energy in ways others' are not — the disability-and-climate-vulnerability intersection, in which a person reliant on powered medical equipment, or acutely vulnerable to heat or cold, holds a relation to energy reliability that the population-scale average conceals. The organism-scale condition is that the energy system's reliability and affordability be cultivated with the most energy-vulnerable bodies in view, so that the right of energy access is real for those for whom an outage or an unaffordable bill is not an inconvenience but a danger.

The three constitutional categories run across the scales. Grid governance is the category that holds the question of whether the grid is governed as a commons or as private infrastructure, and at what scales its governing authority sits. Energy ownership is the category that holds the question of who owns generation — communities, cooperatives, municipalities, and Indigenous nations, or remote utilities and financial actors — and the Indigenous energy-sovereignty tradition specifies that this category is, for Indigenous land, a question of jurisdiction and consent. The just-transition question is the category that holds the commitment to those whom the wind-down of the fossil system displaces. These categories are what this document specifies and hands forward. Document XII.09's provisioning scale band, on provisioning decision rights, is where grid governance, energy ownership, and the energy-access right are operationalized as bounded decision rights at the habitat and population scales; this document names what XII.09's provisioning scale band must instantiate and does not perform the rights specification. Document XII.01, on eco-mimetic policy-making, supplies the method for legislating energy policy in the cultivation register; Document VI.04 carries the circular material flows that the energy transition's hardware — the turbines, panels, batteries, and transmission infrastructure — requires; Document IX.01 supplies the climate stress-test against which the energy system's biospheric commitment is assessed; and Document XIII.01 integrates the energy cultivation conditions specified here with the conditions specified across the rest of the arc into the cultivation protocol.

Energy is cultivation terrain, not commodity. A constitutional order that treats its energy system as a commodity to be supplied in ever-greater quantity has mistaken the throughput for the service, and the destabilized climate, the concentrated ownership, the energy poverty, and the dispossessed communities that the commodity framing has produced are the record of the mistake. The cultivation framing's alternative is the one this document has specified: energy as a service cultivated to fit the shape of a polity's life, the transition held as a disturbance-adapted commitment rather than a design-imposed schedule, the ownership and governance of the system carried as cultivation conditions at the habitat and population scales, the Indigenous energy-sovereignty tradition at primary-articulator standing, and the just-transition question held as the condition of the transition's legitimacy. Of the energy system, as of the land and the food it depends on, the project's summary phrase holds: it is grown, not built — and a constitution adequate to it cultivates the conditions of the growing.
