## Part One — Multilevel Selection as Cultivation-Substrate Apparatus

The multilevel-selection literature engages the evolutionary substrate of cooperation, norms, and group-level adaptation. Elliott Sober and David Sloan Wilson's *Unto Others: The Evolution and Psychology of Unselfish Behavior* (1998) is the foundational contemporary articulation: selection operates at multiple levels of the biological-social hierarchy, and the evolution of cooperation in social species cannot be tracked through gene-level or individual-level selection alone. David Sloan Wilson and Edward O. Wilson's "Rethinking the Theoretical Foundation of Sociobiology" (*Quarterly Review of Biology*, 2007) carried the claim into a general reformulation: multilevel selection is the apparatus through which group-level adaptation is rendered analytically tractable.

The cultural-multilevel-selection synthesis runs through Peter J. Richerson and Robert Boyd's *Culture and the Evolutionary Process* (1985), *Not by Genes Alone: How Culture Transformed Human Evolution* (2005), and *The Origin and Evolution of Cultures* (2005). Their commitment is that human evolution is constituted through gene-culture coevolution — cultural-evolutionary processes are the substrate through which human cognitive, motivational, and social capacities have been evolved, not an overlay on a fixed genetic baseline. Joseph Henrich's *The Secret of Our Success* (2015) carries the point through cultural learning as the species-defining capacity, and *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020) traces the historical-cultural genesis of the psychologically peculiar profile — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic — that much of the behavioral-science literature has mistaken for a human universal. William H. Durham's *Coevolution: Genes, Culture, and Human Diversity* (1991) supplies the foundational gene-culture-coevolution framework.

The strong critiques of group selection are preserved as analytical history rather than dismissed. J. B. S. Haldane and W. D. Hamilton's inclusive-fitness work, George C. Williams's *Adaptation and Natural Selection* (1966), and John Maynard Smith's "Group Selection and Kin Selection" (*Nature*, 1964) established the mid-twentieth-century case that apparent group-level adaptation reduces to gene-level and kin-level selection. The multilevel-selection response, through Sober and Wilson and through David Sloan Wilson's *Darwin's Cathedral: Evolution, Religion, and the Nature of Society* (2002), does not foreclose the debate; it engages the apparatus through which contemporary cultural-evolutionary analysis proceeds, on which the gene-level critique and the multilevel-selection reformulation are now widely understood as alternative bookkeeping conventions over the same underlying dynamics rather than as rival empirical claims.

Under the sixth-pass framing, the multilevel-selection apparatus is read as cultivation-substrate specification: cooperation, norms, and group-level adaptation are the evolutionary substrate of the cultivation conditions under which larger-scale polities have formed across deep time. The relocated material from Document II.01 Part Four §III, on group selection and the evolution of cooperation, is carried here as substrate. Michael Tomasello's shared-intentionality framework — across *A Natural History of Human Thinking* (2014), *A Natural History of Human Morality* (2016), and *Becoming Human: A Theory of Ontogeny* (2019) — identifies the species-defining cognitive innovation as the capacity to form joint goals and coordinate around joint commitments; Sarah Blaffer Hrdy's *Mothers and Others: The Evolutionary Origins of Mutual Understanding* (2009) carries the point through cooperative breeding as the evolutionary-cognitive substrate of distributed responsibility. The cooperative-cognitive substrate the Western evolutionary-anthropological canon has assembled is recent and partial recovery of what the Indigenous and Eastern relational-ontology traditions, engaged in Part Seven, have carried as primary articulation.

## Part Two — The Hunter-Gatherer Cognitive Baseline as Organism-and-Organ-System Scale

The hunter-gatherer cognitive baseline specifies the evolved cognitive substrate of the human at the organism-and-organ-system scale of the nested-scales reference frame. Robin Dunbar's social-brain hypothesis — through *Grooming, Gossip, and the Evolution of Language* (1996), "The Social Brain Hypothesis" (*Evolutionary Anthropology*, 1998), and *Human Evolution: A Pandora's Box* (2014) — specifies a small-band cognitive ceiling: the eponymous Dunbar's number of roughly one hundred fifty stable social relations marks the limit of the network a human can track through direct interpersonal cognition. The number is not a hard threshold but a regularity that bears directly on the scaling problem of governance: any polity larger than the small band governs through relationships its members cannot hold in direct cognition, and must therefore rely on the cultural-evolutionary scaling mechanisms Part Three engages.

Christopher Boehm's *Hierarchy in the Forest: The Evolution of Egalitarian Behavior* (1999) establishes that hunter-gatherer political organization sustains egalitarianism through reverse dominance hierarchies — coalitions of subordinates that pre-empt the emergence of permanent hierarchical domination. Egalitarianism, in Boehm's account, is not the absence of politics but an active and continuous political achievement. *Moral Origins: The Evolution of Virtue, Altruism, and Shame* (2012) carries the analysis to the evolutionary origins of human moral cognition. Polly Wiessner's long-term ethnographic and archaeological corpus on the Ju/'hoansi documents the small-scale political-economic dynamics — the leveling mechanisms, the gift networks, the night-time conversational life — through which egalitarian organization is sustained across decades.

David Graeber and David Wengrow's *The Dawn of Everything: A New History of Humanity* (2021) documents the empirical variability of political organization across the deep human past and inverts the standard stage-progression narrative — hunter-gatherer to agriculture to hierarchy to civilization — that the Western evolutionary-political-historical tradition long operated under. Marshall Sahlins's *Stone Age Economics* (1972), including "The Original Affluent Society," established the foundational engagement with hunter-gatherer political economy as an alternative to the development-stage framing rather than a deprived antecedent of it. Pierre Clastres's *Society Against the State* (1974) engages stateless societies' active resistance to state-formation as a political-organizational commitment rather than a residual or pre-political condition. James C. Scott's *Against the Grain: A Deep History of the Earliest States* (2017) and *The Art of Not Being Governed: An Anarchist History of Upland Southeast Asia* (2009) extend the engagement to the early state and to the peoples who have organized themselves to remain outside its reach.

Under the sixth-pass framing, the hunter-gatherer cognitive baseline is the organism-and-organ-system scale of the nested-scales reference frame: small-band cognition is the evolved cognitive substrate against which the contemporary scaling problem of governance is read. The relocated material from Document II.01 Part Two §II — Dunbar's number, kinship and reciprocity at scale — and Part Four §IV on the hunter-gatherer legacy is carried here as substrate; the shared-substrate engagement with Document VIII.01's primary-source treatment of Boehm, Sahlins, Graeber-Wengrow, and Henrich is operationalized through cross-citation per `project/relocations/Document_7_relocations.md`.

## Part Three — Cultural-Evolutionary Scaling Mechanisms as Cultivation Conditions

The cultural-evolutionary scaling mechanisms are the apparatus through which small-band cognition has been extended to large-scale political units across deep human history. Each mechanism is engaged here both for how it scales cooperation and for the characteristic failure mode it generates when it scales — because the failure modes are the scale-mismatch problems the rest of the arc must address.

Norms are the evolved cultural-cognitive technology that carries shared expectation beyond the reach of direct interpersonal cognition. Cristina Bicchieri's *The Grammar of Society: The Nature and Dynamics of Social Norms* (2006) and *Norms in the Wild: How to Diagnose, Measure, and Change Social Norms* (2017) specify the analytical apparatus: a social norm is a behavioral rule sustained by a sufficient proportion of a population's conditional preference to conform given the expectation that others conform and the belief that others expect conformity. Norms scale cooperation because they let a stranger predict a stranger; but they scale through ritual and prestige amplification, and the characteristic failure mode is the generation of exclusion and out-group hostility along the lines of norm-prescribed inclusion — the norm that binds an in-group is the same norm that marks the outsider.

Ritual is the scaling mechanism Roy A. Rappaport's *Ritual and Religion in the Making of Humanity* (1999) places at the foundation of human social-political organization; Harvey Whitehouse's modes-of-religiosity work, in *Inside the Cult* (1995) and *Modes of Religiosity* (2004), distinguishes the imagistic mode of rare, high-arousal ritual from the doctrinal mode of frequent, low-arousal ritual and specifies how each transmits and bonds at different scales; Catherine Bell's *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (1992) supplies the practice-theoretic account. Ritual scales coordinated cooperation beyond what direct interpersonal cognition can sustain; the characteristic failure mode is the costly-signaling-and-cult dynamic — the cost ritual imposes on participants escalates, and the bonding ritual produces can be captured by cult-like formations.

Prestige is the scaling mechanism Joseph Henrich and Francisco J. Gil-White specify in "The Evolution of Prestige: Freely Conferred Deference as a Mechanism for Enhancing the Benefits of Cultural Transmission" (*Evolution and Human Behavior*, 2001): deference freely conferred on those judged worth learning from, distinct from the dominance-based deference extracted by force. Prestige scales coordination beyond direct dominance enforcement; the characteristic failure mode is the drift of prestige into oligarchy and concentrated cultural authority, as freely conferred deference accumulates and hardens into entrenched position — the failure mode Documents II.02 and II.03 engage at the contemporary scale.

Fictive kinship is the scaling mechanism through which the solidarity and obligation of biological kinship are extended to non-kin. The comparative-anthropology literature on fictive-kin extension, including Maurice Bloch's work on ritual kinship, documents the devotional and ceremonial architectures through which kin-terms and kin-obligations are extended to larger units. Fictive kinship scales solidarity; the characteristic failure mode is the generation of ethnic and national exclusion, as kin-extending solidarity hardens an in-group boundary.

Imagined communities are the scaling mechanism Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities: Reflections on the Origin and Spread of Nationalism* (1983) specifies: the large-scale political unit is constituted through a shared symbolic imagination — print capitalism, the standardized vernacular, the simultaneity of the newspaper and the novel — that lets members conceive a communion with people they will never meet. Imagined communities scale political coordination to the largest units in the historical record; the characteristic failure mode is nationalism and the political-violence pathologies the comparative-nationalism literature documents.

Under the sixth-pass framing, the scaling mechanisms are cultivation conditions: the conditions under which the biocultural polity has historically scaled, and whose characteristic failure modes are the scale-mismatch problems the rest of the arc must address. The cultivation framing holds that the analytical task is not to design institutions that neutralize the scaling-mechanism substrate — the design-imposition framing engaged in Document II.01 — but to specify cultivation conditions under which the scaling mechanisms operate to sustain rather than to undermine political-cultural-ecological flourishing. The Indigenous and Eastern primary articulators of cultivation conditions, engaged in Part Seven, carry the deep-substrate articulation of those conditions in their primary registers.

## Part Four — The Comparative-Anthropology-of-Governance Archive

The comparative-anthropology-of-governance archive engages the empirical-historical record of governance formations across seven political-organizational modalities. It is engaged as an empirical archive of cultivation-condition variations, not as a developmental sequence — each formation is a historically situated specification of cultivation conditions under which a biocultural polity has grown, with characteristic capabilities and characteristic failure modes, and none is a stage on a ladder the others ascend.

The hunter-gatherer formation engaged in Part Two is the foundational case. The comparative engagement extends through Richard Lee's corpus on the !Kung, Frank Marlowe's *The Hadza: Hunter-Gatherers of Tanzania* (2010), and Colin Turnbull's *The Forest People* (1961) on the Mbuti — the last engaged with the contemporary critique of Turnbull's romanticizing frame. The Australian Aboriginal foundational traditions are engaged through Tyson Yunkaporta's (Apalech Clan) *Sand Talk* (2019), Mary Graham's (Kombumerri and Wakka Wakka) work on Aboriginal relational ontology, and Irene Watson's (Tanganekald and Meintangk) *Aboriginal Peoples, Colonialism and International Law: Raw Law* (2015).

The horticultural formation is engaged through the Amazonian and Melanesian-Highlands ethnographic archive — Clastres's *Society Against the State* (1974), Philippe Descola's *Beyond Nature and Culture* (2005) on the analytics of animism and naturalism, Roy Wagner's *The Invention of Culture* (1981) — and through the African horticultural-formations literature of Mary Douglas and Jack Goody. The agrarian formation is engaged through Karl Wittfogel's *Oriental Despotism* (1957), whose hydraulic-state thesis is engaged with the substantial contemporary critique that has qualified it; through the classical Mediterranean and Eurasian agrarian archive; through the Chinese agrarian-bureaucratic tradition in Mark Elvin's *The Pattern of the Chinese Past* (1973) and Mark Edward Lewis's Han-and-Tang corpus; and through the Indian agrarian formations in D. D. Kosambi's *An Introduction to the Study of Indian History* (1956) and Romila Thapar's *The Past Before Us* (2013).

The pastoral formation is engaged through Anatoly M. Khazanov's *Nomads and the Outside World* (1984) on the Central Asian and Sahelian pastoral-political archive, and through the African pastoral-formations literature of the Evans-Pritchard tradition, engaged with the contemporary critique of its colonial-anthropological framing. The industrial formation is engaged through Charles Tilly's *Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1990* (1990) on war and capital accumulation in European state-formation, Michael Mann's *The Sources of Social Power* tetralogy (1986–2013) on the ideological, economic, military, and political sources of power in long-historical perspective, and James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (1998) on the legibility-and-simplification dynamics of state-formation, with Eugen Weber's *Peasants into Frenchmen* (1976) on the empirical labor of national integration.

The post-industrial formation is engaged through Wolfgang Streeck's *Buying Time: The Delayed Crisis of Democratic Capitalism* (2014) and *How Will Capitalism End?* (2016) on contemporary welfare-state, financialized-economy, and neoliberal formations. The post-industrial American slice of the archive, which Document X.01 carries at the political-economy register, is cross-cited per `project/relocations/Document_9_relocations.md` rather than duplicated: this document draws on Document X.01's treatment while engaging the comparative archive at the cross-cultural register. The contemporary digital-network formation is engaged through Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) on the platform-state and algorithmic-governance dynamic — engaged at depth in Documents II.02, II.03, and II.02's cognitive-and-informational-regime section — through Beth Simone Noveck's work on digital democracy, and through the Pol.is and vTaiwan documentation engaged in Documents XII.03 and XI.01.

The archive is an empirical record of cultivation-condition variation. The Indigenous-knowledge-as-living-governance treatment of the Haudenosaunee Great Law, Andean Buen Vivir, and African Ubuntu as governance formations operating at scale is carried by Document VIII.01 §§3.4–3.6 and cross-cited here per `project/relocations/Document_7_relocations.md`, engaged as cultivation specification across cultural-political-historical contexts rather than as instances arranged on a developmental scale.

## Part Five — The Nation-State Literature as Theoretical Archive

The nation-state literature is engaged as a theoretical archive of cultivation-condition failure and recovery at the industrial and post-industrial scales. Ernest Gellner's *Nations and Nationalism* (1983) specifies the industrial-economy substrate of nationalism — the nation as the cultural form an industrial economy requires for its mobile, literate, standardized workforce. Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (1983), engaged in Part Three as a scaling mechanism, is engaged here for its account of national imagination. Eric J. Hobsbawm's *Nations and Nationalism Since 1780* (1990) and the volume he edited with Terence Ranger, *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), document the historical genesis of national-traditional categories that present themselves as ancient. Anthony D. Smith's *The Ethnic Origins of Nations* (1986) and *Nationalism and Modernism* (1998) supply the ethno-symbolic alternative to the strong modernist account, arguing that national formations draw on pre-existing ethnic symbolic material rather than being conjured wholesale by industrial modernity. Charles Tilly and Michael Mann, engaged in Part Four, carry the state-formation analysis; James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (1998) carries the legibility analysis. Partha Chatterjee's *The Nation and Its Fragments: Colonial and Postcolonial Histories* (1993) engages the colonial and postcolonial nation and presses the critical question of the Eurocentric-modernity assumptions embedded in the Western nationalism literature.

Under the sixth-pass framing, the nation-state is read as one historically situated cultivation-condition formation. Its characteristic capabilities — large-scale coordination, the welfare-state architecture, military-and-fiscal capacity — and its characteristic failure modes — ethno-nationalist exclusion, scale-mismatch with both supra-national and sub-national governance terrain, and capture by political-economic disturbance regimes — are diagnostic for the contemporary architectural problem. The nation-state literature articulates the theory of a historically specific formation, not the cultivation conditions of human political organization as such.

## Part Six — The Sub-Species Evolutionary Backdrop and the Analytical Status of the Human

The sub-species evolutionary backdrop engages the paleoanthropological record establishing that the human is an analytically contestable category emerging from multi-species coexistence, interbreeding, and absorption rather than a fixed biological baseline. Svante Pääbo's *Neanderthal Man: In Search of Lost Genomes* (2014) narrates the recovery of Neanderthal genomes and the empirical demonstration of Homo sapiens–Neanderthal interbreeding — the finding that most living people outside sub-Saharan Africa carry Neanderthal-derived sequence. Tom Higham's *The World Before Us: The New Science Behind Our Human Origins* (2021) carries the contemporary record, including the Denisovan archive recovered largely from genomic rather than skeletal evidence, Homo floresiensis, and the deep-time multi-species human past. Rebecca Wragg Sykes's *Kindred: Neanderthal Life, Love, Death and Art* (2020) engages the Neanderthal record as evidence of full cognitive and cultural personhood rather than of a deficient precursor.

The broader paleoanthropological literature is engaged through Chris Stringer's *Lone Survivors: How We Came to Be the Only Humans on Earth* (2012), read with the multi-species-coexistence amendment the post-Pääbo record has required, and through John Hawks's work on the paleogenomic record. Under the sixth-pass framing, the sub-species backdrop is the analytical-status-of-the-human substrate: the boundary between Homo sapiens and the broader hominin record is historically contingent, and the analytical category "human" is itself a cultivation outcome rather than a pre-cultivation given. The point coordinates with Document III.01 (Part III) on the biocultural-governance ontology's reading of the substrate-as-biological-given assumption the founding-era anthropology operated under, engaged in Document II.01. The commitment is not eliminative of the category "human" — the cultivation work the project carries operates within that category. The commitment is that the category is constituted through cultivation processes rather than given before them, and the analysis attends to the cultural-historical-evolutionary processes through which it has been and continues to be constituted.
