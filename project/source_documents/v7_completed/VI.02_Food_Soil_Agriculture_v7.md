# VI.02 — Food, Soil, and Agriculture as Cultivation Across Nested Scales

## Part VI — The Material Substrate

*Fifth-pass designation: Document P. This document is the second document of Part VI, specifying food, soil, and agriculture as scale-matched cultivation, resting on the land-relation substrate of Document VI.01.*

Agriculture is the etymological and the substantive prototype of cultivation. The word the sixth-pass project uses for its central constitutional move — cultivation, set against design-imposition — descends from *agri-cultura*, the working of the field, and the descent is not a verbal accident. The practice of growing food from soil is the practice in which human beings first learned, and have most continuously rehearsed, what it means to tend a living system one does not control: to prepare conditions, to plant, to wait on seasons and weather, to respond to what the system does rather than to impose a finished specification upon it, and to hand the field on, improved or degraded, to those who come after. When this project says that a constitutional order is grown rather than built, it is reaching for the knowledge that agriculture carries. A document on food, soil, and agriculture is therefore not one sectoral-policy document among the material-substrate documents of Part VI; it is the document in which the project's root metaphor is examined at its source.

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad — biocultural governance as constitutional ontology, eco-mimetic policy-making as operational method, disturbance-adapted implementation as temporal orientation — articulated in full in Document III.01 and not re-argued here. For food, soil, and agriculture the triad bears in a single direction: the planet's food-and-soil system is cultivation terrain, not a commodified-production system. The dominant framing treats agriculture as an industry — a sector that converts inputs into a commodity output, to be optimized for yield and cost like any other industry — and the framing has produced, across the twentieth century, a food system of historically unprecedented gross output and historically unprecedented damage to the soils, waters, biogeochemical cycles, and human and ecological communities the food system depends on. The cultivation framing treats the food-and-soil system as a living formation cultivated across nested scales: a relation between peoples and soils, watersheds, seeds, and biomes, carrying the obligations that living relations carry, and constituting the material substrate on which the polity's biocultural life is grown. This document specifies the food-and-soil system in those terms.

The document assumes the constitutional ontology and the nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, the systems-science substrate of Document V.01, the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline of Document VII.01, and the foundational-normative inheritance of Document I.01, in which land-and-resource sovereignty was specified at Indigenous primary-articulator standing. It rests directly on Document VI.01, which specified land relations as the foundational substrate of Part VI and named four land-relation conditions a constitutional architecture must cultivate; this document specifies the food-and-soil cultivation that the land relation carries. It does not perform the biological-disturbance-regime diagnostic of Document II.02's biological-regime section or the physical-environmental-disturbance diagnostic of Document II.02's physical-environmental-regime section; it draws on both as diagnostic context. It does not specify the bounded provisioning decision rights, which are the work of Document XII.09's provisioning scale band, or the eco-mimetic policy-making method, which is the work of Document XII.01; it specifies the cultivation-condition constitutional categories that those documents operationalize. The analytical work proceeds in six movements: the regenerative-agriculture and soil-health canon, with the grazing-science caveats fully engaged; the agroecology canon and the food-sovereignty doctrine; the Indigenous agricultural and food-sovereignty traditions at primary-articulator standing; the Black agrarian and food-sovereignty tradition at primary-articulator standing; the dalit agrarian critique and the peasant-and-smallholder political economy; and the constitutional implications specified across the nested scales.

# Part One — The Regenerative-Agriculture and Soil-Health Canon

The regenerative-agriculture canon engaged in this part is a body of practice and writing, developed largely within the Euro-American agricultural world across the twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, that has converged on a single recovered insight: that soil is a living system, that the dominant industrial agriculture has been mining it, and that an agriculture organized around the regeneration of the soil's biological life rather than around the chemical substitution for it is both possible and, on the accumulating evidence, more durable. The canon is engaged here at scholarly depth and as recovery — a recovery, within the Western agricultural tradition, of cultivation knowledge that the Indigenous, peasant, and traditional agricultural systems engaged in later parts of this document have carried continuously. The part engages the canon's principal contributors and then engages, substantively and at length, the grazing-science caveats that one strand of the canon requires.

Masanobu Fukuoka's *The One-Straw Revolution* (1978) stands at the philosophical headwater of the canon. Fukuoka, a Japanese microbiologist who left a career in plant pathology to farm his family's land on Shikoku, developed across decades what he named natural farming, or "do-nothing" farming — an agriculture organized around four refusals: no tillage, no chemical fertilizer, no weeding by cultivation or herbicide, and no dependence on chemical pest control. The refusals are not a program of deprivation; they are, in Fukuoka's account, the removal of interventions that each create the problem the next intervention is then required to solve. Tillage destroys the soil's structure and its weed-suppressing balance, which then requires herbicide; chemical fertilizer produces a crop dependent on it and a soil that has ceased to feed itself; the cycle is one of escalating intervention masking escalating dependence. Fukuoka's alternative — the seed pelletized in clay and broadcast over a living ground cover, the straw of one crop returned as the mulch of the next, the rotation of rice and winter grain without the flooding that conventional rice culture treats as indispensable — is an agriculture that works with the field's own tendencies rather than overriding them. And Fukuoka was explicit that natural farming is not, finally, a technique: the closing movement of *The One-Straw Revolution* specifies that the ultimate aim of cultivation is not the growing of crops but the cultivation of human beings. The line states, at the canon's source, the proposition this document's opening drew from the etymology — that agriculture is a school of the disposition the cultivation framing requires.

The North American organic tradition gives the canon its institutional spine. J. I. Rodale's *Pay Dirt* (1945) introduced to a broad American readership the composting-and-soil-biology method that Rodale had drawn from Sir Albert Howard's work on Indian agriculture, and the Rodale Institute that grew from Rodale's Soil and Health Foundation has, across the decades since, conducted the long-term Farming Systems Trial that supplies the canon with one of its sustained empirical records — a side-by-side comparison, run continuously since 1981, of organic and conventional grain systems, documenting the organic systems' competitive yields, superior performance under drought, and accumulation of soil organic matter. The trial matters to a constitutional document because it converts the canon's central claim from advocacy into a multi-decade measured record: an agriculture organized around soil biology is not a romantic preference but a documented alternative.

The contemporary soil-health movement carries the canon into working practice. Gabe Brown's *Dirt to Soil* (2018) is the account of a North Dakota farm and ranch rebuilt, after four consecutive years of weather-destroyed crops left the family unable to afford conventional inputs, around what Brown's practice distilled into five principles of soil health: minimize disturbance, keep the soil covered, maintain living roots in the ground as much of the year as possible, maximize plant and animal diversity, and integrate grazing animals into the cropping system. Nicole Masters, in *For the Love of Soil* (2019), supplies the agroecological-consulting register — the diagnosis of degraded soil ecosystems and the practical regeneration of their biology — and extends the movement's reach across the working farms of New Zealand, Australia, and North America. David Montgomery's two books frame the movement in the long view: *Dirt: The Erosion of Civilizations* (2007) traces, as a geomorphologist, the recurring historical pattern in which societies have exhausted the soil that carried them, soil being generated by weathering and biological activity far more slowly than tillage agriculture has typically removed it; and *Growing a Revolution: Bringing Our Soil Back to Life* (2017) documents, from farms across several continents, the convergent recovery — minimal disturbance, continuous cover, diverse rotations — through which farmers have reversed the exhaustion and rebuilt soil within a single working career. Wes Jackson's *Becoming Native to This Place* (1994) and the work of The Land Institute he founded in Salina, Kansas, supply the canon's most radical and most patient strand: the development of perennial grain crops and perennial polycultures modeled on the native prairie, an agriculture that would hold living roots in the ground across all seasons and years, as the prairie does, and so end the annual tillage and bare-soil exposure that Montgomery's long history identifies as the recurring engine of soil loss. Jackson's framing — that a people must become native to its place, must learn the place rather than impose upon it — names the disposition the whole canon is reaching toward and that this document specifies as a cultivation condition.

One strand of the canon requires the document to engage a genuine scientific controversy substantively rather than to pass it over, and the document does so here. Allan Savory's *Holistic Management* (1988) advances holistic planned grazing: the proposition that livestock, concentrated into dense herds and moved frequently across the land in a planned rotation that mimics the behavior of wild grazing herds under predator pressure, can restore degraded grassland, reverse desertification, and — in the strong form Savory advanced in widely circulated public lectures — sequester enough atmospheric carbon in grassland soils to reverse climate change. The holistic-management framework also contributes a genuinely useful decision-making method, one that requires a land manager to set goals across ecological, social, and financial dimensions together rather than optimizing any one alone. The integration of grazing animals into cropping systems is, moreover, a real and well-supported element of the regenerative canon, and it appears in Gabe Brown's fifth principle for sound reasons: well-managed grazing can extend ground cover, cycle nutrients, and improve soil biology.

But Savory's strong claims do not survive contact with the controlled rangeland-science literature, and a constitutional document must say so plainly, because constitutional architecture must not be built on an unsupported empirical claim. Briske and colleagues, in "Rotational Grazing on Rangelands: Reconciliation of Perception and Experimental Evidence" (2008), published in *Rangeland Ecology and Management*, synthesized the large body of controlled grazing experiments and found that rotational grazing has not, in the experimental record, produced the consistent superiority over continuous grazing — in plant production, in animal production, or in rangeland condition — that its proponents perceive in practice. The paper named the gap between perception and experiment as the phenomenon itself requiring explanation. The broader rangeland-science critique notes that Savory's own early Charter Grazing Trials in Zimbabwe did not replicate the dramatic outcomes the public claims describe, and that the desertification-reversal results reported from holistically managed properties have generally not been demonstrated under the controlled conditions that would distinguish the grazing method from confounding factors of climate, site, and management intensity. On the carbon claim, the synthesis is firmer still: the Food Climate Research Network's *Grazed and Confused* report and the associated Carbon Brief analysis specify that grazing-land soil-carbon sequestration is real but bounded — it is modest in magnitude, it saturates as soils approach a new equilibrium, it is reversible if management lapses, and it cannot, at any plausible global scale, offset more than a small fraction of the greenhouse-gas emissions that livestock production itself generates. The claim that managed grazing could reverse climate change is not supported.

The document's analytical resolution holds both findings together. Regenerative grazing belongs in the regenerative canon for its documented contributions: the integration of animals into cropping systems, the local improvements in ground cover and soil biology achievable under attentive management, and the holistic decision framework's insistence on goals across dimensions. The strong climate claim does not belong in a constitutional architecture, because the controlled evidence does not bear it, and a cultivation framing that staked the food system's climate response on grazing carbon would have committed the design-imposition error in a particularly costly form — building structure on a claim the evidence does not support, and risking the expansion of livestock production under a climate justification the science withdraws. The caveat is the model for how this document engages the whole regenerative canon: with credit for the recovered insight that soil is living and must be regenerated, and with the discipline to hold each empirical claim to the evidence that is actually available for it.

# Part Two — Agroecology and the Food-Sovereignty Doctrine

Where the regenerative canon of Part One developed largely as practice in search of its science, the agroecology canon engaged in this part developed as a science that placed itself, deliberately, in the service of a movement — and that has been, from its formation, explicit that the farming systems it studies as models are the traditional, peasant, and Indigenous systems engaged in this document's later parts at primary-articulator standing. Agroecology is therefore the canon through which the Western agricultural sciences have most directly acknowledged that the cultivation knowledge they are recovering was carried, and is carried, by the traditions Parts Three through Five engage. The part engages the science, the food-sovereignty doctrine that the agroecology movement carries, and the peasant political economy that frames both.

Miguel Altieri's *Agroecology: The Science of Sustainable Agriculture* (1995) established agroecology as a discipline: the application of ecological principles to the design and management of agroecosystems. Altieri's analytical move was to treat the farm not as a site where inputs are converted to output but as an ecosystem — an assemblage of soils, plants, animals, insects, microbes, and water flows whose interactions can be cultivated toward stability, fertility, and pest regulation, or can be suppressed and replaced by purchased inputs. And Altieri was explicit that the models for ecologically sound agroecosystem design are the traditional and peasant farming systems of Latin America and beyond — systems that have sustained fertility and managed pests through diversity, rotation, and intercropping across centuries, and that the agricultural sciences had dismissed as backward precisely because they did not resemble the input-intensive monoculture the sciences had been built to serve. Stephen Gliessman's *Agroecology: The Ecology of Sustainable Food Systems*, in its third edition of 2014, carried the discipline to its mature framing: agroecology as at once a science, a practice, and a movement, and the transformation of a food system as a sequence of levels — from improving the efficiency of industrial inputs, through substituting ecological processes for those inputs, to the redesign of the whole agroecosystem, and finally to the re-establishment of direct connections between those who grow food and those who eat it and the rebuilding of a food system on the values of equity and sustainability. The FAO's adoption of an agroecology framework, and its articulation of ten interlocking elements of agroecology — among them diversity, the co-creation and sharing of knowledge, synergies, recycling, resilience, human and social values, and culture and food traditions — registers the discipline's passage from a contested margin to a recognized framework within the principal international food-and-agriculture institution.

Vandana Shiva's *Stolen Harvest* (2000) and *Earth Democracy* (2005) carry the agroecology canon into its sharpest political register. Shiva's analysis specifies the industrial food system as a system of enclosure — the conversion of the seed, the soil, the water, and the knowledge of farming, all of which had been held as commons and renewed by farmers themselves, into commodities owned and sold by corporations. *Stolen Harvest* documents the mechanism in the seed in particular: the displacement of farmer-saved, freely exchanged, locally adapted seed by purchased proprietary seed, protected by patent and by the legal prohibition of saving, so that the farmer who had been the renewer of the seed becomes its annual purchaser. *Earth Democracy* generalizes the analysis into a positive doctrine — living economies, living democracy, the defense and reclamation of the commons against enclosure — and supplies the food-sovereignty movement with one of its principal theorists. The doctrine itself was given its canonical statement by the international peasant movement La Via Campesina, and the Nyéléni Declaration, issued from the Forum for Food Sovereignty held at Nyéléni in Mali in 2007, is its load-bearing text. Food sovereignty, as the Declaration defines it, is the right of peoples to healthy and culturally appropriate food produced through ecologically sound and sustainable methods, and their right to define their own food and agriculture systems. The definition is constitutionally precise, and the document carries it forward as a constitutional principle in Part Six. Food sovereignty is distinct from food security: food security asks whether people have enough food and is satisfied by adequate supply however produced and however controlled; food sovereignty asks who decides — who controls the seed, the land, the water, the markets, and the terms on which food is grown — and treats the locus of that decision as the constitutional question. The distinction between sufficiency of supply and locus of decision is the same distinction Document VI.01 drew, through the Land Back articulation, between consultation and consent; food sovereignty is the food-system form of it.

The peasant-and-smallholder political-economy literature frames the agroecology canon and disciplines its romanticism. Henry Bernstein's *Class Dynamics of Agrarian Change* (2010) is the necessary corrective: Bernstein insists, from a Marxian agrarian-political-economy standpoint, that "the peasantry" is not a single, unified, virtuous class standing outside capitalism, but is internally differentiated by class — that agrarian change produces accumulating and dispossessed strata among small farmers alike, and that a food-sovereignty politics which speaks of the peasantry as undifferentiated will misread the agrarian world it seeks to defend. Jan Douwe van der Ploeg's *Peasants and the Art of Farming* (2013) supplies the complementary account from the other side: drawing on the early-twentieth-century Russian agronomist Chayanov, van der Ploeg specifies the peasant mode of farming as a distinct and persistent way of organizing agricultural production — one built on a relatively autonomous, self-controlled, and self-renewed resource base, oriented to the nurture of that base across generations rather than to the maximization of extracted return — and argues that this mode is not a residue awaiting modernization but a living and even expanding form, a "repeasantization" visible in the movement of farmers back toward autonomy and ecological practice. Held together, Bernstein's class analysis and van der Ploeg's account of the peasant mode keep the agroecology canon honest: they prevent the food-sovereignty doctrine from resting on a romantic peasant who does not exist, while preserving what is real and constitutionally significant in the peasant form — the orientation to a self-renewed resource base nurtured across generations, which is the cultivation disposition itself, expressed in the political economy of the farm.

# Part Three — Indigenous Agricultural and Food-Sovereignty Traditions

The Indigenous agricultural and food-sovereignty traditions engaged in this part stand at primary-articulator standing. They are not engaged as a values-supplement to the regenerative and agroecology canons of Parts One and Two, and the ordering of the document — the Western canons first — must not be read as a ranking. The agroecology canon, as Part Two specified, was itself built by studying traditional and Indigenous farming systems as its models; the regenerative canon recovers, within the Euro-American agricultural world, cultivation knowledge that the traditions engaged here have carried without interruption. These traditions are the prior articulation. They are engaged in their own registers, under the multi-eyed-seeing discipline of Document VII.01, and the part is careful — as Document III.01's commitment requires — not to collapse the distinct traditions into a single composite "Indigenous agriculture" that would flatten what each specifies.

The Three Sisters tradition — the intercropping of corn, beans, and squash that is shared, with variations, across many Indigenous peoples of North America — is engaged here through Marilou Awiakta's *Selu: Seeking the Corn-Mother's Wisdom* (1993). Awiakta, writing from a Cherokee and Appalachian inheritance, articulates the Three Sisters not as a clever polyculture technique but as a teaching. Selu, the Corn-Mother, is the figure through whom the Cherokee tradition carries the knowledge of corn, and the knowledge is at once agronomic and ethical: the corn provides the stalk that the beans climb, the beans fix the nitrogen that feeds the corn and the squash, the squash spreads its broad leaves to shade the ground, hold moisture, and suppress the weeds — and the three together yield, on the same ground, more than any one of them could alone, while feeding rather than depleting the soil. Awiakta's analytical point is that the agronomic relation and the ethical relation are the same relation: the Three Sisters garden is a model of how beings of different gifts sustain a common life by each supplying what the others need, and the Cherokee tradition reads the garden as a teacher of that ethic. The garden's nutritional complementarity — the amino acids of the beans completing the protein of the corn, the squash supplying what neither does — extends the same logic onto the plate. The Indigenous Food and Agriculture Initiative, the contemporary Native-led policy and research body, carries the tradition into the present register of tribal food-system rebuilding and federal food-and-agriculture law. The Three Sisters is engaged here as the tradition engages it: a cultivation system that is simultaneously an agronomy, a nutrition, and an ethics, indivisibly.

The *milpa* tradition of Mesoamerica is engaged through Enrique Salmón's *Eating the Landscape* (2012). Salmón, writing from a Rarámuri inheritance, articulates the milpa — the maize-centered, shifting, biodiverse cultivation system of Mesoamerica and the greater Southwest — within what he names kincentric ecology: the understanding that the human community and the other-than-human community of a place are kin, members of a single extended family, and that cultivation is the conduct of that kinship. *Eating the Landscape* specifies that the milpa is not merely a field but a process that moves across a landscape and through time, generating across its phases a remarkable diversity of cultivated and gathered foods, and that the foods carry the identity of the people who grow them — that to eat the landscape, in Salmón's title, is to sustain the kinship and the memory together. Salmón's account also specifies the milpa as a living reservoir of crop genetic diversity: the maize landraces of Mesoamerica, thousands of them, locally adapted and continuously reselected by the farming families who grow them, are the milpa's product and the milpa their condition. The constitutional weight of this — that crop genetic diversity is not a stock held in a seed bank but a relation sustained by continuing cultivation — is carried into Part Six's specification of seed sovereignty.

The Andean *chacra* tradition is engaged through the work of Eduardo Grillo Fernández and the PRATEC collective (the Proyecto Andino de Tecnologías Campesinas). The chacra — the cultivated plot of an Andean farming family — is articulated within the PRATEC corpus as inseparable from Andean cosmovision. The chacra is not land that the family works upon; it is a member of the *ayllu*, the relational community that Document III.01 engaged through the Andean tradition, and cultivation is a conversation among the human community, the chacra, the wild or uncultivated *sallqa*, and the deities of place and weather, the *huacas* and the *Pachamama*. PRATEC's formulation — *criar y dejarse criar*, to nurture and to let oneself be nurtured — specifies the relation precisely: the farmer raises the chacra and the crops, and is raised by them in turn; nurture runs in both directions, and neither party is the mere instrument of the other. The chacra tradition articulates, with a directness the Western canons do not reach, that cultivation is reciprocal — that the cultivator is also cultivated — and the document carries the formulation forward as one of its load-bearing specifications of what cultivation is.

The Pacific Islander *ahupuaʻa* tradition is engaged through the work of Davianna Pōmaikaʻi McGregor, a Native Hawaiian (Kānaka Maoli) scholar, in *Nā Kuaʻāina: Living Hawaiian Culture* (2007). The ahupuaʻa is the traditional Hawaiian land division, running characteristically from the mountain ridge to the sea and beyond to the reef, organized so that a community holds, within a single managed unit, the full altitudinal range of resources — the upland forest, the *loʻi* terraces where taro is grown in flowing water, the lowland gardens, the shore, and the fishery — linked by the flow of water from summit to reef. McGregor's analysis specifies that the ahupuaʻa is a watershed-scale cultivation system, matching the unit of management to the unit of hydrological and ecological function, and that its knowledge is carried by the *kuaʻāina*, the rural Hawaiians of the country districts, whom McGregor identifies as the keepers of the living practices that survived the plantation era in cultural *kīpuka* — refugia, named for the islands of older forest that lava flows leave intact, from which the wider landscape can be reseeded. The ahupuaʻa tradition articulates, as a working historical system, the ecosystem-and-watershed scale of cultivation that Part Six specifies as a constitutional category.

The Māori *mahinga kai* tradition is engaged through the Waitangi Tribunal record. Mahinga kai — the customary food-gathering places and the practices of gathering them — is, in the Tribunal's jurisprudence, a category of Treaty-protected interest: the Tribunal's reports, including its findings on the Ngāi Tahu claim, established that mahinga kai is integral to the relation the Treaty of Waitangi guaranteed, and that the degradation of waterways and the alienation of gathering places is a breach of that guarantee. Mahinga kai articulates food-gathering as a constitutional interest with standing in a working legal system, and the document carries it as evidence that the food-cultivation relation can be, and in Aotearoa New Zealand has been, recognized at constitutional weight. Robin Wall Kimmerer's (Citizen Potawatomi) *Braiding Sweetgrass* (2013), engaged in Document VI.01 for the gift relation to land, is engaged here specifically for the Honorable Harvest as the protocol of food cultivation and gathering: the discipline of asking permission, taking only what is given and only what is needed, never the first or the last, using what is taken with respect, sharing it, and reciprocating — the protocol that holds a food relation within the bounds of its own continuance. And the Indigenous food-sovereignty movement is engaged through the Declaration of Atitlán of 2002, the outcome of the first Indigenous Peoples' Global Consultation on the Right to Food, which specified Indigenous food sovereignty as inseparable from collective rights to land, territory, and resources and from the cultural and spiritual relationships that constitute Indigenous foodways — and through the Indigenous Terra Madre network, which carries the movement across the world's Indigenous food communities. The Indigenous food-sovereignty doctrine engaged here is distinct from the La Via Campesina doctrine of Part Two: it is grounded in collective rights to territory and in the kinship and ceremonial relations that the milpa, the chacra, the ahupuaʻa, and the Three Sisters each carry, and the document holds the two food-sovereignty articulations as distinct rather than merging them.

# Part Four — The Black Agrarian and Food-Sovereignty Tradition

The Black agrarian and food-sovereignty tradition engaged in this part stands at primary-articulator standing. Document VI.01 engaged the Black agrarian tradition for the land relation it articulates — land as the material basis of freedom, formed under enslavement and its long afterlife, with repair integral to the relation. This part engages the tradition specifically for what it articulates about food, soil, and agriculture: a tradition in which the cultivation of food has been, across the long Black struggle in the Americas, at once the site of the gravest coercion and the practiced ground of self-determination, community provisioning, and freedom.

Leah Penniman's *Farming While Black* (2018), and the work of Soul Fire Farm from which it is written, articulate the tradition in its contemporary food-sovereignty register. Penniman's book recovers, against an erasure that has coded ecological agriculture as a white inheritance, the African and African-diasporic roots of regenerative practice — the raised-bed and polyculture systems, the composting and terracing, the agroforestry knowledge that enslaved Africans carried into the diaspora and that the historical record has too often attributed elsewhere. Penniman engages the figure of the ancestor who braided seeds into her hair before the Middle Passage as the founding image of a cultivation inheritance that survived the attempt to extinguish it, and articulates Black food sovereignty as a practice that is simultaneously agronomic, reparative, and spiritual: the reclamation of the relation to soil from the trauma in which it was forged. Monica White's *Freedom Farmers: Agricultural Resistance and the Black Freedom Movement* (2018) supplies the historical analysis. White's intervention, engaged in Document VI.01 for the land relation, bears here on food: she specifies that Black agricultural cooperatives have functioned, from Reconstruction forward, as instruments of what she names collective agency — the capacity of a community to feed itself, and so to be politically independent of those who would coerce it through hunger. Fannie Lou Hamer's Freedom Farm Cooperative in Sunflower County, Mississippi, is White's central case: founded so that Black families evicted from plantation land for registering to vote would have land, food, and the political independence that material self-sufficiency makes possible, the cooperative ran a communal vegetable operation and a "pig bank" that loaned breeding stock among families — an architecture of provisioning designed to convert agricultural production directly into freedom from coercion.

The tradition's institutional and legal dimensions complete the engagement. The Federation of Southern Cooperatives and Land Assistance Fund, formed in 1967, carried the cooperative-agriculture and land-retention tradition through the civil-rights era and beyond, and the National Black Food and Justice Alliance carries the contemporary coalition for Black food sovereignty and Black land. The structural-dispossession history that this tradition has been built against is documented in Pete Daniel's *Dispossession: Discrimination Against African American Farmers in the Age of Civil Rights* (2013), which specifies, from the administrative record, how the United States Department of Agriculture and its county-level apparatus systematically denied Black farmers the credit, the disaster relief, the extension services, and the program access extended to white farmers, and so drove a catastrophic loss of Black farmland precisely across the decades of formal civil-rights advance. The legal scholar Thomas W. Mitchell has specified one of the principal mechanisms by which Black land has been lost: heirs' property — land passed down without a will, held by all the descendants together as tenants in common, and rendered acutely vulnerable, because any single co-owner or any speculator who acquires a single fractional interest can force a partition sale of the whole. Mitchell drafted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act to close that vulnerability, and his work specifies land-tenure law itself as a site where the cultivation relation is secured or destroyed. Ashanté Reese's *Black Food Geographies: Race, Self-Reliance, and Food Access in Washington, D.C.* (2019) carries the tradition into the contemporary urban register: Reese's ethnography of a historically Black Washington neighborhood specifies that the uneven geography of food access is not a natural "food desert" but, in the term she and others insist on, food apartheid — a produced and racialized landscape — and documents the long practice of Black self-reliance, the community gardens, the informal provisioning networks, the corner-store relationships, through which the community has cultivated food access against that geography. The Black agrarian and food-sovereignty tradition is engaged, in sum, as the articulation of food cultivation under the specific history of enslavement, dispossession, and the long Black struggle for the land and the food that freedom materially requires — an articulation the regenerative, agroecology, and Indigenous traditions do not supply, and that the constitutional architecture of Part Six must hold at its own standing.

# Part Five — The Dalit Agrarian Critique and Peasant Political Economy

This part integrates two bodies of analysis that the cultivation framing of food and agriculture cannot proceed honestly without: the dalit agrarian critique, which specifies that the agrarian order is also a caste order, and the comparative peasant-and-smallholder political economy, which specifies how agricultural modernization has acted upon the world's cultivating peoples. The two are engaged together because they perform a single disciplining function — they prevent the cultivation framing from romanticizing the agrarian, by holding in view the hierarchy and the violence that agrarian systems have carried.

The dalit agrarian critique is engaged at primary-articulator standing. B. R. Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* (1936) is its foundational text, and although the address is best known for its assault on caste as such, its bearing on land and agriculture is direct and was developed across Ambedkar's wider work: in the Indian agrarian order, caste and land are a single structure. Dalits — the communities held outside and below the caste order, formerly termed untouchable — have been, across the subcontinent's agrarian history, overwhelmingly the landless: the agricultural laborers, the bonded workers, the cultivators of others' fields, structurally excluded from the ownership of the land they work. Ambedkar's analysis specifies that this exclusion is not incidental to caste but is among its material foundations, and the dalit land-rights corpus that descends from him — the sustained demand for the redistribution of land to the landless, and Ambedkar's own proposals for state ownership and collective cultivation of agricultural land as a route out of caste-bound tenancy — places land redistribution at the center of the dalit emancipatory program. Anand Teltumbde's writings on contemporary dalit agrarian conditions specify that the structure has persisted through and beyond India's agricultural modernization: the Green Revolution's productivity gains accrued disproportionately to landed castes, and dalit agricultural laborers have remained, in large measure, landless and dependent, with rural caste violence frequently triggered by dalit assertion over land and wages. Sharmila Rege's dalit-feminist scholarship specifies the further stratum the critique requires: the dalit woman agricultural laborer occupies the agrarian order's most exposed position, at the intersection of caste, class, and gender, and a food-and-agriculture analysis that does not see her has not seen the agrarian order at all. The dalit agrarian critique is integrated here, and carried into Part Six, as the standing reminder that the constitutional category of land tenure is never caste-neutral, gender-neutral, or class-neutral, and that food sovereignty in an agrarian society stratified by inherited hierarchy is empty unless it reaches the landless.

The comparative peasant-and-smallholder political economy completes the part. James C. Scott's *The Moral Economy of the Peasant* (1976) specifies the subsistence ethic that has organized peasant agricultural life: the priority of securing the household's subsistence over the maximization of return, the "safety-first" arrangement of cultivation against the risk of the bad year, and the web of customary claims — on the landlord, on the village, on the patron — that constitute, in Scott's term, the peasantry's moral economy, its socially recognized right to subsistence. Scott's analysis specifies that peasant rebellion has historically been triggered not by poverty as such but by the violation of that moral economy — by the colonial state and the market imposing claims that breached the right to subsistence — and the analysis bears directly on the cultivation framing, because it identifies the right to subsistence as a real, historically grounded moral claim that agrarian peoples have themselves asserted and defended. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (1998), engaged in Document VI.01 for the legibility critique of land tenure, bears here on agriculture specifically: the high-modernist simplification of the field — the imposition of monocropped, scientifically standardized, centrally legible cultivation in place of the diverse, locally adapted, *mētis*-laden systems that peasant and Indigenous farmers had developed — is, in Scott's account, a recurring engine of agricultural failure, and the document carries the warning into Part Six as a constraint on its own specifications. Eric Wolf's *Peasants* (1966) supplies the anthropological account of the peasant condition — the cultivator who must produce not only to feed the household but to meet the "funds of rent" extracted by those who hold power over the land — and Wolf's *Peasant Wars of the Twentieth Century* (1969), through its six studies of peasant-based revolution, specifies that the peasantry has been, across the century, a decisive political agent, and that the dispossession of cultivators from the land has been among the century's most consequential and most violently contested transformations. The peasant political economy holds the cultivation framing to the historical record: agriculture has been a terrain of extraction, hierarchy, dispossession, and revolt, and a constitutional architecture for food and soil must be built in knowledge of that, not in flight from it.

# Part Six — Constitutional Implications Across the Nested Scales

The five preceding parts have engaged the canons and traditions through which food, soil, and agriculture are to be understood as cultivation. This closing part specifies what follows for a constitutional architecture: the cultivation conditions the food-and-soil system requires at each scale of the nested-scales reference frame of Document III.01, and the constitutional categories — land tenure, water rights, seed sovereignty, the food-sovereignty doctrine, and the right to food — through which those conditions are held. The part specifies cultivation-condition constitutional categories; it does not specify agricultural-practice design, which the cultivation framing leaves to be grown locally, and it does not specify the bounded decision rights, which are the work of Document XII.09's provisioning scale band. It names what that document and the synthesis of Document XIII.01 are to operationalize.

At the biosphere and ecozone scales, the food-and-soil system is the single largest human force acting on the planetary substrate, and the constitutional condition is that the food system be cultivated within the planetary boundaries that Document V.01 engages from the Earth-system-science literature. Agriculture is the dominant driver of the transgression of the biogeochemical-flow boundary — the nitrogen and phosphorus loadings released by synthetic-fertilizer use and concentrated livestock; it is the dominant driver of land-system change, through the conversion of forest and grassland to cropland and pasture; it is a principal driver of biosphere-integrity loss, through the habitat conversion and chemical load that have made agriculture the leading proximate cause of biodiversity decline; and it is the dominant human use of freshwater. The biospheric cultivation condition is therefore that the aggregate food-system load on these boundaries be held within them across the geological and seven-generations temporal horizons — a commitment that no single farm, polity, or generation can secure alone, and that the constitutional architecture must carry as a biosphere-scale commitment in the precise sense Document VI.01 specified for land at the biospheric scale. This part does not perform the biological-disturbance-regime diagnostic of Document II.02's biological-regime section or the physical-environmental-disturbance diagnostic of Document II.02's physical-environmental-regime section; it draws on both, and specifies the food system's aggregate planetary load as the cultivation condition the biospheric scale imposes.

At the ecosystem scale, the cultivation condition concerns the watershed and the bioregion as the units of food-system architecture. The ahupuaʻa tradition of Part Three is the working historical model: a food system organized so that the unit of cultivation and management matches the unit of hydrological and ecological function — the watershed from ridge to sea — rather than the surveyed parcel or the administrative county. The ecosystem-scale condition is that the constitutional architecture recognize and enable watershed-and-bioregional food-system institutions, matched to the scale at which soil, water, and the agricultural landscape actually function. Water rights are the constitutional category that bears most directly here: the allocation of water among uses, and the protection of the flows that sustain both cultivation and the watershed's ecological life, is an ecosystem-scale constitutional question, and the document names water rights as a category Document XII.09's provisioning scale band must operationalize at this scale.

At the habitat scale, the cultivation condition concerns the agricultural mosaic — the landscape-level patterning of fields, margins, hedgerows, woodlots, wetlands, and uncultivated ground through which an agricultural landscape sustains the pollinators, the pest predators, the soil biota, and the wild species on which cultivation itself depends. The agroecology canon of Part Two specifies the mosaic's function; the milpa tradition of Part Three specifies it as a system moving across a landscape through time. The habitat-scale constitutional condition is that the architecture enable the cultivation of diverse agricultural mosaics rather than the legible monocultural simplification that Scott's analysis in Part Five identifies as a recurring engine of failure.

At the population scale, the cultivation condition is community food sovereignty: the condition, specified by both food-sovereignty doctrines of this document — the La Via Campesina doctrine of Part Two and the Indigenous food-sovereignty doctrine of Part Three — that the community of those who grow and eat food hold the authority to define its own food and agriculture system. Food sovereignty is the population-scale constitutional category, and the document carries the Nyéléni Declaration's definition and the Declaration of Atitlán's territorially grounded Indigenous articulation forward as the doctrine that Document XII.09's provisioning scale band's provisioning decision rights at the population scale must instantiate.

At the organism scale, the cultivation condition is the right to food: the right of each person to adequate, culturally appropriate, nourishing food, recognized in international human-rights law through the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights and elaborated in its General Comment on the right to adequate food, and engaged in this project's foundational-normative inheritance through Document I.01. The organism-scale category is the right to food together with the embodied nutrition and health that food cultivation finally serves — the recognition that the food system's purpose is the nourishment of actual bodies, and that a system delivering caloric sufficiency while producing malnutrition, diet-driven disease, and the nutritional hollowing that Part Three's account of the Three Sisters set against has not met the organism-scale condition.

At the organ-system scale, the cultivation condition reaches into the body itself: the gut microbiome — the microbial community of the digestive system, now understood to be shaped by the diversity and quality of the food substrate it receives and to bear, in turn, on immune function, metabolism, and health — is itself a cultivation terrain, and the food system is the condition of its cultivation. The organ-system scale specifies that the nested-scales frame runs continuously from the biosphere to the microbial community within the organism, and that the food-and-soil system couples them: the same diversity that the agricultural mosaic sustains at the habitat scale is the diversity that the gut microbiome requires at the organ-system scale, and a food system cultivated well is cultivated well across the whole range.

Two constitutional categories run across all the scales and require a closing word. The first is land tenure. Document VI.01 specified four land-relation conditions — scale-matched land jurisdiction, the Land Back question as a live constitutional commitment, common right as a recognized form of land holding, and stewardship obligation as constitutive of every land relation — and the food-and-soil system rests on all four. The dalit agrarian critique of Part Five and the Black-land-loss history of Part Four add to them a fifth bearing that this document must name: that land tenure is never caste-, race-, gender-, or class-neutral, and that a food-sovereignty architecture which does not reach the landless agricultural laborer and does not repair the documented dispossession of Black and other racialized farmers has not met the land-tenure condition. The second is seed sovereignty. The milpa tradition of Part Three specified that crop genetic diversity is not a stock held in a bank but a relation sustained by continuing cultivation; Vandana Shiva's analysis in Part Two specified the enclosure of the seed through patent and the prohibition of saving as the mechanism by which the farmer-renewer of the seed is converted into its annual purchaser. Seed sovereignty is the constitutional category that holds the seed as a living commons — the farmer's and the community's right to save, exchange, adapt, and continue the seed — against that enclosure, and the document names it, alongside land tenure, water rights, the food-sovereignty doctrine, and the right to food, as the cultivation-condition constitutional categories the food-and-soil system requires.

These categories are what this document specifies and hands forward. Document XII.09's provisioning scale band, on provisioning decision rights, is where land tenure, water rights, seed sovereignty, and community food sovereignty are operationalized as bounded decision rights at the habitat and population scales; this document names what XII.09's provisioning scale band must instantiate and does not perform the rights specification. Document XII.01, on eco-mimetic policy-making, is where the method for legislating food-and-agriculture policy in the cultivation register is specified; the agroecology canon's account of food-system transformation and the regenerative canon's recovered soil-health knowledge are what an eco-mimetic agricultural policy would draw on. Document IX.01's constitutional climate redesign supplies the climate stress-test against which the food system's biospheric load is assessed; Document X.01 supplies the political economy of transformative agricultural legislation against the agricultural-commodity political economy that Document II.02's political-economic-regime section diagnoses; and Document XIII.01 integrates the food-and-soil cultivation conditions specified here with the conditions specified across the rest of the arc into the cultivation protocol.

Food, soil, and agriculture are the prototype of cultivation and they are the substrate of the polity's bodily life. A constitutional order that treats its food system as an industry — a sector to be optimized for output and cost — has mistaken the prototype of cultivation for a machine for converting inputs into a commodity, and the soils, waters, biogeochemical cycles, and cultivating peoples that the food system has exhausted across the industrial century are the record of the mistake. The cultivation framing's alternative is the one this document has specified: the food-and-soil system as a living formation cultivated across the nested scales, with the regenerative and agroecology canons engaged as recovery, the Indigenous, Black agrarian, and dalit traditions engaged at primary-articulator standing, the grazing-science caveats held honestly, and the constitutional conditions named scale by scale. Of the food system, as of the land it grows from, the project's summary phrase is exactly true: it is grown, not built — and a constitution adequate to it cultivates the conditions of the growing.
