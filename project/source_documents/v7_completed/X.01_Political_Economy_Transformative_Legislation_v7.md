## PART ONE — TRANSFORMATIVE LEGISLATION: DEFINITION, PANARCHY-CYCLE FRAMING, AND THE ACKERMAN ENGAGEMENT

### 1.1 Defining transformative legislation: beyond the Ackermanian constitutional moment

Bruce Ackerman's three-volume *We the People* — *Foundations* (1991), *Transformations* (1998), and *The Civil Rights Revolution* (2014) — constitutes the most institutionally articulated theoretical account of constitutional change outside the formal Article V amendment process that contemporary American constitutional theory has produced. Ackerman distinguishes "normal politics," in which legislative and executive action operates within an inherited constitutional baseline, from "constitutional moments," in which sustained popular mobilization, presidential leadership, congressional supermajorities, and judicial reorientation cumulatively transform the constitutional baseline itself without formal Article V amendment. Ackerman identifies three such moments in American history — the Founding itself, Reconstruction, and the New Deal — and *The Civil Rights Revolution* extends the framework to the legislative wave of 1964 to 1965, treating it as a fourth constitutional moment whose institutional consolidation through the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and the Fair Housing Act of 1968 reordered the constitutional meaning of the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments more profoundly than any judicial decision since *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954).

The Ackermanian framework has value. It directs attention to the empirical fact that the American constitutional order has changed substantially since 1787 in ways the formal amendment process does not fully capture; that the changes are produced by political-mobilization processes operating over decades rather than by single legislative enactments; and that the constitutional doctrine through which subsequent courts interpret the resulting transformations is itself a product of the political processes rather than an independent foundation. The framework also has limitations the project's analytical lens makes legible. Three are particularly consequential.

The first limitation is that the framework imports a conception of constitutional change as discrete moments with defined onset, peak, and resolution that does not match the empirical pattern of how transformative change has actually occurred. The New Deal "constitutional moment" in Ackerman's account runs from approximately 1933 through the "switch in time" of 1937 and the consolidation of the Roosevelt Court through the early 1940s. But the intellectual, political, and institutional preparation for the New Deal extended back at least to the 1880s — through the populist movement, the Progressive Era, the labor movement, the institutionalist tradition in academic economics — and the institutional consolidation of the New Deal extended forward through the Great Society and beyond. Treating the moment as 1933 to 1937 truncates both the antecedent infrastructure and the subsequent consolidation in ways that misrepresent the actual dynamics.

The second limitation is that the framework's reliance on the formal categories of presidential leadership, congressional supermajorities, and judicial reorientation as markers of a constitutional moment systematically underweights legislative waves that produced major constitutional transformation without filling all three categories simultaneously. The environmental legislative wave of 1969 to 1976 — the National Environmental Policy Act, the Clean Air Act amendments, the Clean Water Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, the Toxic Substances Control Act, and a half-dozen other major statutes — fits awkwardly. It was enacted under a Republican administration with bipartisan congressional majorities, it produced administrative and constitutional transformations whose magnitude rivals the New Deal's regulatory architecture, and it is barely visible in Ackerman's framework. The framework's categories underweight legislative waves whose political coalitions do not match the New Deal template, even when the constitutional transformation those waves produced is comparable.

The third limitation, and the one most directly grounded in the project's analytical framework, is that the Ackermanian account of constitutional change as discrete moments with defined onset, peak, and resolution does not capture the ecological reality of how complex adaptive systems actually transform. Lance Gunderson and C. S. Holling's *Panarchy: Understanding Transformations in Human and Natural Systems* (2002), engaged at `V.01_CAS_Emergence_Adaptive_Governance_v7`, describes systems as cycling through phases of growth (r), conservation (K), release (Ω), and reorganization (α), with transformations occurring not as singular events but as cascading reconfigurations across multiple scales. Brian Walker and David Salt's *Resilience Thinking* (2006) and *Resilience Practice* (2012) carry the engagement to the practical level. Carl Folke's resilience corpus, including "Resilience and Sustainable Development" (*Ambio*, 2002), engages resilience under compound-disturbance conditions. Applied to constitutional change, the panarchy framework specifies that transformative legislation is the visible legislative phase of a longer cycle including movement infrastructure-building, elite ideological reorientation, judicial doctrinal preparation, and the slow accumulation of administrative and bureaucratic capacity. The legislation itself is the moment of release and reorganization, but it is unintelligible without the longer accumulation that precedes it and the slower consolidation that follows.

A more adequate definition of transformative legislation specifies four characteristics. A legislative wave is transformative when it (first) creates institutions or rights that did not previously exist in federal law and that subsequent administrations and Congresses cannot easily dismantle without sustained counter-mobilization; when it (second) reorders the relationship between federal authority, state authority, and private authority over a domain in ways that subsequent constitutional doctrine treats as the new baseline; when it (third) generates an administrative and regulatory infrastructure capable of sustained implementation across multiple presidential administrations; and when it (fourth) reorients public expectation about the legitimate scope of governmental responsibility in the relevant domain. By this fourfold test, the New Deal, the civil rights wave, and the environmental wave clearly qualify. The Affordable Care Act partially qualifies. The Inflation Reduction Act qualifies on dimensions one and three but is contested on dimensions two and four. The Progressive Era reforms qualify, with complications discussed below. The Great Society partially qualifies. Most other major legislation does not.

The strategic implication of this definitional refinement is that the question for the contemporary period is not whether the present moment is a constitutional moment in Ackerman's sense — that is partly the question Ackerman's framework asks but is partly an artifact of the framework — but rather where the constitutional system is in the panarchy cycle, what conservation-phase rigidities have accumulated, what release-phase conditions are emerging, and what reorganization possibilities the longer cumulative work of movements and intellectual traditions has prepared. The historical analysis that follows is organized to produce material for that latter assessment rather than to identify constitutional moments in the Ackermanian sense.

### 1.2 Panarchy as analytical category, not metaphor

The reframing on which this document turns deserves explicit statement. Across the project's prior documents, the panarchy framework has been engaged as the systems-science substrate for adaptive governance under disturbance (`V.01_CAS_Emergence_Adaptive_Governance_v7`); for the climate-stress-test architecture (`IX.01_Constitutional_Climate_Redesign_v7`); for the disturbance-regimes diagnostic (Documents `II.01` through `II.08`); and for the structural commitments of Part XII. The panarchy framework's empirical genealogy is the ecological-resilience literature descending from Holling's "Resilience and Stability of Ecological Systems" (*Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics*, 1973), through Walker, Holling, Carpenter, and Kinzig's "Resilience, Adaptability and Transformability in Social-Ecological Systems" (*Ecology and Society*, 2004), through the Resilience Alliance corpus that Gunderson, Folke, Carpenter, and Walker have assembled across two decades. The empirical reference cases include forest-disturbance cycles, fishery dynamics, savanna fire regimes, lake-eutrophication transitions, and the cross-scale dynamics of social-ecological systems in cases from the Maine lobster fishery to the Mekong delta to East African rangelands.

The analytical move of this document is to read American legislative cycles as panarchy cycles in the same sense — as adaptive cycles operating at the political-economic-institutional scale, with the four phases (growth, conservation, release, reorganization) as substantive specification of legislative-process dynamics, with cross-scale interactions ("remember" and "revolt" in Holling's vocabulary) as substantive specification of how legislative cycles couple to slower-scale constitutional and faster-scale movement dynamics, and with the contemporary moment's diagnosis as adaptive-cycle position rather than as merely descriptive characterization. The move is not metaphorical extension; it is the empirical claim that legislative cycles share the structural dynamics that the resilience-and-adaptive-cycle literature has documented in ecological and social-ecological systems, and that the analytical apparatus developed for those systems applies to legislative cycles directly.

The empirical warrant for the move runs along several lines. First, the growth phase (r) of American political-economic cycles — the post-Civil-War industrial expansion, the post-1945 mid-century settlement, the post-1980 neoliberal consolidation — exhibits the rapid accumulation of novel components, the exploitation of available resources, and the high-variance early-stage dynamics that the r phase specifies. Second, the conservation phase (K) of these cycles — the late Gilded Age, the 1970s late-New-Deal-settlement period, the contemporary late-neoliberal period — exhibits the increasing efficiency, increasing connectedness, increasing rigidity, and increasing vulnerability to disturbance that the K phase specifies. Third, the release phase (Ω) of these cycles — the Depression of 1929 to 1933, the polycrisis-era release conditions accumulating across 2008 to the present — exhibits the rapid collapse of accumulated structure under disturbance the rigidified system cannot absorb. Fourth, the reorganization phase (α) — the Progressive-Era institutional construction, the New Deal regulatory construction, the post-1968 conservative-coalition reorganization — exhibits the recombination of released components into novel configurations that may or may not resemble the prior system. The cycle dynamics are not analogy; they are the structure of legislative-cycle history when read at the appropriate temporal scale.

Second, the cross-scale interactions the panarchy framework specifies appear directly in legislative-cycle history. The "remember" function — the way larger and slower scales provide the memory and context that allow smaller, faster scales to reorganize after collapse — operates in legislative cycles through the slower-scale constitutional and intellectual-historical resources that persist through legislative-cycle release phases and become available to reorganization phases. The New Deal's reorganization phase drew on the slower-scale resources of Progressive-Era institutional learning, of European welfare-state experimentation reaching back to Bismarck's social-insurance legislation of the 1880s, and of the institutionalist economic tradition descending from John R. Commons and Wesley Mitchell. The "revolt" function — the way smaller, faster scales can destabilize larger, slower scales — operates in legislative cycles through the way social-movement mobilization, electoral discontinuity, and judicial-doctrinal innovation can destabilize constitutional architecture that had appeared settled. The civil rights wave's effective revolt against the *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), constitutional baseline, through the cumulative work of the NAACP litigation strategy and the SCLC and SNCC organizing, is paradigmatic.

Third, the framework's specification of adaptive capacity and resilience as system properties — distinct from engineered stability — applies directly to legislative architecture. Some legislative designs (the Federal Reserve System, the Social Security Act, the Clean Air Act's State Implementation Plan structure, the Voting Rights Act's preclearance architecture) embody polycentric structure and adaptive feedback mechanisms that produce durability across disturbance. Other legislative designs (the Sherman Antitrust Act's open-textured statutory commands, the National Industrial Recovery Act's centralized regulatory architecture) lack these properties and prove fragile across the same disturbances. The distinction is not arbitrary; it tracks the resilience-versus-engineered-stability distinction the adaptive-management literature has documented across ecological and social-ecological systems.

The strategic implication is that the panarchy framework supplies the analytical apparatus through which the historical waves can be read as adaptive cycles, the contemporary moment can be diagnosed by its adaptive-cycle position, and the strategic prescription can be specified as cultivation work appropriate to that position. The framework is not metaphor; it is the empirical-analytical substrate of the strategic specification that follows. The legislative-cycle-mimicking-adaptive-cycle architecture that `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v7` operationalizes as eco-mimetic policy-making method is the operational expression of the same analytical claim.

## PART TWO — THE HISTORICAL LEGISLATIVE-WAVE ARCHIVE READ AS PANARCHY CYCLES

### 2.1 The Progressive Era wave: antecedent conditions and institutional preparation

The Progressive Era regulatory wave runs from approximately 1887, with the Interstate Commerce Act, through 1920, with the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment, and includes the Sherman Antitrust Act (1890), the Pure Food and Drug Act (1906), the Federal Reserve Act (1913), the Sixteenth Amendment authorizing the federal income tax (1913), the Seventeenth Amendment establishing direct election of senators (1913), the Clayton Antitrust Act (1914), the Federal Trade Commission Act (1914), the Eighteenth Amendment establishing Prohibition (1919), and the Nineteenth Amendment establishing women's suffrage (1920). The wave is sometimes treated as preliminary to the New Deal rather than as transformative in its own right, but the treatment understates both its achievement and its strategic significance for what came after.

The political conditions that made the Progressive Era wave possible included an economic transformation — the consolidation of industrial capitalism, the rise of the great trusts, the urbanization that created mass working-class concentrations in northeastern and midwestern cities — that produced widely visible social dislocations no inherited governance framework could address. Theda Skocpol's *Protecting Soldiers and Mothers: The Political Origins of Social Policy in the United States* (1992) and *Diminished Democracy: From Membership to Management in American Civic Life* (2003) have shown how the political infrastructure that made Progressive legislation possible was built incrementally across decades, through the Civil War pension system, the women's club movement, the settlement house movement, the elite reform organizations whose membership overlapped extensively with the legislators and executives who would later draft the Progressive statutes, and the dense network of cross-class fraternal associations that constituted the American civic infrastructure of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The infrastructure was in place by 1900; the legislative output of 1906 to 1920 was its activation, not its origination. Kim Phillips-Fein's *Invisible Hands: The Businessmen's Crusade against the New Deal* (2009) and the broader political-sociology literature have engaged Skocpol's theoretical claim that durable social-policy legislation in the United States has historically required the combination of mass-membership organizations, elite reform networks, and federal structural-political openings.

Read under the panarchy framework, the Progressive wave is the reorganization phase of an adaptive cycle whose release phase was the long depression of 1873 to 1896 and the populist insurgency of the 1880s and 1890s. The post-Civil-War industrial growth phase had accumulated the trust-and-railroad architecture that exhibited K-phase rigidity by the 1880s; the cumulative dislocations of urbanization, agricultural distress, and labor conflict constituted the disturbance the rigidified architecture could not absorb; the populist movement, the Greenback movement, the early labor movement, and the women's suffrage movement constituted the early-α reorganization components that would consolidate in the Progressive legislative wave. The four constitutional amendments of 1913 to 1920 — Sixteenth (income tax), Seventeenth (direct election of senators), Eighteenth (Prohibition, subsequently repealed), Nineteenth (women's suffrage) — operate as the institutional consolidation of the reorganization phase, restructuring the federal fiscal capacity, the legislative-representation architecture, and the constitutional baseline of political community.

The decision-quality of Progressive legislation, examined through the analytical apparatus that decision-science scholarship makes available (Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011; Philip Tetlock's work in *Expert Political Judgment*, 2005; Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner's *How Big Things Get Done*, 2023), is mixed in instructive ways. The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 represents the highest-quality decision-output of the Progressive wave. Carter Glass and Henry Parker Willis, working from a year of detailed planning that drew on the National Monetary Commission's eighteen-volume study of European central banking, produced a structural design for American monetary policy that has survived in essential form for over a century. The Act's central institutional innovation — a quasi-governmental Federal Reserve Board with twelve regional reserve banks, designed to balance financial-center expertise with regional representation and to insulate monetary policy from direct electoral pressure — anticipates by decades the polycentric-governance principles Elinor Ostrom would later articulate in *Governing the Commons* (1990). Whether the actual Federal Reserve has performed well by these criteria is a separate question; the design, considered as institutional engineering, reflects analytical sophistication most American legislation does not achieve.

The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 represents the opposite pole. The Act was drafted with such ambiguity — prohibiting "every contract, combination... or conspiracy, in restraint of trade or commerce" — that the courts spent the next four decades constructing the actual content of antitrust doctrine through case law, with results varying dramatically across judicial administrations. Senator John Sherman expressed in floor debate his uncertainty about the legal meaning of the language he was sponsoring. Flyvbjerg's identification of optimism bias in megaproject planning recognizes precisely this pattern of legislators committing to broad statutory commands without specifying implementation mechanisms, on the assumption that subsequent administrative or judicial processes will fill in operational content. When the subsequent processes are captured by regulated industries — as antitrust enforcement substantially was in the period from *United States v. E. C. Knight Co.*, 156 U.S. 1 (1895), through the Taft administration — the broad statutory commands generate outcomes bearing little relationship to the political coalition's intentions.

The constitutional and institutional obstacles the Progressive wave overcame are instructive for the project's larger argument. The Sixteenth Amendment, authorizing the federal income tax, was necessary because the Supreme Court in *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.*, 157 U.S. 429 (1895), had struck down the income tax as an unapportioned direct tax forbidden by Article I, Section 9. The Seventeenth Amendment was necessary because Article I, Section 3 had vested the choice of senators in state legislatures, an arrangement indistinguishable by the late nineteenth century from direct corporate purchase. The Nineteenth Amendment was necessary because the original constitutional text had simply omitted women from the political community, and a century of interpretive practice had treated the omission as authoritative. The Article V amendment process, now widely treated as practically unavailable, was used four times in seven years (1913 to 1920) to address Progressive Era reform priorities. This historical fact is important for the contemporary analysis in Part Four.

The Progressive wave's most important strategic lesson, viewed through the project's framework, is that the wave did not occur in isolation. It was preceded by four decades of movement infrastructure-building — labor organizing, women's club federations, settlement houses, muckraking journalism, university social science, urban reform politics — that constructed the political coalition, the elite ideological commitments, and the administrative expertise the legislative wave required. The legislation of 1906 to 1920 was the harvest of organizing that began in the 1870s. This temporal pattern, which the panarchy framework predicts and which Skocpol's institutional analysis confirms, reappears in every subsequent transformative wave.

### 2.2 The New Deal wave: crisis, coalition, and the construction of the modern administrative state

The New Deal legislative wave runs from the Hundred Days legislation of March-June 1933 through the consolidation of the Second New Deal in 1935 and the legislative aftermath through 1938. The wave includes the Agricultural Adjustment Act (1933, struck down in *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1 (1936), and re-enacted in 1938), the National Industrial Recovery Act (1933, struck down in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935)), the Glass-Steagall Banking Act (1933), the Securities Act (1933) and Securities Exchange Act (1934), the Federal Emergency Relief Act (1933), the Tennessee Valley Authority Act (1933), the Civilian Conservation Corps Act (1933), the Public Works Administration legislation (1933), the Social Security Act (1935), the National Labor Relations Act (1935), the Banking Act (1935), the Public Utility Holding Company Act (1935), the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act (1936), the Fair Labor Standards Act (1938), and a body of additional regulatory and relief legislation. The wave's scope, regulatory ambition, and institutional consequences exceed those of any other legislative period in American history with the possible exception of the Reconstruction amendments and statutes.

The material conditions that produced the New Deal coalition were extreme and unmistakable. By March 1933, when Franklin Delano Roosevelt was inaugurated, unemployment had reached approximately 25 percent of the civilian labor force; gross domestic product had fallen by approximately 30 percent from its 1929 peak; the banking system had largely collapsed (with bank holidays declared in thirty-eight states by inauguration day); agricultural prices had fallen by roughly 60 percent; industrial production had fallen to roughly half its 1929 level. The conditions were not merely those of severe recession but of systemic institutional failure on a scale no other American crisis since the Civil War has approached. The 1932 election produced unified Democratic control of Congress with majorities of 313 to 117 in the House and 59 to 36 in the Senate, alongside Roosevelt's 472 to 59 electoral college victory. The political coalition included industrial labor (organized and unorganized), tenant farmers, urban ethnic populations, African Americans in the few northern cities where they could vote, southern whites whose loyalty to the Democratic Party preceded any New Deal commitments, intellectuals and reformers from the Progressive tradition, and a fraction of the business class — particularly in finance and consumer goods — who had concluded that capitalism's survival required regulatory reconstruction.

Read under the panarchy framework, the New Deal sits at the release-and-reorganization transition of a panarchy cycle whose conservation phase was the late-Gilded-Age K phase that the Progressive wave had partly reorganized but whose financial-and-industrial architecture had reconsolidated across the 1920s. The 1929-1933 collapse was a release event of the kind the K phase's brittleness predicts: the financial-and-industrial architecture's high connectivity (the integrated banking-securities system, the integrated industrial-agricultural credit system) propagated initial disturbance through the entire system at speeds the regulatory architecture could not contain. The New Deal reorganization phase drew on the "remember" resources accumulated in the Progressive Era — institutionalist economics, settlement-house policy expertise, the labor-organizing tradition descending from the AFL and the rising CIO, the European welfare-state precedents of Bismarckian Germany and Edwardian Britain — and recombined them into the new regulatory and welfare-state architecture that would persist as the New Deal settlement for the subsequent four decades.

The movement infrastructure that produced the coalition had been built over decades. The American Federation of Labor and the rising industrial union movement (consolidating into the Congress of Industrial Organizations in 1935) supplied organized working-class capacity. The National Consumers' League, the National Women's Trade Union League, the Settlement House movement, and the reform-organization network descending from the Progressive Era supplied policy expertise and the female reform leadership Frances Perkins (first female cabinet secretary in American history) would bring into the federal government. The university-based social science of the Wisconsin School (John R. Commons, Edwin Witte, Wilbur Cohen) and the Columbia institutionalist tradition (Wesley Mitchell, Adolph Berle, Rexford Tugwell) supplied the intellectual framework and the personnel for the administrative agencies the New Deal would create. Frances Fox Piven and Richard Cloward in *Poor People's Movements: Why They Succeed, How They Fail* (1977) have argued that the unemployment councils and tenant councils that mobilized the urban poor in 1932 and 1933 were essential to forcing the political response the New Deal represented; their argument that movement disruption rather than orderly lobbying produced the political opening for transformative legislation is supported by the timing and content of the 1933 emergency legislation.

The decision-quality of early New Deal legislation, examined under the conditions Tetlock identifies as adequate to good political judgment, was strikingly poor. The first hundred days of the Roosevelt administration produced fifteen major statutes, drafted at extraordinary speed, often by small teams working over weekends, with limited deliberation either within the executive branch or in Congress. The Emergency Banking Act of 1933 was drafted on Sunday, March 5, introduced into the House on Thursday, March 9, passed unanimously by voice vote in the House without a printed copy of the bill having been distributed to most members, passed by the Senate the same evening, and signed by Roosevelt that night. The National Industrial Recovery Act was drafted in approximately six weeks by a small group operating outside the regular legislative drafting processes, and produced an administrative structure (the National Recovery Administration) the Supreme Court would unanimously reject in *Schechter Poultry* as an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power.

The poor decision-quality of early legislation is partly explained by what Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect theory predicts: under conditions of severe loss, decision-makers prefer high-variance gambles to certain continuation of the loss state. The Roosevelt administration was making policy under exactly these conditions, and the appetite for legislative experimentation under crisis conditions produced both its successes and its constitutional and operational failures. The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 was struck down in *Butler*. The National Industrial Recovery Act was struck down in *Schechter Poultry*. Sections of the National Bituminous Coal Conservation Act were struck down in *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238 (1936). By the 1936 election the Roosevelt administration was operating in a constitutional environment that had effectively reversed substantial portions of its initial program.

The decision-quality of the second New Deal — the legislation enacted from 1935 onward — was dramatically better, for reasons the decision-science literature makes legible. The Social Security Act of 1935 was drafted by the Committee on Economic Security under Frances Perkins's chairmanship over approximately a year, drawing on detailed actuarial studies, comparative analysis of European social insurance systems (Germany's was nearly fifty years old), and extensive consultation with the policy experts trained in the Wisconsin School tradition. Edwin Witte's *The Development of the Social Security Act* (1962), drawing on his contemporaneous notes as the Committee's executive director, documents the deliberative process in detail; what emerges is legislation whose institutional architecture — old-age insurance, unemployment insurance, public assistance, the federal-state administrative structure that implements them — has proven remarkably durable across nine decades. The National Labor Relations Act of 1935, drafted by Senator Robert Wagner's office over multiple sessions and informed by the experience of the National Industrial Recovery Act's labor provisions, similarly reflects sustained institutional design rather than crisis improvisation.

The contrast between the first hundred days and the legislation of 1935 to 1938 illustrates a pattern decision science would predict. Under conditions of acute crisis with limited information, even sophisticated decision-makers will produce poorly designed legislation subsequent processes must repair or replace. Under conditions of moderate crisis with sustained deliberation and access to relevant expertise, the same political coalitions can produce legislation whose institutional architecture is genuinely innovative and durable. The strategic implication, for the contemporary analysis, is that the question of what kind of legislative process the political conditions actually permit matters as much as the question of whether transformative legislation is politically possible. A legislative wave produced under conditions like March 1933 will likely produce statutes the legal and constitutional system will dismantle within five years; a wave produced under conditions like 1935 will produce statutes that endure.

The constitutional crisis of 1935 to 1937 deserves engagement on its own terms because it constitutes one of the principal episodes in which the constraints of the inherited constitutional architecture confronted the demands of transformative legislation. The Supreme Court's *Lochner*-era jurisprudence, developed across four decades from *Lochner v. New York*, 198 U.S. 45 (1905), through *Schechter Poultry* and *Butler* and *Carter Coal*, operated on a substantive due-process framework constraining federal and state regulatory authority in ways the New Deal's drafters confronted as structural barrier. Roosevelt's response — the court-packing plan of February 1937, formally the Judicial Procedures Reform Bill — proposed adding up to six new Justices to the Supreme Court, ostensibly on the basis of judicial efficiency but transparently to overcome the constitutional invalidation of New Deal legislation. The plan failed legislatively, but the political pressure it produced, combined with the cumulative academic and judicial work of the legal-realist tradition that had been delegitimating *Lochner*-era jurisprudence for two decades, produced the "switch in time that saved nine": Justice Owen Roberts's vote shifts in *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish*, 300 U.S. 379 (1937), and *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1 (1937), that effectively ended the *Lochner* era and inaugurated the New Deal constitutional settlement under which the Commerce Clause, the Spending Clause, and the administrative state would operate without serious constitutional constraint for approximately the next four decades. Barry Cushman's *Rethinking the New Deal Court: The Structure of a Constitutional Revolution* (1998) has complicated the conventional account by suggesting the doctrinal shifts had been underway since the early 1930s and were not simply a response to political pressure; the historiographical revision does not displace the strategic significance.

The strategic significance of the 1937 episode for the project's analytical framework is that the episode demonstrates that constitutional doctrine is responsive to political pressure of sufficient magnitude, that the doctrinal framework constraining transformative legislation is not a stable feature of American constitutionalism but a contingent product of judicial composition and political conditions, and that judicial transformation is essential to legislative durability. It also demonstrates that the cumulative legal-academic work that prepared the doctrinal ground for the post-1937 settlement — the legal-realist tradition associated with Karl Llewellyn, Felix Cohen, Roscoe Pound, Louis Brandeis, and the Harvard and Columbia legal academies of the 1920s and 1930s — operated on a multi-decade time horizon during which its strategic significance was not legible to the actors involved.

The biomimicry and adaptive-management apparatus identifies particular New Deal statutes as more or less ecologically intelligent in their design. The Tennessee Valley Authority, established by the TVA Act of 1933, is the closest the New Deal came to a genuinely polycentric, watershed-scale institution of the kind Ostrom's commons-governance work would later valorize. The TVA's structure — a federal corporation with regional governance, integrated mandates spanning flood control, navigation, agriculture, electricity generation, and economic development, and a watershed-scale jurisdiction crossing state boundaries — anticipated many of the institutional-design principles contemporary ecological-governance scholarship has rediscovered. The TVA's actual record is mixed (its early decades involved racial discrimination, community displacement, and ecological transformation of the river system that contemporary ecological standards would not endorse), but its institutional architecture remains the most innovative thing the New Deal produced. The Soil Conservation Service, established by the Soil Conservation Act of 1935 in response to the Dust Bowl, similarly reflected ecological intelligence. Hugh Hammond Bennett, the Service's first chief, had argued for two decades that American agricultural practice was producing soil erosion at rates incompatible with long-term agricultural viability. The Service's institutional design — federal technical assistance combined with locally governed Soil Conservation Districts that adapted federal practice to local agroecological conditions — implemented a polycentric structure managing the federal-local tension constructively, and connects forward to the soil-and-agriculture cultivation `VI.02_Food_Soil_Agriculture_v7` specifies.

The Social Security Act presents a more complicated ecological-design picture. Its old-age insurance program, with its trust-fund structure and its actuarial pay-as-you-go financing, has shown remarkable durability across nine decades of political and economic transformation; the program has adapted to extended life expectancy, dramatic demographic shifts, multiple inflationary episodes, and repeated political attacks without losing its core structure. The unemployment insurance program, by contrast, was designed as a federal-state hybrid with substantial state-level discretion, and the variation across states has produced systematic underprotection in the southern states whose conservative Democratic senators required the federalist structure as the price of their votes. The federalist concession Roosevelt accepted to obtain Social Security passage in 1935 has produced ninety years of unemployment-insurance underperformance in the states that needed it most, an enduring institutional cost the legislation's drafters recognized but were unable to avoid.

### 2.3 The civil rights wave: movement, litigation, and coercive federal power

The civil rights legislative wave runs from the Civil Rights Act of 1957 through the Civil Rights Act of 1968 and includes the Civil Rights Act of 1957, the Civil Rights Act of 1960, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and the Fair Housing Act of 1968. The wave is the case in which movement infrastructure most visibly produced legislative transformation under conditions of relatively contained crisis. Unlike the New Deal, the civil rights wave was not produced by a generalized economic catastrophe affecting the entire population; it was produced by a sustained, organized, geographically focused movement that imposed political costs on the federal government substantial enough to overcome the long-standing Senate veto on civil rights legislation that had defeated every serious effort since Reconstruction.

The constitutional precursors to the civil rights statutes ran through the litigation strategy Charles Hamilton Houston, Thurgood Marshall, and the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund executed across the four decades from the 1930s to the 1950s. Houston's strategic insight, articulated when he became Vice-Dean of Howard Law School in 1929, was that the *Plessy v. Ferguson* doctrine of "separate but equal" could be challenged most effectively by demanding the actual equality the doctrine purported to require — equal physical facilities, equal teacher qualifications, equal libraries, equal funding — in jurisdictions whose segregation systems made that equality economically impossible. The strategy ran from *Murray v. Pearson*, 169 Md. 478 (1936), through *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 305 U.S. 337 (1938), through *Sipuel v. Board of Regents*, 332 U.S. 631 (1948), through *Sweatt v. Painter*, 339 U.S. 629 (1950), through *McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents*, 339 U.S. 637 (1950), and finally to *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954) and 349 U.S. 294 (1955). The strategy was sustained across multiple generations of lawyers, multiple administrations of NAACP leadership, and multiple cycles of political backlash, and it produced the constitutional doctrine the legislative wave of the 1960s would extend.

The decision-quality of the litigation strategy was extraordinary by any standard the decision-science literature offers. Houston, Marshall, and the lawyers around them executed what Tetlock and Dan Gardner's *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction* (2015) would recognize as superforecasting practice: they made specific, falsifiable predictions about which cases the Court would accept and decide favorably; they updated those predictions across each iteration; they declined cases that did not match the strategic profile; they sequenced their cases to build doctrine cumulatively rather than seeking comprehensive rulings prematurely. The decision to pursue equality in graduate education before challenging segregation in primary and secondary education reflected analytical sophistication most contemporary social-movement litigation has not matched.

The movement infrastructure that produced the legislative wave was even more extensive than the litigation infrastructure. Charles Payne's *I've Got the Light of Freedom: The Organizing Tradition and the Mississippi Freedom Struggle* (1995) documents the half-century of organizing in the Mississippi Delta that preceded the dramatic events of 1964 and 1965. Ella Baker's organizing tradition, descending from her work with the NAACP youth councils in the 1940s, through the Southern Christian Leadership Conference in the late 1950s, into her formative role in the founding of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee in 1960, supplied the organizational philosophy and the on-the-ground capacity the Mississippi Freedom Summer of 1964 would require. The Highlander Folk School in Tennessee, founded in 1932 by Myles Horton and Don West, supplied the educational infrastructure that trained organizers across the southern movement. The historically Black colleges, the Black church networks, the labor unions of A. Philip Randolph's Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters tradition, and the women's club networks of figures like Mary McLeod Bethune supplied the institutional substrate. Doug McAdam's *Political Process and the Development of Black Insurgency, 1930-1970* (1982) documents the political-process model by which the slow accumulation of organizational capacity, combined with the changing political-opportunity structure, produced the movement that became visible to white observers only in the early 1960s. Taylor Branch's three-volume *America in the King Years* — *Parting the Waters* (1988), *Pillar of Fire* (1998), and *At Canaan's Edge* (2006) — provides the comprehensive narrative of the period.

Read under the panarchy framework, the civil rights wave is the reorganization phase of an adaptive cycle whose conservation phase was the post-Reconstruction Jim Crow architecture and whose release dynamics were generated through the cumulative pressure of the Great Migration, the Second World War's mobilization of African American military and industrial labor, the post-war reorientation of American foreign-policy commitments under Cold War competition with the Soviet Union, and the cumulative legal and movement pressure the NAACP and the southern organizing tradition had built. The wave's reorganization output — the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the Fair Housing Act of 1968, and the constitutional doctrine *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241 (1964), and *Katzenbach v. McClung*, 379 U.S. 294 (1964), constructed — restructured the federalism architecture, the constitutional baseline of antidiscrimination law, and the public-accommodation regulatory framework in ways subsequent constitutional doctrine has substantially constrained but not reversed.

The decision-quality of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, examined under the analytical framework decision science makes available, was substantially higher than the early New Deal legislation. The bill had been drafted by the Department of Justice over many months, refined across multiple congressional iterations following Kennedy's assassination, and informed by a quarter-century of accumulated legal and policy work. Title VI, prohibiting discrimination in federally funded programs, drew on prior administrative practice in the federal contracting context. Title VII, the employment-discrimination provisions, drew on the experience of the Fair Employment Practices Committee that Roosevelt had established by executive order in 1941 and on the state-level fair-employment-practices laws adopted in approximately twenty states. Title II, the public-accommodations provisions, drew on the constitutional analysis the Justice Department and academic commentators had developed across the years following the *Civil Rights Cases*, 109 U.S. 3 (1883). The Act's institutional architecture — administrative enforcement through the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, judicial enforcement through private causes of action, federal funding leverage through Title VI's compliance mechanism — reflected sustained design rather than crisis improvisation.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 was, if anything, more carefully designed. The bill emerged from the Department of Justice's analysis of why the Fifteenth Amendment had failed to protect Black voting rights for nine decades, and its core innovation — the preclearance requirement of Section 5, requiring covered jurisdictions to obtain federal approval before changing voting procedures — was specifically calibrated to defeat the institutional pattern of jurisdictional adaptation by which southern states had circumvented every previous voting-rights enforcement effort. The coverage formula of Section 4 was an empirical response to the actual pattern of voting suppression: jurisdictions with low voter registration in 1964 and the use of literacy tests or similar devices were presumptively covered, with provisions for bailout for jurisdictions that demonstrated compliance. The Act's drafters anticipated, accurately, that the southern jurisdictions would attempt to circumvent the new requirements through procedural innovation, and they built the preclearance requirement specifically to prevent this circumvention. The Act worked, by every empirical measure available; Black voter registration in covered jurisdictions roughly doubled within five years, Black officeholding increased dramatically across the subsequent decades, and the formal disfranchisement structures that had defined southern politics for ninety years were dismantled.

The Voting Rights Act's preclearance structure embodied an adaptive-management logic of the kind Carl Walters articulated in *Adaptive Management of Renewable Resources* (1986): the Act anticipated covered jurisdictions would respond strategically to the new constraint, built in monitoring mechanisms to detect that response, and authorized federal intervention to prevent successful circumvention. The Act's renewal cycles — 1970, 1975, 1982, 2006 — were structured to permit revision based on accumulating evidence about the pattern of jurisdictional behavior under the Act. This is genuinely adaptive institutional design, and it worked for nearly fifty years. The Act's ecological intelligence was, however, ultimately defeated by *Shelby County v. Holder*, 570 U.S. 529 (2013), in which Chief Justice Roberts's majority opinion struck down the coverage formula on the theory the formula reflected conditions that had improved since 1965 and was no longer constitutionally justified. The decision is a textbook illustration of what J. B. Ruhl and Robin Kundis Craig in "Designing Administrative Law for Adaptive Management" (*Vanderbilt Law Review*, 2014) have described as the failure of conventional administrative law to accommodate adaptive management: the Court treated the empirical conditions of voting discrimination as essentially static, evaluated whether the formula matched the current snapshot, and ignored that the formula's purpose was to constrain dynamic strategic adaptation by covered jurisdictions over time.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 has shown more durable adaptive capacity than the Voting Rights Act has been permitted to show, partly because Title VII's institutional structure — administrative enforcement through the EEOC, supplemented by private litigation — built in feedback mechanisms that have permitted the law's interpretation to adapt across multiple economic and demographic transformations. The transformation of Title VII from a tool focused on overt discrimination to a tool capable of addressing disparate-impact discrimination (in *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424 (1971)), to harassment claims (in *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 (1986)), to gender-identity claims (in *Bostock v. Clayton County*, 590 U.S. 644 (2020)) reflects an adaptive capacity the statute's structure permitted. *Bostock* was decided by a Roberts Court with Justice Gorsuch writing for a six-justice majority that found textualist support for protecting gay and transgender employees from discrimination — itself an illustration of the durability of the legislative architecture the 1964 Congress constructed.

### 2.4 The environmental wave: NEPA, the Clean Air Act, and the ecologically informed legislation of the long 1970s

The environmental legislative wave of 1969 to 1976 — the National Environmental Policy Act (1969), the Clean Air Act amendments (1970), the Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments (1972, conventionally called the Clean Water Act), the Marine Mammal Protection Act (1972), the Endangered Species Act (1973), the Safe Drinking Water Act (1974), the Toxic Substances Control Act (1976), the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (1976), the Federal Land Policy and Management Act (1976), and the National Forest Management Act (1976) — is the case in which legislation explicitly informed by ecological science was enacted in the largest volume in American history. The wave is also, for the project's analytical framework, the case that most directly illustrates both the possibilities and the limits of legislation that takes ecological science seriously while operating within an inherited constitutional structure designed for an entirely different theory of human-environment relations.

The political conditions that produced the environmental wave are sometimes under-analyzed because the wave occurred in a period of substantial Republican strength — Richard Nixon signed NEPA, the Clean Air Act, the Endangered Species Act, and most of the other major statutes — and because contemporary partisan polarization makes the bipartisan environmental consensus of the 1970s appear historically anomalous. Adam Rome's *The Bulldozer in the Countryside: Suburban Sprawl and the Rise of American Environmentalism* (2001) and *The Genius of Earth Day: How a 1970 Teach-In Unexpectedly Made the First Green Generation* (2013) document the conditions that made the environmental wave politically possible. The post-war suburbanization of the United States, the explosive growth in chemical and pesticide production, the visible deterioration of urban air and water quality, and the publication and reception of Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) had produced by the late 1960s a substantial cross-class, cross-partisan constituency for environmental protection. The first Earth Day on April 22, 1970, organized by Senator Gaylord Nelson of Wisconsin, drew an estimated twenty million participants — roughly ten percent of the American population — at a scale no environmental mobilization since has matched.

The movement infrastructure that supported the environmental wave was both deeper and shallower than the civil-rights movement infrastructure. It was deeper in that the conservation movement had institutional roots in the Progressive Era — the Sierra Club (founded 1892), the Audubon Society (founded 1905 in its national form), the Wilderness Society (founded 1935), the Nature Conservancy (founded 1951), the Environmental Defense Fund (founded 1967), the Natural Resources Defense Council (founded 1970) — that supplied policy expertise, lobbying capacity, and litigation resources. Robert Gottlieb's *Forcing the Spring: The Transformation of the American Environmental Movement* (1993, revised 2005) traces the organizational lineage. Samuel Hays's *Conservation and the Gospel of Efficiency: The Progressive Conservation Movement, 1890-1920* (1959) and *Beauty, Health, and Permanence: Environmental Politics in the United States, 1955-1985* (1987) provide the institutional history.

The infrastructure was shallower, however, in the sense that the major environmental organizations of the late 1960s and early 1970s were predominantly white, middle- and upper-class, professional in orientation, and oriented toward Washington-based litigation and lobbying rather than mass organizing. The labor and civil rights movements that produced the New Deal and the civil rights wave had rank-and-file organizing capacity the environmental movement substantially lacked. This difference would prove consequential. When the political coalition behind environmental regulation came under sustained attack beginning in the late 1970s — through the deregulatory turn of the Carter administration, the Reagan administration's environmental rollback, and the conservative legal movement's long campaign against environmental regulation — the environmental movement's relatively thin organizational base in working-class and racially diverse communities limited its capacity for political counter-mobilization. The environmental-justice movement that emerged in the 1980s, beginning with the protests against PCB dumping in Warren County, North Carolina, in 1982, attempted to broaden the constituency, with mixed and ongoing results.

The decision-quality of the environmental legislation was high by the analytical standards Tetlock and the decision-science literature provide. NEPA was drafted by a small team led by Lynton Caldwell, a political scientist whose 1963 article in *Public Administration Review* had argued for the integration of ecological analysis into government decision-making. The Act's central innovation — the requirement that federal agencies prepare environmental impact statements for major federal actions, with public comment and judicial review — embodied an institutional theory about how to force scientific analysis into bureaucratic decision-making that has had multi-decadal consequences. The Clean Air Act of 1970, drafted under the leadership of Senator Edmund Muskie's staff, established a regulatory architecture — National Ambient Air Quality Standards set by the EPA based on protecting public health, with state implementation plans bearing primary enforcement responsibility — that has shown remarkable durability across fifty-five years of subsequent political and economic transformation. The Endangered Species Act of 1973 established a decision rule (no federal action that would jeopardize the continued existence of a listed species) of unusual stringency, with *Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill*, 437 U.S. 153 (1978), demonstrating the Act's stringency was real rather than rhetorical.

Read under the panarchy framework, the environmental wave is a reorganization phase of an adaptive cycle whose release dynamics were generated through the cumulative ecological-disturbance literature, the visible-pollution disturbances of the late 1960s, and the cultural mobilization Carson's work catalyzed. The wave's reorganization output constructed the institutional architecture — the Environmental Protection Agency, established by executive reorganization in 1970, the Council on Environmental Quality, the regulatory infrastructure across the Department of the Interior, the Forest Service, the Fish and Wildlife Service — through which contemporary American environmental governance still operates. The architecture's institutional features — federal standard-setting with state implementation under the Clean Air Act; technology-based effluent standards combined with water-quality-based standards under the Clean Water Act; listing-and-recovery processes under the Endangered Species Act; environmental-impact-statement procedures under NEPA — embody the polycentric and adaptive-management principles Ostrom and the resilience literature would identify as load-bearing.

The constitutional vulnerability of the wave's product has been substantial. The standing doctrine consolidated in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555 (1992), has limited environmental litigation. The Court's application of regulatory-takings doctrine to environmental regulation, particularly in *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council*, 505 U.S. 1003 (1992), has limited the ability of state and federal regulators to address ecological harms when those regulations significantly diminish private property values. The major-questions doctrine the Roberts Court has elevated, particularly in *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022), has constrained agency authority to address climate and other large-scale environmental problems. The overruling of *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 603 U.S. 369 (2024), has shifted statutory-interpretation authority from agencies to courts in ways whose full consequences for environmental regulation are still working out. The constitutional architecture has, in short, been extensively used by interests opposed to environmental regulation to constrict the operational capacity of the legislation the wave produced.

### 2.5 The Great Society: elite-designed legislation without movement constituency

The Great Society legislation of 1964 to 1968 — Medicare, Medicaid (both 1965), the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965), the Higher Education Act (1965), the Immigration and Nationality Act (1965), the Older Americans Act (1965), the Public Broadcasting Act (1967), and dozens of other major statutes — sits in an analytically interesting position relative to the civil rights and environmental waves. The Great Society overlapped chronologically with the civil rights wave and was enabled by the same Democratic supermajorities, but its origins and political logic were different. Where the civil rights legislation was forced by movement pressure, the Great Society was designed by the Johnson administration and its allied policy experts, drawing on policy ideas accumulated through decades of liberal academic and foundation work but without the kind of mass-movement constituency that the civil rights wave commanded.

The decision-quality of Great Society legislation varied across statutes. Medicare's institutional design, drawing on the precedent of Social Security and on a half-century of policy work descending from the Progressive Era's failed efforts at national health insurance, has proven remarkably durable; the program's basic structure has persisted across nine presidential administrations and remains popular across partisan lines despite repeated efforts to restructure it. Medicaid's design, by contrast, replicated the federalist concession structure of unemployment insurance — federal funding combined with state administration and substantial state-level discretion — that produced systematic underprotection in the southern states whose conservative congressional members required the federalist structure. The result has been six decades of inequality in Medicaid coverage and benefits across states, with the Affordable Care Act's Medicaid expansion of 2010 and its constitutional vulnerability under *NFIB v. Sebelius*, 567 U.S. 519 (2012), continuing to play out the consequences of the original 1965 design choice. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act has been altered through subsequent reauthorizations including the No Child Left Behind Act of 2001 and the Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015, illustrating the pattern in which Great Society legislation lacked the movement-constituency protection the New Deal's most durable statutes (Social Security in particular) commanded.

The strategic implication of the Great Society experience, for the contemporary analysis, is that legislative transformation produced by elite policy initiative without sustained movement infrastructure is more vulnerable to political reversal than transformation produced by movement-driven legislation. The Great Society's most durable elements — Medicare, the immigration reform, the higher-education programs — survived because they built constituencies through their operation; the elements that depended on continuing political support without building durable constituencies were vulnerable to the political backlash that arrived within a few years of enactment. The Office of Economic Opportunity's Community Action Programs, designed to involve "maximum feasible participation" of poor people in the design and implementation of antipoverty programs, attempted a polycentric structure Daniel Patrick Moynihan in *Maximum Feasible Misunderstanding* (1969) argued was poorly designed; the actual record of community action is more complex than Moynihan's critique suggested, but the political backlash against community action contributed to the contraction of the War on Poverty.

### 2.6 The Affordable Care Act and the Inflation Reduction Act: contemporary partial cases

Two contemporary legislative episodes deserve engagement as partial cases that illuminate the conditions for and limits of transformative legislation under contemporary political conditions. The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act of 2010 and the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 represent the two most consequential pieces of social and environmental legislation enacted in the four decades since the long environmental wave closed in the late 1970s. Neither fully meets the fourfold definition of transformative legislation specified at Section 1.1, but each meets it on some dimensions and not on others, and the analysis of which dimensions and why is diagnostic for the contemporary strategic question.

The Affordable Care Act's enactment depended on the brief period of unified Democratic control of Congress and the presidency in 2009 to 2011, on the cumulative health-policy work descending from the Clinton-era reform effort and the Massachusetts state-level reform Mitt Romney signed in 2006, and on a remarkable degree of congressional procedural improvisation following the loss of the filibuster-proof Senate majority after Edward Kennedy's death and Scott Brown's election. The Act's institutional architecture — the individual mandate, the Medicaid expansion, the health-insurance exchanges, the regulatory restrictions on insurance-industry practices, and the subsidy structure — reflected sustained policy design over roughly two decades of accumulating expertise. The decision-quality was higher than the early New Deal but lower than the second New Deal, with several institutional-design choices (the inadequately funded subsidy structure, the absence of a public option, the dependence on state cooperation for Medicaid expansion) that subsequent operation has revealed as significant vulnerabilities.

The constitutional fragility of the ACA has been considerable. *NFIB v. Sebelius* upheld the individual mandate as a tax while restricting Congress's spending power to coerce state Medicaid expansion — a decision that produced the coverage gap in non-expansion states that has affected millions of low-income Americans for over a decade. *King v. Burwell*, 576 U.S. 473 (2015), preserved the federal exchange subsidy structure against a textual challenge that would have effectively destroyed the law in most states. The ACA's narrow margins of constitutional and political survival — the 5-4 *NFIB* decision, the 6-3 *King* decision, the failure of the Republican repeal effort in 2017 by a single Senate vote — illustrate the difficulty of producing transformative legislation that can withstand sustained constitutional and political assault when the underlying movement infrastructure is comparatively thin.

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 produced the largest single climate-policy investment in American history — approximately $370 billion in tax credits, grants, and loan-program authority for clean-energy deployment, electric-vehicle infrastructure, residential and commercial building electrification, agricultural conservation, and environmental justice — along with the Medicare drug-price-negotiation provisions and the corporate alternative minimum tax. The Act's structural choice to operate through tax credits and spending mechanisms rather than through regulatory mandates was deliberate: tax and spending provisions face fewer constitutional doctrinal constraints than regulatory provisions, the major-questions doctrine the Supreme Court constructed in *West Virginia v. EPA* operates principally against regulatory mechanisms, and the standing doctrine that constrains environmental litigation operates principally against regulatory rather than fiscal provisions. The Act's institutional architecture has proven partially durable through the early implementation phase, but its political-economic vulnerability to subsequent administrations is substantial: tax credits can be rescinded, grant programs can be defunded, and the implementation infrastructure depends on annual appropriations hostile administrations can constrain. The early movements toward such dismantling under the second Trump administration — the redirection of clean-energy programs, the rollback of EPA regulatory implementation, the executive orders restricting offshore-wind permitting — illustrate that the IRA's institutional architecture was designed for survival under Democratic administrations rather than for the durable transformation the historical waves achieved.

### 2.7 Six patterns from the historical analysis read under panarchy

The historical analysis produces six patterns that structure the contemporary assessment.

The first pattern is that movement infrastructure built over decades is the necessary condition for transformative legislation, with crisis conditions accelerating but not creating the political coalition. Every transformative wave was the visible legislative phase of a longer panarchy cycle — α-phase reorganization drawing on the slower-scale "remember" resources accumulated across multiple K-phase decades. The New Deal harvested the work of the populist movement, the Progressive movement, the labor movement, and the institutionalist tradition in academic economics. The civil rights wave harvested the half-century of NAACP litigation and Black community organizing. The environmental wave harvested the conservation-movement infrastructure descending from the Progressive Era. Without the prior infrastructure, the political coalition for legislative transformation does not assemble.

The second pattern is that the decision-quality of legislation produced under acute crisis conditions is systematically lower than the decision-quality of legislation produced under conditions that permit sustained deliberation, but acute crisis conditions are often necessary for the political coalition to form. The New Deal of 1933 produced the operationally weak and constitutionally vulnerable legislation of the first hundred days; the New Deal of 1935 to 1938 produced the institutionally durable Social Security Act and National Labor Relations Act. The strategic implication is that movement infrastructure capable of sustaining political pressure beyond the acute-crisis phase is essential to producing legislation whose institutional architecture will endure into the K-phase consolidation.

The third pattern is that judicial transformation is essential to legislative durability, and the constitutional doctrine that limits transformative legislation is responsive to political pressure of sufficient magnitude. The "switch in time" of 1937 was produced by the political pressure of court-packing combined with the cumulative legal-academic work of legal realism that had been delegitimating *Lochner*-era jurisprudence for two decades. The civil rights wave's constitutional sustainability rested on the Warren Court's reorientation, itself the product of the cumulative work of the civil-rights legal infrastructure. Any transformative wave the contemporary period might eventually produce will require comparable judicial preparation that the conservative composition of the contemporary federal judiciary makes substantially difficult.

The fourth pattern is that legislation operating through positive institutional construction (Social Security, Medicare, the EPA's regulatory architecture) tends to produce more durable transformation than legislation operating through prohibitions and rights enforcement (the Voting Rights Act's preclearance, antidiscrimination enforcement). Positive institutional construction creates constituencies and administrative capacity that defend the legislation across political cycles; prohibitions and rights enforcement create only the political coalition that secured the legislation, which can dissipate when the immediate political moment passes. The polycentric, adaptive-feedback architectures that embody resilience-thinking principles have shown the highest durability over time; the architectures that committed to broad statutory commands without specifying implementation mechanisms have proven the most vulnerable.

The fifth pattern is that the constitutional architecture within which transformative legislation operates is itself transformed by the legislation, but the transformations are reversible by sufficient counter-mobilization. The post-1937 New Deal settlement was substantially reversed by the conservative legal countermovement of the 1980s through the present, with the Commerce Clause framework narrowed in *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), and *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598 (2000), the spending-power framework constrained in *NFIB v. Sebelius*, and the administrative-state framework substantially constrained by the major-questions doctrine and the post-*Chevron* deference framework. Constitutional transformations are not permanent achievements but ongoing political contests whose direction depends on which side has the legal-academic and judicial-appointment infrastructure to maintain or reverse them.

The sixth pattern, which connects the analysis directly to the project's framework, is that legislative waves are visible α-phase reorganizations of longer panarchy cycles whose multi-decade dynamics structure both the conditions for the wave and the consolidation that follows. The wave's strategic significance is unintelligible without the longer cycle. The contemporary strategic question is therefore not whether contemporary conditions permit legislation comparable to the historical waves — they generally do not — but where the contemporary period is in the panarchy cycle of which any future legislative wave will be the visible phase, and what the strategic work of the contemporary phase consists of given the panarchy-cycle position the empirical analysis specifies.

## PART THREE — THE MULTI-TRADITIONAL COMPARATIVE-POLITICAL-ECONOMY ARCHIVE

The historical analysis of Part Two engages the American transformative-legislation archive at scholarly depth. Under the sixth-pass framing, that archive does not stand alone as the substantive substrate of political-economic analysis. The comparative-international transformative-legislation archive stands at primary-engagement standing alongside the American archive: the German *Energiewende*, the European Green Deal, the Latin American Pink Tide and the Bolivian-Ecuadorian constitutional transformations, the Indian and South African transformative-constitutionalism architectures, and the Chilean and Tunisian constitutional processes carry comparative political-economic weight equivalent to the New Deal, civil rights, and environmental waves. This Part engages the comparative archive at the depth the analytical task requires.

### 3.1 The German *Energiewende* and the political economy of energy-system transformation

The German *Energiewende* — literally "energy transition," but more substantively a multi-decade political-economic project of decarbonization, nuclear phaseout, and decentralized renewable-energy deployment — constitutes the most documented case of a transformative legislative-and-policy project at the energy-system level outside the American IRA precedent. The *Energiewende* genealogy reaches back to the West German anti-nuclear movement of the late 1970s and the founding of the Green Party (Die Grünen) in 1980; the legislative consolidation runs through the Renewable Energy Sources Act (Erneuerbare-Energien-Gesetz, EEG) of 2000, the post-Fukushima nuclear-phaseout decision of 2011, and the cumulative legislative-and-regulatory architecture through the Energiewirtschaftsgesetz (Energy Industry Act) framework. Conrad Kunze and Sören Becker's *Energy Democracy in Europe: A Survey and Outlook* (2014), drawing on the broader *Energiewende* documentation, engages the political-economic structure of the transformation, the role of citizen-energy cooperatives (*Bürgerenergiegenossenschaften*) in the deployment of renewable capacity, the political coalitions that sustained the architecture across three decades of partisan reorganization, and the institutional learning that distinguishes the German case from less successful European energy-transition efforts.

The *Energiewende* is analytically significant for the project's framework on several dimensions. First, the architecture is genuinely polycentric in the Ostromian sense — federal frameworks (the EEG) combined with state (Land) implementation authority combined with municipal energy-utility (*Stadtwerke*) operational capacity combined with cooperative citizen-energy ownership — and the polycentric architecture has produced the renewable-energy deployment rates (over 50 percent of electricity generation by 2024) that more centralized European energy-policy frameworks have not matched. Second, the architecture has operated across multiple government changes (Schröder's SPD-Green coalition; Merkel's CDU-led coalitions including the Grand Coalitions with the SPD; Scholz's "traffic light" SPD-Green-FDP coalition) without collapsing under partisan reorganization. Third, the architecture has built durable constituencies through its operation — citizen-energy cooperatives, the renewable-energy industry workforce, the municipal-utility operators, the agricultural landowners whose income depends on wind and solar leasing — in a pattern analogous to Social Security's constituency-building under the project's fourth historical pattern. Fourth, the architecture has shown adaptive capacity across multiple technological transitions (from feed-in tariffs to auction-based deployment, from large-scale solar to distributed rooftop deployment, from coal-baseload to gas-and-storage transitional infrastructure to all-renewable target structures).

The *Energiewende* is also significant for the limitations and costs the architecture has confronted. The phaseout of nuclear capacity combined with the slower-than-projected coal phaseout has produced higher carbon emissions in the transitional period than a different sequencing might have produced. The eastern German lignite regions have faced economic-transition costs the *Strukturwandel* (structural-transformation) policy architecture has only partially addressed. The dependence on Russian natural gas as transitional fuel, exposed by the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, demonstrated the geopolitical vulnerabilities of energy-system transitions that depend on external fossil-fuel supply during the transition period. These limitations do not invalidate the *Energiewende* model; they specify the conditions that the model's operational learning has had to confront and that any analogous American transformation would similarly confront.

### 3.2 The European Green Deal and the supranational regulatory architecture

The European Green Deal, articulated by the von der Leyen Commission in December 2019 and consolidated through the European Climate Law (Regulation (EU) 2021/1119), the "Fit for 55" legislative package, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), the Just Transition Fund, and the broader regulatory architecture across Directorate-Generals, constitutes the most ambitious supranational climate-policy framework in operation. The Green Deal differs from the American IRA in operating principally through regulatory mandates and carbon-pricing mechanisms rather than through tax-credit and spending mechanisms — the European Union's institutional architecture, with its qualified-majority voting in the Council and its co-decision procedure with the European Parliament, supports regulatory mandates in ways the American constitutional architecture (after *West Virginia v. EPA*) does not. The European Climate Law's binding 2030 emissions-reduction target of 55 percent below 1990 levels, the climate-neutrality-by-2050 target, the Emissions Trading System's extension to maritime transport and the new ETS2 covering buildings and road transport, and the Renewable Energy Directive's binding national targets together constitute a regulatory-and-pricing architecture whose political-economic structure operates at the supranational scale.

The Green Deal's political-economic significance includes the role of the Just Transition Fund (€17.5 billion across the 2021-2027 multi-annual financial framework, with substantial additional resources mobilized through cohesion policy and Recovery and Resilience Facility funding) in addressing the regional and sectoral transition costs of decarbonization. The Just Transition framework, drawing on the conceptual work of the International Labour Organization's Just Transition guidelines (2015), the European Trade Union Confederation's contributions, and the regional-policy expertise of the European Cohesion Funds, operationalizes the recognition that transformative environmental policy without distributive-justice infrastructure produces the political-economic backlash that the French *gilets jaunes* protests of 2018-2019 made visible. The architecture's strategic significance for the project's framework is that it integrates the material-substrate transition (energy, transport, buildings, industry) with the distributive-justice architecture (the Just Transition framework) in ways the American IRA has not yet matched.

The Green Deal also faces substantial political-economic constraints. The 2024 European Parliament elections produced significant losses for the Greens and the broader Green Deal coalition; the Mercosur trade-agreement negotiations have exposed tensions between the Green Deal's CBAM architecture and the developing-country trading partners' political-economic interests; the Common Agricultural Policy reform has been substantially constrained by the agricultural-political coalition; the regulatory burden on small and medium enterprises has produced political opposition the Commission has had to address through the omnibus simplification proposals of 2025. The architecture's adaptive capacity across these contestations is the test of whether the supranational regulatory architecture can sustain transformative environmental policy at the scale the climate trajectory requires.

### 3.3 Latin American post-neoliberal transformations and the Pink Tide

The Latin American Pink Tide of the early 2000s — the elections of Hugo Chávez in Venezuela (1998), Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in Brazil (2002), Néstor Kirchner in Argentina (2003), Tabaré Vázquez in Uruguay (2004), Evo Morales in Bolivia (2005), Michelle Bachelet in Chile (2006), Rafael Correa in Ecuador (2006), Daniel Ortega's return in Nicaragua (2006), Fernando Lugo in Paraguay (2008), Mauricio Funes in El Salvador (2009) — produced a regional reorientation of political economy that constitutes one of the most substantial post-1990 challenges to the Washington Consensus framework. The Pink Tide governments differed substantially among themselves — the Bolivarian project Chávez articulated was different in kind from the social-democratic project Lula's PT pursued, which was different in turn from the constitutional-refoundation projects Morales and Correa led — but the broader regional trajectory produced sustained reductions in poverty, expansions of social-policy coverage, and partial reorientations of regional-trade and regional-finance architecture.

Ariel Goldstein's *Bolsonaro: The Making of a Far-Right President* (2022) and his broader work on the Pink Tide's reversals; Federico Finchelstein's *A Brief History of Fascist Lies* (2020) and *From Fascism to Populism in History* (2017); and the broader contemporary Latin Americanist political-economy corpus including the work of Steve Ellner, Maristella Svampa, and Massimo Modonesi together constitute the analytical infrastructure through which the Pink Tide's political-economic structure can be engaged. The analytical work specifies that the Pink Tide's economic accomplishments — the substantial reductions in extreme poverty across the region between 2003 and 2014, the expansion of conditional cash-transfer programs (Bolsa Família in Brazil, Asignación Universal por Hijo in Argentina, Bono Juancito Pinto in Bolivia), the deepening of the Mercosur regional-integration architecture, the establishment of the UNASUR and ALBA regional-cooperation frameworks — were partially sustained but also partially reversed by the post-2015 conservative reaction that brought Bolsonaro to power in Brazil, Macri in Argentina, Piñera in Chile, and Áñez in Bolivia, before the partial Pink Tide return of Boric in Chile (2022), Lula's return in Brazil (2022), Petro in Colombia (2022), and the post-2025 Latin American political-economic landscape.

The Pink Tide's significance for the project's framework is on several dimensions. First, the Pink Tide demonstrates that transformative political-economic projects can operate across multiple constitutional architectures — presidential systems with strong-executive traditions, parliamentary systems, constitutional-refoundation processes — and produce substantial distributive and structural outcomes outside the institutional architectures the American historical waves operated within. Second, the Pink Tide reveals the structural vulnerability of transformative projects to the cumulative effects of commodity-price cycles, international-finance pressures, judicial-and-prosecutorial counter-mobilization (the Lava Jato architecture in Brazil being paradigmatic), and the cumulative political-economic counter-mobilization that the project's framework recognizes as the predictable conservative response to transformative legislation. Third, the Pink Tide governments' partial reversibility demonstrates that political-economic transformations operating within inherited constitutional architectures face the same recursive-counter-mobilization vulnerability the post-1980 American conservative legal countermovement has exhibited.

### 3.4 The Bolivian Plurinational State and the Ecuadorian Buen Vivir constitutional architectures

The Bolivian Plurinational State (Estado Plurinacional de Bolivia), constituted under the Constitution of 2009, and the Ecuadorian Buen Vivir constitutional architecture, constituted under the Constitution of 2008, together constitute the most substantial constitutional transformations the Pink Tide produced and the constitutional architectures that integrate Indigenous-cosmological framings with formal constitutional-legal structure at primary-articulator standing. The Ecuadorian Constitution's Articles 71 through 74 establish the Rights of Nature (*derechos de la naturaleza*) as constitutional category, with Pachamama (Mother Earth) recognized as a subject of rights including the right to integral respect for its existence and the maintenance and regeneration of its life cycles, structure, functions, and evolutionary processes. The Bolivian Constitution establishes the plurinational state with thirty-six recognized Indigenous nations, the framework of *Vivir Bien* (living well), the integration of *justicia ordinaria* with *justicia indígena originaria campesina*, and the broader institutional architecture that integrates Andean cosmovisions with constitutional-legal structure.

Catherine Walsh's *Interculturalidad, Estado, Sociedad: Luchas (de)coloniales de nuestra época* (2009), *On Decoloniality: Concepts, Analytics, Praxis* (2018, with Walter Mignolo), and her broader work on decolonial constitutionalism engage the constitutional architecture at the depth the analytical task requires. Walsh's analysis specifies that the Bolivian and Ecuadorian constitutional architectures are not merely the inclusion of Indigenous categories within an inherited European constitutional framework but a substantive transformation of the constitutional architecture itself — the constitutional ontology, the constitutional epistemology, the constitutional grammar of subjecthood and rights — through the integration of Andean cosmovisions at primary-articulator standing. Maria Akchurin's *Constructing the Rights of Nature: Constitutional Reform, Mobilization, and Environmental Protection in Ecuador* (Sage, 2015) documents the political-coalition dynamics through which the Rights of Nature provisions were incorporated into the 2008 Constitution, the role of Indigenous and environmental movements in the constitutional assembly, and the subsequent jurisprudential development through cases including the *Vilcabamba River* case (Loja, 2011) that established the substantive operational meaning of the Rights of Nature in concrete legal contexts. Roger Merino's *The Cynical State: Globalization, Extractivism, and Environmental Conflict in Latin America* (Cambridge University Press, 2020) engages the gap between the constitutional architecture's normative ambition and the operational reality of extractivist political economy that has continued to produce environmental conflict in both countries.

Eduardo Gudynas's corpus on *buen vivir*, *derechos de la naturaleza*, and post-extractivism — including *Derechos de la Naturaleza: Ética biocéntrica y políticas ambientales* (2014) and the broader analytical work — engages the substantive content of the Rights of Nature framework and the political-economic challenges of operationalizing it under conditions of continuing extractivist pressure. The Bolivian Law of Mother Earth (Ley de Derechos de la Madre Tierra, Ley 071, 2010) and the subsequent Ley Marco de la Madre Tierra y Desarrollo Integral para Vivir Bien (Ley 300, 2012) translate the constitutional architecture into legislative-operational form, with the framework articulating the rights of Mother Earth (the right to life, the right to the diversity of life, the right to water, the right to clean air, the right to equilibrium, the right to restoration, the right to live free from contamination) and the institutional mechanisms (the Plurinational Authority of Mother Earth, the *Climate Justice Tribunals*) for operationalizing the rights.

The Bolivian and Ecuadorian architectures' significance for the project's framework is that they demonstrate the institutional vocabulary through which Indigenous-cosmological framings can be incorporated into constitutional-legal structure at primary-articulator standing, the political-coalition dynamics through which such incorporation can be politically achieved (multi-year constituent assemblies with substantial Indigenous-movement participation), and the operational challenges that the gap between constitutional ambition and political-economic reality presents. The architectures connect forward to `VI.01_Land_Relations_and_Property_v7` (the land-relations-and-property analysis the project's Part VI carries) and to the Rights of Nature jurisprudence the broader contemporary international archive has continued to develop.

### 3.5 The Indian and South African transformative-constitutionalism architectures

The Indian and South African transformative-constitutionalism architectures together constitute the most institutionally articulated transformative-constitutional frameworks outside the Latin American cases, and they engage the constitutional question from positions that the comparative analysis must take seriously. Upendra Baxi's *The Future of Human Rights* (Oxford University Press India, 2002, third edition 2008), drawing on Baxi's career-long work in the Indian constitutional context, articulates the framework of transformative constitutionalism as constitutional-legal architecture oriented to substantive social transformation rather than to the preservation of inherited social orders. Baxi's analysis specifies that the Indian Constitution's Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV of the Constitution), the Fundamental Rights chapter (Part III), and the expansive interpretive jurisprudence the Indian Supreme Court has developed across cases from *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) through the social-rights jurisprudence of the 1980s and 1990s constitute a transformative-constitutional architecture that the post-1947 Indian political-economic transformation has only partially operationalized.

Karl Klare's "Legal Culture and Transformative Constitutionalism," in the *South African Journal on Human Rights* 14, no. 1 (1998): 146-188, articulated the South African transformative-constitutionalism framework in the context of the 1996 South African Constitution's substantive-equality, socio-economic-rights, and transformative-justice architecture. Klare's analysis specifies that the South African Constitution's institutional architecture — the substantive-equality jurisprudence under Section 9, the socio-economic-rights jurisprudence under Sections 26 through 29 (housing, healthcare, food, water, social security, education), the institutional architecture of the Chapter 9 institutions (the Public Protector, the Human Rights Commission, the Gender Commission), and the broader constitutional architecture of transformative justice — constitutes a constitutional framework whose normative ambition substantially exceeds the inherited European and American constitutional architectures and whose operational record has been substantially constrained by the political-economic realities of post-apartheid South African inequality.

Pius N. Langa's "Transformative Constitutionalism" in *Stellenbosch Law Review* 17, no. 3 (2006): 351-360, Sandra Liebenberg's *Socio-economic Rights: Adjudication under a Transformative Constitution* (Juta, 2010), and the broader South African constitutional scholarship including the work of Heinz Klug, Marius Pieterse, Theunis Roux, and the Constitutional Court's own jurisprudence across cases including *Government of the Republic of South Africa v. Grootboom* (2000), *Minister of Health v. Treatment Action Campaign (No. 2)* (2002), *Mazibuko v. City of Johannesburg* (2009), and the recent socio-economic-rights jurisprudence collectively constitute the analytical infrastructure through which the South African transformative-constitutionalism framework can be engaged. The framework's significance for the project's analytical work is that it demonstrates how constitutional architecture can institutionalize substantive social transformation as constitutional commitment rather than as merely permitted political project, how the socio-economic-rights framework can operate jurisprudentially even under conditions of severe political-economic constraint, and what the gap between constitutional ambition and political-economic operational reality looks like across multiple decades.

The Indian transformative-constitutionalism architecture has been engaged by Pratap Bhanu Mehta's work, including his contributions to *The Oxford Handbook of the Indian Constitution* (2016, edited by Sujit Choudhry, Madhav Khosla, and Mehta), Madhav Khosla's *India's Founding Moment: The Constitution of a Most Surprising Democracy* (Harvard University Press, 2020), and the broader Indian constitutional-law scholarship. The Indian Supreme Court's *Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996), recognizing the precautionary principle and the polluter-pays principle as part of Indian environmental jurisprudence; *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (multiple cases across decades); and the more recent climate-related jurisprudence in *M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (2024), recognizing a right to be free from the adverse effects of climate change, together constitute the operational record of Indian transformative-constitutionalism in the environmental and climate domains. The Indian framework connects to the project's analytical work through its integration of substantive social-transformation commitments with environmental-and-ecological commitments in a constitutional architecture distinct from both the European-American and the Latin American models.

### 3.6 The Chilean and Tunisian constitutional processes

The Chilean constitutional process initiated by the *estallido social* of October 2019 and the Tunisian constitutional process initiated by the Jasmine Revolution of December 2010 through January 2011 together constitute the two most recent comprehensive constitutional-refoundation processes outside Latin America, and their substantive outcomes (the rejection of the 2022 Chilean draft constitution by referendum, the subsequent 2023 redrafting process and the second referendum's rejection, the 2014 Tunisian Constitution and its subsequent constitutional architecture under Saied's 2022 unilateral constitutional changes) engage the project's framework on the limits and conditions of constitutional transformation.

The Chilean process emerged from sustained social mobilization against the post-Pinochet economic-and-constitutional order. The 1980 Pinochet Constitution, drafted under authoritarian conditions and amended multiple times across the post-1990 democratic transition, had retained institutional features (the binominal electoral system until 2015, the institutional senators until 2005, the constitutional-tribunal architecture, the institutional restrictions on labor and social-policy reform) that the post-2019 movement identified as the constitutional substrate of the economic-and-social inequalities the *estallido* had mobilized against. The Constitutional Convention (Convención Constitucional) of 2021-2022 produced a draft constitution that was the most ambitious progressive constitutional proposal in the contemporary Latin American context — articulating plurinationality, environmental and Rights of Nature provisions, expansive social-rights guarantees, the abolition of the Senate and creation of a unicameral legislature, and a broader transformative-constitutional architecture — but the draft was rejected by a 62-38 referendum margin in September 2022. The subsequent process under the Constitutional Council in 2023, dominated by right-wing forces and producing a draft moving in the opposite direction, was also rejected by referendum in December 2023.

The Chilean experience is analytically significant on several dimensions the project's framework engages. First, the experience demonstrates that comprehensive constitutional-refoundation processes operating in democratic conditions face substantial political-economic constraints that can defeat ambitious transformative proposals even when the immediate social-mobilization conditions appear hospitable. The cumulative campaign against the 2022 draft — substantial counter-mobilization by economic-elite networks, the cumulative effect of the post-pandemic economic difficulties, the strategic missteps of the constitutional convention itself (the substantial proportion of independents without prior political-organizational anchoring, the perceived radicalization of certain proposals beyond what the median voter would support) — together produced the rejection. Second, the experience demonstrates that constitutional-refoundation processes can produce regressive as well as progressive outcomes, and that the strategic-prescription work the project's framework specifies must engage the conditions under which constitutional-refoundation processes produce outcomes the framework would support rather than treating constitutional-refoundation as inherently transformative. Third, the experience connects forward to the project's framework's recognition that constitutional-transformation work operates on multi-decade time horizons, that the Chilean process has not concluded with the second referendum, and that the cumulative political-mobilization work the *estallido* catalyzed continues to operate even after the immediate constitutional-refoundation proposals have failed.

The Tunisian process produced the 2014 Constitution that, at the moment of its adoption, was widely treated as the most institutionally articulated democratic constitution in the post-Arab-Spring Middle Eastern context — establishing a semi-presidential architecture with substantial constitutional protections for civil liberties, judicial independence, electoral integrity, and decentralization. The post-2014 operational record was substantially complicated by the cumulative economic difficulties (the post-2011 economic stagnation, the impact of the 2015 terrorist attacks on the tourist sector, the pandemic-era economic difficulties), the political-coalition difficulties (the Ennahda-secularist coalition tensions, the institutional fragmentation that produced ten governments in the decade after the constitution's adoption), and the cumulative political-economic frustration that produced the 2021 political crisis and Saied's subsequent constitutional consolidations through the 2022 referendum-approved constitution that substantially concentrated executive power. The Tunisian experience is analytically significant on the parallel dimension to the Chilean case: constitutional-refoundation processes can produce institutionally articulated democratic constitutions whose subsequent operational record can be substantially constrained by political-economic difficulties and political-coalition fragmentation, and the strategic-prescription work the project's framework specifies must engage these constraints rather than treating constitutional adoption as the end point of constitutional-transformation work.

### 3.7 The integration of the comparative archive

The comparative-international transformative-legislation and constitutional-transformation archive stands at primary-engagement standing alongside the American historical-wave archive. The strategic significance for the project's framework is multiple. First, the archive demonstrates that transformative political-economic projects operating across constitutional architectures, partisan-political systems, and economic-developmental conditions face structurally similar challenges (cumulative counter-mobilization, the gap between constitutional ambition and operational reality, the temporal lag between political-mobilization and durable institutional consolidation) — the structural patterns are not idiosyncratic to American political-economic conditions but generalizable features of transformative legislation under contemporary global political-economic conditions. Second, the archive supplies institutional vocabulary the American historical waves did not draw on — the Rights of Nature architecture, the plurinational-state architecture, the supranational-regulatory architecture of the European Green Deal, the transformative-constitutional-jurisprudence frameworks of India and South Africa — that the project's framework requires for the substantive specification of what transformative legislation under the sixth-pass framing would consist of. Third, the archive's record of partial successes and partial failures supplies the empirical basis for the strategic-prescription work that follows: the conditions under which transformative legislation operates durably, the conditions under which it operates reversibly, and the institutional-design features that distinguish the durable from the reversible. The integration of the comparative archive into the project's analytical work is not decorative; it is the substantive substrate of the strategic specification.

## PART FOUR — THE CONTEMPORARY MOMENT UNDER POLYCRISIS AND PANARCHY DIAGNOSTICS

The historical-and-comparative analysis of Parts Two and Three produces the framework against which the contemporary American period can be assessed. The four dimensions the framework specifies — empirical pressure, movement infrastructure, institutional opportunity structure, intellectual readiness — operate as a diagnostic across which the contemporary period's conditions can be mapped. The honest assessment requires neither the optimism that overstates contemporary readiness for transformative legislation nor the despair that understates the assets the contemporary period has accumulated. The analytical task is to specify what is and is not present, what each presence and absence implies for strategy, and what the integration of the four dimensions reveals about the panarchy-cycle position the contemporary moment occupies.

### 4.1 Empirical pressure: polycrisis, planetary boundaries, and the political-force gap

The empirical conditions of the contemporary period are, by any reasonable measure, more severe than the conditions that produced the historical legislative waves. The climate trajectory documented in successive IPCC reports, including the Sixth Assessment Report (2021-2023), specifies global warming on track to exceed the 1.5°C target of the Paris Agreement within the next decade and to reach 2.5° to 2.9°C by century-end on current policy trajectories, with associated impacts on sea-level rise, agricultural productivity, water availability, biodiversity, human migration, and human mortality that exceed the empirical reference cases in human historical experience. The biodiversity collapse documented in the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services *Global Assessment Report on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services* (2019) specifies extinction rates running at tens to hundreds of times the natural background rate, with approximately one million species facing extinction risk. The Stockholm Resilience Centre's planetary-boundaries framework, introduced by Johan Rockström and colleagues in *Nature* in 2009 and updated through Katherine Richardson and colleagues' "Earth Beyond Six of Nine Planetary Boundaries" in *Science Advances* (2023), specifies that six of the nine biophysical boundaries within which human civilization can safely operate — climate change, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater change, biogeochemical flows of nitrogen and phosphorus, and novel entities (synthetic chemicals and microplastics) — have now been transgressed.

The polycrisis literature engages these empirical conditions as interacting compound disturbances rather than as separable crises. Adam Tooze's *Crashed: How a Decade of Financial Crises Changed the World* (2018), his subsequent essays on polycrisis, and the Cascade Institute corpus on metacrisis and systemic risk (including the work of Thomas Homer-Dixon, Michael Lawrence, and Scott Janzwood) constitute the contemporary analytical infrastructure through which the empirical conditions can be engaged as a unified disturbance landscape rather than as a disaggregated list of crises. The polycrisis framing aligns with the disturbance-regimes architecture Part II of this project carries — the five disturbance regimes (cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic) and their interactions (`II.08_Disturbance_Regime_Interactions_v7`) — and supplies the empirical-conditions specification of the disturbance landscape transformative legislation must respond to.

The inequality crisis is documented across multiple dimensions. Thomas Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2014) and *Capital and Ideology* (2020), drawing on the World Inequality Database that Piketty and his collaborators have constructed with Emmanuel Saez, Gabriel Zucman, and the broader research community, document that wealth and income inequality in the United States and most other wealthy democracies has returned to levels not seen since the Belle Époque of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Jacob Hacker and Paul Pierson's *Winner-Take-All Politics: How Washington Made the Rich Richer — And Turned Its Back on the Middle Class* (2010) documents the political economy by which inequality has been produced and sustained through specific policy choices. Anne Case and Angus Deaton's *Deaths of Despair and the Future of Capitalism* (2020) documents the measurable decline in life expectancy among non-college-educated white Americans, the first sustained decline in life expectancy in any demographic group in a wealthy democracy in modern times.

The democratic-erosion crisis is documented in the comparative-democracy literature. The Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) Institute at the University of Gothenburg, the Economist Intelligence Unit's Democracy Index, Freedom House, and the work of scholars including Steven Levitsky, Daniel Ziblatt, Larry Diamond, and Anna Grzymala-Busse have documented sustained democratic backsliding across multiple wealthy democracies, with the United States showing particularly significant deterioration. Levitsky and Ziblatt's *How Democracies Die* (2018) and *Tyranny of the Minority: Why American Democracy Reached the Breaking Point* (2023) document the institutional pathways by which democratic systems dismantle themselves under conditions of increasing polarization. The events of January 6, 2021, and the subsequent normalization of election denial as a position within one major political party, represent democratic erosion of a kind the comparative literature would identify as severe.

These conditions are, by every empirical standard the social and natural sciences supply, more severe than the conditions that produced any of the historical legislative waves. The Depression of 1933 was severe in a way the contemporary period has not approached at the level of acute economic dislocation, but the contemporary conditions are unfolding across multiple dimensions simultaneously, on longer time horizons, with more difficult-to-reverse tipping dynamics, and with consequences that affect the basic biophysical conditions of human civilization rather than the operating parameters of an existing political-economic order.

The political force of these conditions is, however, dramatically weaker than the political force of the conditions that produced the historical legislative waves. The disjunction between empirical severity and political force is the central feature of the contemporary moment, and it is the feature that demands the most careful analysis. The cognitive science of how publics process information about diffuse, long-deferred, statistically distributed harms — the conditions that characterize the climate, biodiversity, and democratic-erosion crises — is engaged across Documents `II.02_Five_Regime_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v7` and `VIII.01_Minds_Markets_Sacred_v7`. Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, the prospect-theory literature, and the construal-level theory developed by Yaacov Trope and Nira Liberman in "Construal-Level Theory of Psychological Distance" (*Psychological Review*, 2010) converge on the conclusion that human cognition systematically discounts harms that are temporally distant, geographically diffuse, statistically rather than concretely manifested, and not directly attributable to specific identifiable agents. Dan Kahan and the Cultural Cognition Project at Yale Law School have documented that the political identities through which contemporary publics process information distort that processing in ways that prevent the kind of consensus formation that historical waves required. Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber's *The Enigma of Reason* (2017) specifies that human reasoning operates principally in social-deliberative contexts rather than in individual evidence-weighing, and that the standard informational-deficit communication strategies premised on individual evidence-weighing underperform.

The political force of the contemporary crises has been further attenuated by the systematic counter-mobilization Robert Brulle's research on countermovement financing has documented. Brulle's "Institutionalizing Delay: Foundation Funding and the Creation of U.S. Climate Change Counter-Movement Organizations" (*Climatic Change*, 2014) documents how the fossil-fuel industry, the pharmaceutical industry, the financial-services industry, and the broader infrastructure of right-wing think tanks and media institutions have invested billions of dollars over four decades in shaping public understanding of these crises in ways that prevent the political-consensus formation historical waves required. The result is that the empirical severity of the contemporary conditions is not translating into political force at anything like the rate the New Deal era's economic crisis or the civil rights era's moral crisis translated into legislative pressure.

### 4.2 Movement infrastructure: substantial assets, structural limitations

The movement infrastructure available to a contemporary transformative project is mixed, with assets and limitations the analytical framework specifies. The climate-justice movement has intellectual and organizational depth — the major national environmental organizations descending from the 1970s wave (Sierra Club, NRDC, EDF, Audubon), the more recent climate-focused organizations (350.org, Sunrise Movement, the Climate Justice Alliance), the policy-focused think tanks (the Center for American Progress's climate work, the Brookings Institution's climate work, RMI), the academic research infrastructure (the major university climate centers, the National Academies' climate work), and the philanthropy infrastructure that has grown since approximately 2018. The political leverage has been limited by features that distinguish the movement from the labor movement of the 1930s or the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s: the climate movement substantially lacks the rank-and-file organizing capacity in working-class communities, in racially diverse communities, and in geographies outside coastal urban areas that the historical movements possessed; the movement's relationship to the Democratic Party has been one of policy advocacy and electoral support rather than the kind of coalition partnership that organized labor maintained with the Democratic Party from the 1930s through the 1960s; the movement's organizational forms have been substantially shaped by the foundation-funded nonprofit model whose accountability, strategic-capacity, and rank-and-file-integration consequences critics including Kyle Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi), in his work on climate justice and Indigenous peoples, and Naomi Klein, in *This Changes Everything: Capitalism vs. The Climate* (2014), have analyzed.

The racial-justice movement that re-emerged after George Floyd's murder in 2020 produced what was, by some measures, the largest protest movement in American history, with estimated participation of 15 to 26 million people across the summer of 2020. The legislative response at the federal level has been minimal, with the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act repeatedly failing to pass the Senate; the state and local responses have been more substantial in some jurisdictions and reversed in others. The political backlash, both within the Democratic Party (with substantial movement away from "defund the police" framing) and across the partisan divide (with the Republican Party's intensified embrace of law-and-order politics and its deployment of critical race theory as a political wedge issue), has constrained the movement's translation into durable institutional change.

The Indigenous-rights movement — anchored in the Standing Rock resistance to the Dakota Access Pipeline (2016-2017), the Land Back movement, the Rights of Nature jurisprudence developing in tribal courts, and the international Indigenous-rights infrastructure descending from the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) — has accumulated organizational and legal capacity the historical analytical framework has underestimated. Nick Estes (Lower Brule Sioux) in *Our History Is the Future: Standing Rock Versus the Dakota Access Pipeline, and the Long Tradition of Indigenous Resistance* (2019); Glen Sean Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene) in *Red Skin, White Masks: Rejecting the Colonial Politics of Recognition* (2014); Leanne Betasamosake Simpson (Michi Saagiig Nishnaabeg) in *As We Have Always Done: Indigenous Freedom through Radical Resistance* (2017); and the broader Indigenous-studies and Indigenous-law literature have built an analytical and political framework that integrates Indigenous knowledge traditions with contemporary legal advocacy in ways the project's framework has identified as essential to constitutional renewal. The movement's political leverage at the federal level has been constrained by the small demographic share of Indigenous peoples in the contemporary United States (approximately one percent of the population) and by the persistent constitutional structure of tribal sovereignty within American federalism, but the movement's intellectual and strategic contribution to the broader transformative project has been disproportionate to its raw political weight.

The labor movement, substantially absent from the political coalition for transformative legislation since the 1970s, has shown signs of revival in the contemporary period that may or may not develop into the organizational capacity historical waves required. The unionization successes at Amazon Labor Union's Staten Island warehouse (2022), at Starbucks (over 400 stores by 2024), and across academic and digital-media workplaces; the Writers Guild and Screen Actors Guild strikes of 2023; the United Auto Workers' "Stand Up Strike" against the Big Three automakers in 2023; and the broader rise in strike activity to levels not seen since the 1980s — these developments suggest labor-movement revival is possible. The Bureau of Labor Statistics data on union density show union membership at approximately 10 percent of the workforce as of 2024, with private-sector union density at approximately 6 percent — figures that are an order of magnitude below the union density that produced the New Deal coalition.

The structural limitations of contemporary movement infrastructure, despite the accumulated assets, are equally important. Skocpol's *Diminished Democracy* documented the decline in cross-class mass-membership civic associations whose membership and political capacity declined dramatically across the second half of the twentieth century. Jonathan Rodden's *Why Cities Lose: The Deep Roots of the Urban-Rural Political Divide* (2019) documents the geographic concentration of contemporary movement infrastructure in coastal urban areas, with limited reach into rural, exurban, and small-city communities whose political weight the inherited federalism structure over-represents. Hahrie Han's *How Organizations Develop Activists* (2014) and her subsequent work with Elizabeth McKenna and Michelle Oyakawa, *Prisms of the People: Power and Organizing in Twenty-First-Century America* (2021), specify the systematic shift from organizing to mobilizing across the contemporary movement-infrastructure ecosystem — organizations that produce durable political power are those that engage in genuine organizing rather than relying on mobilizing infrastructure, and the contemporary funder ecosystem, the digital-organizing technology stack, and the consultant infrastructure have systematically biased toward mobilizing at the cost of organizing.

The synthesis on the movement-infrastructure dimension is that the contemporary period has intellectual depth and considerable absolute scale but with organizational forms different from those of the historical waves and with rank-and-file organizing capacity in working-class communities, in racially diverse communities, and in geographies outside coastal urban areas that is less developed than the historical waves required. The contemporary movement infrastructure is sufficient to support legislative episodes of the IRA's scale and durability profile, but not yet sufficient to support transformative legislation of the New Deal's or civil rights wave's scale.

### 4.3 Institutional opportunity structure: the conservative legal countermovement and the contemporary judiciary

The institutional opportunity structure within which the contemporary period must operate has been constructed by the four-decade conservative legal countermovement whose strategic significance the project's framework must engage. The countermovement is the constitutional reaction to the legislative waves of the 1930s through the 1970s, and its success has been substantial enough that the institutional opportunity structure for transformative legislation is less hospitable than it was during any of the historical waves.

The infrastructure of the conservative legal countermovement is the most institutionally articulated product of right-of-center political organization in American history. The countermovement's foundational institutional development includes the founding of the Federalist Society in 1982 by law students at Yale, Chicago, and Harvard, with subsequent expansion to chapters at virtually every American law school and to the Lawyers Division that operates as the principal professional network of conservative attorneys. Steven Teles's *The Rise of the Conservative Legal Movement: The Battle for Control of the Law* (2008) documents how the Federalist Society's strategic design — explicitly modeled on the perceived success of the liberal legal infrastructure of the post-war period — created a recruiting and credentialing pipeline that produces conservative legal talent at scale. The institutional infrastructure also includes the Olin Foundation's law-and-economics program, which funded the establishment of law-and-economics centers at most major American law schools and produced the academic foundations of the deregulatory and property-rights jurisprudence the contemporary Court has adopted. Jane Mayer's *Dark Money: The Hidden History of the Billionaires Behind the Rise of the Radical Right* (2016) documents the broader funder infrastructure.

The doctrinal output of the countermovement has been substantial. *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), expanded constitutional protection of corporate political spending in ways that have changed the political-economic environment for transformative legislation. *Shelby County v. Holder* dismantled the Voting Rights Act's preclearance structure, with documented consequences for voter suppression in the formerly covered jurisdictions. *Janus v. AFSCME, Council 31*, 585 U.S. 878 (2018), restricted public-sector union organizing through First Amendment doctrine the Court constructed without substantial statutory or historical foundation. *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, 597 U.S. 215 (2022), overturned *Roe v. Wade* and *Planned Parenthood v. Casey*, eliminating the constitutional protection for abortion access. *West Virginia v. EPA* elevated the major-questions doctrine in ways that constrain administrative regulation of climate, financial, telecommunications, public health, and other major policy domains. *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard*, 600 U.S. 181 (2023), invalidated race-conscious affirmative action in higher education on a Fourteenth Amendment theory that ignored sixty years of contrary precedent. *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* overturned *Chevron* deference, shifting statutory-interpretation authority from agencies to courts. *Trump v. United States*, 603 U.S. 593 (2024), constructed a doctrine of presidential immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts that substantially expands executive power in ways the unitary-executive theory the project rejects has long sought.

The cumulative pattern is one of constitutional doctrine actively constructing constraints on the kind of transformative legislation this document examines. The doctrines are not stable features of American constitutionalism that the project must work around; they are the judicial output of a four-decade conservative legal movement that has had as one of its explicit objectives the constitutional dismantling of the administrative and rights-protective architecture the historical waves produced. The conservative legal movement is the constitutional countermovement to the legislative waves of the 1930s through the 1970s, and its success has been substantial.

The legislative procedural structure compounds the constitutional constraint. The Senate filibuster's effective requirement of sixty votes for substantial non-budget legislation, the budget-reconciliation process's narrow scope under the Byrd Rule, the House Rules Committee's procedural control over legislative consideration, and the broader procedural architecture under which contemporary legislation is enacted produce structural conditions in which transformative legislation comparable to the historical waves is more difficult to enact than during any of the prior waves. Norman Ornstein and Thomas Mann in *It's Even Worse Than It Looks: How the American Constitutional System Collided with the New Politics of Extremism* (2012) have documented the transformation in detail. The historical waves operated under filibuster rules that required two-thirds for cloture (until 1975) but under congressional procedural conditions that permitted bipartisan cooperation on substantive issues; the contemporary period operates under filibuster rules requiring three-fifths for cloture but under partisan-polarization conditions that have made the threshold effectively higher in operation than the formal rules require.

The state-level landscape, by contrast, is the institutional opportunity the project's polycentric-governance framework would identify as most promising under contemporary conditions. State-level innovation in climate policy — California's cap-and-trade program, the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative covering eleven northeastern states, renewable portfolio standards in approximately thirty states, building-electrification policies emerging in major metropolitan areas — has produced regulatory infrastructure that operates substantially independently of federal climate policy. State-level innovation in voting rights — automatic voter registration in approximately twenty states, same-day registration in approximately twenty states, no-excuse absentee voting in approximately two-thirds of states — has produced voting infrastructure that operates substantially independently of the federal voting-rights framework *Shelby County* dismantled. State-level innovation in labor law — California's AB 5 and similar legislation, the state-level minimum wages exceeding the federal minimum, the public-sector bargaining frameworks in states with substantial public-union density — has produced labor regulatory infrastructure outside the constraints of federal labor law. The state-level opportunity structure is, however, asymmetric across states in ways that produce systematic interstate inequality, with the states most receptive to climate, labor, voting-rights, and other progressive innovation largely concentrated on the coasts and in the urban Midwest, and the states most resistant concentrated in the South and the rural West.

### 4.4 Intellectual readiness: substantial assets, integration challenges

The intellectual readiness of the contemporary period is the dimension on which the contemporary period most clearly exceeds the historical waves. The scientific understanding of the domains the project's framework engages — climate, biodiversity, ecological economics, decision quality, cognitive bias, polycentric governance, biomimicry, Indigenous knowledge — provides analytical resources the historical waves' intellectual infrastructure did not approach. The implication is not that intellectual readiness is sufficient for transformative legislation — it manifestly is not — but that the intellectual foundation for any future transformative wave the contemporary period might eventually produce is more sophisticated than the foundations the historical waves operated from.

The intellectual assets are extensive. The climate-science infrastructure including the IPCC, the World Climate Research Programme, and the network of national climate-research programs has produced empirical understanding of the climate system whose precision and sophistication exceed any prior period. The ecological-economics literature including the work of Herman Daly, Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen, Robert Costanza, Joan Martinez-Alier, and the broader scholarly community has produced an analytical framework for the embedded-economy paradigm that constitutes an alternative to the inherited neoclassical framework. The decision-science literature including the work of Kahneman, Tversky, Gerd Gigerenzer, Tetlock, Cass Sunstein, Flyvbjerg, Robert Lempert, and the broader community has produced analytical resources for evaluating decision quality the historical waves did not have. The polycentric-governance literature descending from Ostrom's work has produced an institutional framework that integrates the multi-scale dynamics contemporary governance challenges require. The biomimicry framework descending from Janine Benyus's *Biomimicry: Innovation Inspired by Nature* (1997) and operationalized through Kate Raworth's *Doughnut Economics: Seven Ways to Think Like a 21st-Century Economist* (2017) has produced a positive-design vocabulary for governance the historical waves did not have. The Indigenous-knowledge integration descending from Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi), Vine Deloria Jr. (Standing Rock Sioux), Kyle Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi), Glen Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene), Leanne Betasamosake Simpson (Michi Saagiig Nishnaabeg), Daniel Wildcat (Yuchi), and the broader Indigenous-scholarship community has produced institutional vocabulary the historical waves did not engage at all.

The integration challenge is the gap between the intellectual development across these fields and the integration of the development into the political-coalition assembly the strategic project requires. The historical waves were preceded by intellectual movements that were integrated with the political coalitions that produced the legislation — the institutionalist tradition with the New Deal, the legal-realist tradition with the post-1937 settlement, the civil-rights legal infrastructure with the Warren Court reorientation, the environmental academic infrastructure with the environmental wave. The contemporary intellectual readiness is more substantial than any of these prior intellectual movements but is less integrated with the political coalitions that would translate it into legislative outcomes. The integration challenge is one of the principal strategic questions the project's framework engages, and it is the bridge between the intellectual readiness of the contemporary period and the political-coalition assembly transformative legislation would require.

### 4.5 The contemporary moment in the panarchy cycle

The four-dimensional assessment produces a synthesis the panarchy framework specifies. The American constitutional system is not in a r-phase or early-K-phase state; it has accumulated the institutional rigidities, the precedent-density, and the political-investment thickness that characterize late-K-phase or early-Ω-phase systems. The signs the framework specifies are visible. Constitutional doctrine is increasingly contested, with central precedents being overturned (*Dobbs*, the *Chevron* framework, *Roe*, the Voting Rights Act preclearance) and with constitutional authority increasingly disputed. The legitimacy of the institutional architecture is increasingly contested across the political spectrum, with the Court's approval ratings at historic lows, with congressional approval ratings consistently below twenty percent across the past two decades, and with the executive branch's institutional norms increasingly violated and contested. The administrative state is increasingly contested both doctrinally and politically, with the unitary-executive theory the project rejects gaining ground in constitutional doctrine and with the Schedule F executive-order architecture proposing the structural transformation of the civil service. The federalism architecture is increasingly contested, with state-level innovation in some jurisdictions running far ahead of federal capacity and with state-level retrenchment in others producing constitutional confrontations the framework had not anticipated. The empirical conditions the constitutional architecture must manage — climate, biodiversity, demographic transformation, technological transformation, the fiscal pressures of an aging population — are accumulating at rates the inherited institutional architecture is structurally unable to absorb.

The panarchy framework specifies that systems in late-K or early-Ω phases are not in equilibrium and not in stable adaptation; they are accumulating the conditions for release and reorganization that the framework predicts will eventually occur. The framework also specifies that the timing and form of the release-reorganization phase are not predictable in detail. The release can be triggered by any of a wide variety of disturbances the rigidified system cannot absorb — economic crisis, ecological catastrophe, geopolitical confrontation, electoral discontinuity, judicial-legitimacy collapse, mass-casualty event, or some combination of these — and the reorganization that follows can take a wide variety of forms depending on which institutional and intellectual resources are available at the time of release. The strategic implication is that the work of the present cannot be the prediction of the release event or the prefiguration of the reorganization form, but the construction of the institutional and intellectual resources that will be available to the reorganization phase when it eventually arrives.

The framework's specification of the "remember" cross-scale interaction is particularly relevant. In Holling's panarchy, "remember" describes the way larger and slower scales provide the memory and context that allow smaller, faster scales to reorganize after collapse. In a forest fire, the soil seed bank persists through the fire and re-seeds the forest in the reorganization phase that follows; the fire does not destroy the forest's reorganizing capacity if the slower-cycle resources have been preserved. Applied to constitutional reorganization, the "remember" function is performed by the institutional resources, the intellectual frameworks, the demonstration cases, and the personnel networks the present period accumulates and that will be available as resources to the reorganization phase whenever the release phase eventually concludes. The strategic significance is that the work of the present is the construction of the "remember" resources, not the construction of the reorganized constitutional architecture itself.

## PART FIVE — THE STRATEGIC PRESCRIPTION: CULTIVATION WORK ACROSS THE PANARCHY CYCLE

The diagnostic produces the strategic prescription as cultivation work across the panarchy cycle, oriented toward the eventual reorganization phase the framework specifies follows the release phase the inherited constitutional system has now entered. The prescription is multi-scale, multi-decade infrastructure-building under hostile federal conditions. The prescription is articulated through the components the historical pattern identified — movement infrastructure-building, intellectual reorientation, litigation strategy, institutional preparation, political-coalition assembly — and is operationalized through the material-substrate cultivation Part VI specifies and the eco-mimetic policy-making method `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v7` articulates. The strategic posture distinguishes itself from the alternative postures contemporary political conversation has produced.

### 5.1 What the prescription is not

The prescription differs from the strategic posture of immediate-legislative-action that the historical analogies might suggest. The diagnostic specifies that the political conditions for transformative legislation comparable to the historical waves are not present, and that legislative action under contemporary conditions can produce the partial achievements of the Affordable Care Act and the Inflation Reduction Act but cannot produce the transformative architecture the project's diagnostic phase specified as necessary. The prescription does not relinquish legislative work — substantial legislative work at federal and state scales is part of the multi-decade infrastructure-building — but it does recognize that legislative achievement under contemporary conditions is partial and infrastructure-building rather than transformative.

The prescription differs from the strategic posture of constitutional convention or formal Article V amendment that some contemporary commentary has urged. The Article V amendment process, requiring two-thirds of both houses of Congress and three-fourths of the states for ratification, is structurally infeasible under contemporary conditions for amendments addressing the project's framework's concerns. The constitutional-convention pathway, requiring two-thirds of state legislatures to call and three-fourths of states to ratify any proposals, has not been used since 1787 and is procedurally untested in ways that produce uncertainty about its operational feasibility, and the political-coalition that would assemble such a convention under contemporary conditions would not produce the amendments the project's framework would support; a contemporary constitutional convention would be more likely to produce a constitutional architecture more hostile to the project's framework than the current architecture is.

The prescription differs from the strategic posture of "exit" that some libertarian and some left-accelerationist commentary has urged. The proposal to abandon the federal constitutional framework in favor of state-level secession, regional confederation, or sub-federal community-construction underestimates the integration of the American economy, the cross-state externalities of climate and other ecological systems, the constitutional protections that exit-strategies would forfeit, and the historical record of secessionist proposals in American constitutional history. The prescription operates within the assumption that the federal constitutional framework will continue to be the operating environment within which the multi-decade work is conducted, even as that framework is increasingly contested and transformed by the cycle the panarchy framework specifies.

The prescription differs from the strategic posture of "after-the-collapse" prefiguration that some apocalyptic commentary has urged. The proposal to focus exclusively on the construction of post-constitutional institutional architecture, on the assumption that the inherited federal framework will collapse imminently and the work of the present is to prefigure what comes after, underestimates the political-economic resources of the inherited framework, the durability that complex institutional systems exhibit even in late-K phases, and the strategic costs of disengaging from the present institutional architecture before its collapse has actually occurred.

### 5.2 The material-substrate cultivation as substantive content of transformative legislation

The strategic prescription operates through the recognition that transformative legislation under the sixth-pass framing is not policy-area-by-policy-area incrementalism but cultivation across the material-substrate terrain Part VI specifies. The material-substrate documents — `VI.01_Land_Relations_and_Property_v7` (the land-relations-and-property analysis), `VI.02_Food_Soil_Agriculture_v7` (food, soil, and agriculture as cultivation), `VI.03_Energy_as_Cultivation_v7` (energy systems as cultivation), `VI.04_Cradle_to_Cradle_v7` (cradle-to-cradle and circular material flows), `VI.05_Regenerative_Built_Environment_v7` (the regenerative built environment), `VI.06_Health_as_Cultivation_v7` (health as cultivation), and `VI.07_Care_Kinship_Social_Reproduction_v7` (care, kinship, and social reproduction) — together specify the substantive content of what transformative legislation under the sixth-pass framing would cultivate. The legislation operationalizes the cultivation across the material-substrate terrain through the eco-mimetic policy-making method `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v7` articulates: the legislative cycle mimics the adaptive cycle, the policy architecture is grown rather than built, the implementation is disturbance-adapted across nested spatial and temporal scales.

The integration is not metaphorical. The substantive transformative legislation the project's framework would prescribe is the legislative architecture that cultivates the land-relations-and-property reconstruction `VI.01` specifies (federal-and-state legislation operationalizing community land trusts, Indigenous co-governance arrangements, public-trust frameworks, and the broader land-relations transformation the Part VI analysis carries); that cultivates the food-soil-and-agriculture reconstruction `VI.02` specifies (federal-and-state legislation operationalizing agroecology, soil-restoration, the agricultural-policy reorientation Wendell Berry's and the broader agrarian tradition has articulated, and the broader food-system transformation); that cultivates the energy-system reconstruction `VI.03` specifies (federal-and-state legislation operationalizing distributed renewable deployment, citizen-energy cooperatives, energy-democracy frameworks drawing on the German *Energiewende* learning, and the broader energy-system transformation); that cultivates the cradle-to-cradle and circular-material-flows reconstruction `VI.04` specifies; that cultivates the regenerative built-environment reconstruction `VI.05` specifies; that cultivates the health-as-cultivation reconstruction `VI.06` specifies; and that cultivates the care, kinship, and social-reproduction reconstruction `VI.07` specifies. The legislative architecture is grown across the material-substrate terrain rather than imposed as policy-area-by-policy-area regulatory specification.

The strategic prescription's specification of the material-substrate cultivation as substantive content of transformative legislation distinguishes the sixth-pass framing from prior legislative-strategy frameworks that have treated legislative architecture as separable from the material-substrate terrain it operates on. The historical legislative waves operated on inherited theories of the relationship between legislative architecture and material substrate that have proven inadequate to the contemporary conditions; the sixth-pass framing operates on the theory that legislative architecture is intelligible only as cultivation across the material-substrate terrain, and that the strategic-prescription work the project's framework specifies must integrate the legislative-cycle dynamics Part X carries with the material-substrate cultivation Part VI carries with the eco-mimetic policy-making method Part XII carries.

### 5.3 The components of the multi-decade infrastructure-building

The components of the multi-decade infrastructure-building can be enumerated through the historical pattern Part Two identified. The historical waves were the visible legislative phases of longer panarchy cycles whose components were specifiable: movement infrastructure-building, intellectual reorientation, litigation strategy, institutional preparation, and the political-coalition assembly that translates these components into legislative outcomes. The contemporary period must engage all of these components, with the recognition that some are substantially developed and others are substantially under-developed, and that the work of the present is to develop the components whose under-development constrains the eventual legislative possibilities.

Movement infrastructure-building under contemporary conditions must address the limitations the four-dimensional assessment identified — the decline in cross-class mass-membership civic associations, the geographic concentration of progressive infrastructure in coastal urban areas, and the systematic shift from organizing to mobilizing. The labor-organizing revival, the community-organizing revival represented by the People's Action network, the Center for Popular Democracy, the Working Families Party, the Movement for Black Lives, and various state-level organizing infrastructures represent contemporary substantive developments that the multi-decade project should engage. The strategic discipline is to support organizing-rather-than-mobilizing methodologies that produce durable power-building, to channel resources toward cross-class and geographically dispersed organizing rather than toward professionalized advocacy, and to integrate the issue-specific organizing across climate, democracy, racial-justice, and economic-justice domains that contemporary movement infrastructure has often kept separate.

Intellectual reorientation under contemporary conditions involves the integration of the intellectual development across the fields the project's framework engages — ecological science, decision theory, polycentric governance, biomimicry, doughnut economics, Indigenous knowledge, cognitive bias and political psychology — with the political-coalition assembly that translates intellectual development into political force. The historical pattern of the legal-realist tradition's integration with the post-1937 New Deal settlement, of the civil-rights legal infrastructure's integration with the Warren Court reorientation, of the environmental academic infrastructure's integration with the environmental wave, all specify that intellectual development without political-coalition integration does not produce legislative transformation.

Litigation strategy under contemporary conditions must operate within the constitutional architecture the conservative legal countermovement has constructed. The contemporary litigation strategy cannot replicate the offensive-litigation posture of the civil-rights and environmental waves, which operated within constitutional architectures more hospitable to expansive interpretation of federal regulatory and rights-protective authority. The contemporary litigation strategy must include defensive litigation to preserve the institutional achievements of prior waves against the ongoing dismantling — the Voting Rights Act litigation, the Clean Air Act and Clean Water Act litigation, the antidiscrimination litigation — alongside offensive litigation in the substantive domains where the contemporary constitutional architecture permits advancement (state-level constitutional litigation, tribal-court Rights of Nature litigation, international human-rights litigation that operates outside the American constitutional architecture).

Institutional preparation under contemporary conditions involves the construction of the regulatory, technical, and administrative capacity any eventual transformative wave will require for sustained implementation. The historical pattern specifies that legislation operating through positive institutional construction tends to produce more durable transformation than legislation operating through prohibitions and rights enforcement, and that the institutional capacity that supports positive institutional construction must be built in advance of the legislation rather than constructed within the legislative process. The contemporary period has institutional preparation in some domains — the climate-policy technical capacity built across state agencies, the policy-research capacity of contemporary advocacy organizations, the administrative capacity the ACA and IRA have begun to construct — and gaps in others. The strategic discipline is to invest in institutional capacity-building that will be available as resources to eventual transformative legislation, including state-level regulatory-agency capacity, the technical-academic infrastructure that supports rigorous policy development, and the personnel pipelines through which the next generation of public-administration practitioners will be trained.

Political-coalition assembly under contemporary conditions is the most challenging of the components, because contemporary political-economic conditions — the partisan polarization, the geographic-political asymmetries, the cultural-cognition entrenchment — produce structural constraints on coalition assembly the historical waves did not face in the same form. The contemporary substantive resources for political-coalition assembly across cultural-cognition divides include the race-class narrative work of Anat Shenker-Osorio, the deep-canvassing methodology of Joshua Kalla and David Broockman, the More in Common research on hidden tribes and shared values, the public-narrative methodology of Marshall Ganz, the deep-organizing methodology of Jane McAlevey, the organizing-versus-mobilizing distinction of Han, and the broader cognitive-aware mobilization methodology contemporary practitioners have developed. The strategic discipline is to invest in these methodologies as substantive infrastructure rather than as messaging consultancy, and to integrate the methodologies with the organizing-rather-than-mobilizing approach that produces durable power-building.

### 5.4 The time horizon and the question of patience

The strategic prescription's specification of multi-decade time horizon deserves engagement on its own terms because it confronts a tension the contemporary political moment has made acute. The empirical severity of the contemporary crises — climate change advancing on a trajectory that will exceed 1.5°C within the decade, biodiversity collapse accelerating, democratic erosion producing constitutional discontinuities the framers did not anticipate — produces urgency that the multi-decade time horizon may seem to inadequately respect. The tension between the urgency of the empirical conditions and the patience of the strategic prescription is not a tension the framework can resolve through clever formulation; it is a structural feature of the strategic situation the project's framework must engage honestly.

The honest engagement specifies several considerations. The first is that the multi-decade time horizon does not imply the deferral of substantive action; it implies the recognition that substantive action under contemporary conditions can only be infrastructure-building rather than transformative, and that the substantive action of infrastructure-building must begin immediately rather than be deferred to the moment of transformation. The strategic prescription is not "wait until conditions improve"; it is "build the conditions through the work of the present, recognizing that the transformation the work prepares is on a multi-decade horizon."

The second consideration is that the multi-decade horizon does not imply that the empirical severity of the contemporary crises will be tractable to whatever transformation eventually occurs. The climate trajectory the contemporary period has set in motion will produce impacts regardless of when transformative legislation eventually occurs, and the impacts that occur during the multi-decade infrastructure-building period will be substantial and irreversible. The strategic prescription does not promise that the multi-decade work will avert the impacts the empirical analysis predicts; it promises only that the multi-decade work will construct the institutional and intellectual resources the eventual reorganization phase will draw on, and that those resources are necessary even though they are not sufficient to prevent the impacts the contemporary trajectory has set in motion.

The third consideration is that the multi-decade horizon has historical precedent. The Progressive Era's institutional preparation extended back at least to the 1880s; the New Deal harvested four decades of accumulated infrastructure; the civil rights wave harvested half a century of NAACP litigation and Black community organizing; the environmental wave harvested seven decades of conservation-movement infrastructure descending from the Progressive Era. The historical pattern specifies that transformative legislation operates on multi-decade time horizons in its preparation, and the contemporary period's strategic situation is not anomalous in requiring multi-decade preparation but is simply at the early-to-middle stage of the cycle historical analysis specifies. The patience the strategic prescription requires is not a counsel of resignation but a recognition of the time scales on which transformative change actually operates.

The fourth consideration is that the multi-decade work has the property that its components remain valuable even if the eventual transformation occurs in a form different from the form present strategic analysis would prefer. The institutional capacity-building, the intellectual development, the movement infrastructure-building, the personnel pipelines, the demonstration cases — all of these remain valuable across a wide range of possible eventual transformation pathways, including pathways the contemporary strategic analysis cannot fully anticipate. The strategic posture is one of constructing components whose value is robust across multiple eventual scenarios rather than constructing components whose value depends on a specific scenario whose probability cannot be reliably assessed. The decision-quality literature's emphasis on robust decision-making, exemplified by Robert Lempert, Steven Popper, and Steven Bankes's *Shaping the Next One Hundred Years: New Methods for Quantitative, Long-Term Policy Analysis* (2003), applies to the strategic-prescription level: the multi-decade infrastructure-building should be designed for robustness across multiple eventual transformation pathways rather than optimized for a specific pathway whose probability the contemporary analysis cannot reliably specify.

## PART SIX — THE NON-WESTERN POLITICAL-ECONOMY TRADITIONS AT PRIMARY-ARTICULATOR STANDING

The political-economy analysis of the preceding parts draws substantially on the heterodox-Western and global-decolonial canons (Polanyi, Sen, the ecological-economics and Pink-Tide traditions), and the document here engages the non-Western political-economy traditions at primary-articulator standing rather than as comparative supplement, because the cultivation framing's account of an economy embedded in moral, ecological, and intergenerational obligation is articulated more directly and far earlier in these traditions than in the Western recovery. The engagement is the primary articulation of the embedded-economy commitment the heterodox-Western canon recovers partially.

The Buddhist economic tradition supplies the sharpest articulation of an economy oriented to sufficiency rather than maximization. E. F. Schumacher's "Buddhist Economics," the central essay of *Small Is Beautiful: Economics as if People Mattered* (1973), develops from the Buddhist concept of right livelihood (*sammā ājīva*, the fifth element of the Eightfold Path) an economics whose aim is the maximization of well-being with the minimum of consumption rather than the maximization of consumption — a direct inversion of the growth-maximizing premise the political-economic regime `II.02_Five_Regime_Disturbance_Diagnostic_v7` diagnoses. The Thai Buddhist scholar-monk P. A. Payutto's *Buddhist Economics: A Middle Way for the Market Place* (1994) develops the analysis further, distinguishing *taṇhā* (craving, the desire for sense-pleasure that has no natural limit) from *chanda* (the desire for genuine well-being, which is bounded by what well-being requires), and grounding an economics of moderation in the analysis of desire the Buddhist tradition carries at primary-articulator standing. The contemporary instantiation in Bhutan's Gross National Happiness framework — the constitutional commitment to measure and pursue collective well-being rather than aggregate output — is the political-economic form of the Buddhist sufficiency orientation, and it bears directly on the cultivation framing's account of the economy as the material substrate of a cultivated rather than an accumulative life.

The Islamic moral-economy tradition carries at primary-articulator standing an account of the economy as embedded in binding moral obligation that anticipates by centuries the Polanyian critique of the disembedded market. The prohibition of *ribā* (interest and usury) refuses the autonomous self-augmentation of capital that the financialization configuration the document diagnoses institutionalizes; the obligation of *zakāt* (the binding redistributive levy) embeds redistribution in religious duty rather than leaving it to discretionary charity or contested taxation; and the *waqf* (the perpetual charitable trust, dedicating resources in inalienable endowment for the common welfare across generations) is the institutional form of the intergenerational dedication of resources the cultivation framing requires — the same *waqf* principle the war-powers document `XII.13_War_Powers_and_the_Security_State_v7` carries against the capture of the polity's resources by the war economy. The Islamic moral economy specifies an economy bounded by the prohibition of exploitation, the obligation of redistribution, and the dedication of wealth to the common welfare in perpetual trust — the embedded economy at primary articulation.

The Gandhian tradition supplies the account of an economy organized around local self-reliance and the holding of wealth in trust. Mohandas Gandhi's doctrine of trusteeship — that those who hold wealth hold it not as owners but as trustees for the welfare of society — and the linked commitments to *sarvodaya* (the welfare of all, against the utilitarian welfare of the greatest number) and *swadeshi* (local self-reliance and the preference for local production against the extractive supply chain) together articulate a political economy of decentralized sufficiency that bears on the material-substrate cultivation `VI.01_Land_Relations_and_Property_v7` through `VI.07_Care_Kinship_Social_Reproduction_v7` specify. The African socialist tradition develops the communal economy at primary-articulator standing through Julius Nyerere's *Ujamaa: Essays on Socialism* (1968), which grounds an economics of familyhood (*ujamaa*) in the *ubuntu* relational ontology — the economy as the material expression of the principle that a person is a person through other persons — against both the acquisitive individualism of the capitalist configuration and the class antagonism of the orthodox-Marxist configuration. The Confucian political-economy tradition carries the people-as-root (*mínběn*) doctrine and the well-field (*jǐngtián*) agrarian ideal, with Mencius (Mengzi, fourth century BCE) specifying the people's secure material livelihood as the precondition of moral order and the ruler's legitimacy as conditional on securing it — the obligation of the political order to the material base of the people's flourishing, articulated two millennia before the social-democratic recovery.

These traditions are not variations on a single non-Western political economy; they carry genuine differences in their accounts of property, redistribution, and the role of the state, and the cultivation discipline holds the differences rather than collapsing them into a single political economy. What they share, and what they articulate at primary-articulator standing against the disembedded-market configuration the document diagnoses, is the account of the economy as embedded in moral, relational, ecological, and intergenerational obligation — the account the heterodox-Western and ecological-economics canons recover recently and partially.

## CLOSING — CULTIVATION FRAMING AND FORWARD IMPLICATIONS

The diagnosis identifies what is wrong with the inherited constitutional and legislative architecture under contemporary conditions. The strategic prescription identifies what the work of the present consists of given that what is wrong cannot yet be transformed. The diagnosis and the prescription are not in tension; they are sequential phases of a single strategic logic the panarchy framework makes intelligible.

Read under the cultivation framing, the strategic prescription is not policy-delivery engineering but cultivation work across the panarchy cycle. The legislative cycle is read as adaptive cycle in the strict sense; the contemporary moment is diagnosed by adaptive-cycle position; the strategic prescription is the cultivation work required to move the polity into a configuration more adequate to the Part IX climate stress case and the Part II disturbance-regimes landscape. The cultivation is grown across the material-substrate terrain Part VI specifies, operationalized through the eco-mimetic policy-making method `XII.01_Eco_Mimetic_Policy_Making_v7` articulates, and integrated with the structural commitments Parts XI through XIII specify.

The forward implications coordinate across the project's continuing work. The three operational layers that operationalize the strategic prescription — state-level legislative and institutional innovation, the international and Indigenous-governance dimension, and cognitive-aware political mobilization — are the subject of `XI.01_Three_Operational_Layers_v7`. The climate-paradigmatic stress test under which transformative legislation is most empirically tested is the subject of `IX.01_Constitutional_Climate_Redesign_v7`. The structural commitments transformative legislation operationalizes — intergenerational rectification, currency architecture, the decision-rights cluster — are the subject of `XII.04_Intergenerational_Rectification_v7`, `XII.02_Currency_Architecture_v7`, and `XII.09_Bounded_Decision_Rights_Across_Nested_Scales_v7`. The cultivation-protocol synthesis integrating decision rights across the architecture is the subject of `XIII.01_Decision_Rights_Synthesis_v7`. The capstone reading of the full v6 corpus, including this document's specification of the panarchy-cycle framing as substantive analytical category and the multi-traditional comparative-political-economy archive at primary-engagement standing, is the subject of `XIV.01_Capstone_Synthesis_v7`. The cultivation work proceeds.

The honest acknowledgment this document offers in closing is that the strategic prescription it has produced is sobering rather than inspiring. The empirical severity of the contemporary crises is substantial; the strategic prescription's recognition that transformative legislation comparable to the historical waves is not feasible under current conditions does not match the urgency the empirical severity would demand if urgency alone could produce political force. The project's framework's analytical honesty requires acknowledging this gap rather than papering over it with rhetorical optimism. The honest assessment is that significant damage will occur during the multi-decade period the strategic prescription specifies, that the transformations the eventual reorganization phase produces will not necessarily be the transformations the project's framework would prescribe in the abstract, and that the work of the present is the construction of resources whose deployment will occur in conditions the contemporary analysis cannot fully predict. The strategic posture the project's framework adopts is not optimistic; it is committed. The commitment is to the cultivation work of infrastructure-building under the recognition that the work matters across a wide range of possible futures, that the alternative postures all underperform on grounds the analysis has specified, and that patient multi-decade cultivation has the historical record's support as the strategy through which transformative change has actually been produced. The work of the present is the work the present permits, and the work the present permits is what the strategic prescription specifies. The cultivation work proceeds.
