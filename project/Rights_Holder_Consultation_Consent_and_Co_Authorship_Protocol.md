# Rights-Holder Consultation, Consent, and Co-Authorship Protocol

*A governing process document for the CProj constitutional corpus. It is the concrete operationalization of the response to the appropriation indictment the eighth-pass audit names as the project's deepest vulnerability (`outputs/v7_audits/Eighth_Pass_Audit_v7.md`, V-1) and that the capstone holds open as a live discipline (`project/source_documents/v7_completed/XIV.01_Capstone_Synthesis_v7.md`, Thread E). It is not a constitutional-theory document and does not take a position-based corpus ordinal; it is a process commitment that governs whether and how the corpus may move from a settler-authored draft to a multi-traditional body of work.*

---

## 1. The appropriation question, acknowledged without defense

The corpus draws on Indigenous, Eastern, African, Islamic, Latin American and Andean, Caribbean, Pacific Islander, Jewish, dalit, Black-radical, disability-justice, and neurodivergent traditions, and it carries them at primary-articulator standing. As it stands, it does so **without the prior knowledge, consent, authorship, or authority of the communities and rights-holders whose traditions it engages.** That is the plain fact, and it is stated here first and without softening, because the project's earlier framing — that it engages traditions "in their own registers" and "refuses synthesis-into-architecture" — describes a drafting discipline, not a relationship, and a discipline practiced *about* a tradition is not the same as a relationship *with* the people who carry it.

The strongest form of the indictment is correct on its own terms: a settler-positioned project that reads, excerpts, recombines, and re-presents living traditions has performed an act of extraction, however carefully, so long as the communities that hold those traditions had no part in deciding whether, how, or to what end their knowledge would be used. Primary-articulator standing, as the corpus has practiced it, still means the project chose which articulators to carry and to what end; the self-audit still means the project graded its own extraction; the cross-citation discipline still operates on texts the project selected. None of these dissolves the problem. Eve Tuck (Unangax̂) and K. Wayne Yang's "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" (2012) names the deeper trap precisely: a consultation-and-acknowledgment process can itself become a "settler move to innocence" — a procedure that produces the *feeling* of having addressed the harm while leaving the underlying relations (and, for Indigenous peoples, the land) untouched. This protocol is written in the knowledge that it could become exactly that, and is structured to resist it.

Two consequences follow, and the project commits to both. First, **until the process below is genuinely undertaken, the corpus is provisional and uncredentialed as a multi-traditional work.** It may honestly circulate as what it is — a settler-authored draft that engages these traditions and seeks their correction — but it may not represent itself as a multi-traditional constitutional synthesis, because that standing is not the author's to claim; it can only be conferred by the rights-holders through the process, or withheld. Second, **the process is necessary but not sufficient.** Consultation does not discharge the material decolonial demand. For the Indigenous traditions in particular, the corpus's own carried critique (Coulthard, the Land Back movement) holds that legitimacy is bound to land and sovereignty, not to process; this protocol therefore couples consultation to material commitments (Section 7) and explicitly does not present process as a substitute for them.

A reflexive note belongs here: the present document is itself text generated within the same project that produced the corpus, and a protocol for consulting rights-holders that is itself drafted without consulting them carries the same problem one register up. It is offered as a starting proposal that the rights-holders are themselves entitled to rewrite — Section 4's authority ladder applies to this protocol as much as to the corpus.

## 2. The principles that govern the process

The process is governed by established frameworks the relevant communities have themselves developed, not by criteria the project invents. The project adopts them as binding constraints rather than aspirations.

**Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC).** As articulated in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007), especially Articles 18, 19, and 31 (the right to participate in decisions affecting them, the requirement of consent obtained through their own representative institutions, and the right to maintain, control, protect, and develop their cultural heritage and traditional knowledge). Consent must be *free* (uncoerced, including free of the soft coercion of a finished product presented for endorsement), *prior* (before use, not after), and *informed* (with full disclosure of how the knowledge will be used and circulated). The corpus has already failed the "prior" condition for its existing draft; the protocol's first task is therefore remedial as well as prospective.

**The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (Global Indigenous Data Alliance; Carroll, Garba, et al., *Data Science Journal*, 2020): Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics — the complement to the FAIR data principles, asserting that openness is not the highest value where Indigenous knowledge is concerned, and that authority over its use rests with the peoples it comes from. **OCAP®** (Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession; the First Nations Information Governance Centre) supplies the parallel First Nations framework. **Traditional Knowledge (TK) and Biocultural (BC) Labels** (Local Contexts; Jane Anderson and Kim Christen) and the **Mukurtu** content-management model (Christen, with the Warumungu community) supply the concrete tooling by which provenance, permitted use, and community protocols can be attached to specific passages.

**Relational accountability** (Shawn Wilson, Opaskwayak Cree, *Research Is Ceremony*, 2008): the researcher is accountable to the relationships the work is embedded in, not only to the accuracy of the text. **Decolonizing methodologies** (Linda Tuhiwai Smith, Ngāti Awa and Ngāti Porou, 1999/2012) and the commitment to "standing with" rather than studying (Kim TallBear, Sisseton-Wahpeton Oyate, 2014) govern the posture. And **the right of refusal** (Audra Simpson, Mohawk, *Mohawk Interruptus*, 2014) is honored as a complete and final answer: a community's decision that its tradition not be engaged at all is not an obstacle to be negotiated but a determination to be obeyed, with the corresponding sections withdrawn.

The operative distinction the whole protocol turns on is between **consultation** (informing and seeking input), **consent** (the right to authorize or refuse), and **co-authorship/authority** (the right to write, edit, withdraw, and govern). The project commits to the third, not merely the first, wherever rights-holders wish to exercise it.

## 3. Who the rights-holders are, by tradition

"Rights-holders" is not uniform across the corpus's traditions, and the protocol refuses a single template. The hard work of the first phase is to identify, for each tradition, who holds the authority to speak for it, and to accept that the answer differs and is itself the community's to determine.

For the **Indigenous traditions** the corpus engages by name — Haudenosaunee (Kaianerekowa, the seventh-generation criterion), Diné, Citizen Potawatomi, Yellowknives Dene, Apalech, Standing Rock Sioux, Yuchi/Muscogee, Māori (the *Matike Mai Aotearoa* working group; the iwi and hapū), Aboriginal Australian (the Uluru Statement signatories; specific nations including Yorta Yorta, Wakka Wakka, Cobble Cobble, Goenpul), Andean (the *sumak kawsay* and Pachamama articulations carried into the Ecuadorian and Bolivian constitutions), Pacific Islander, and Sámi — rights-holders are the nations' own governance and knowledge-keeping institutions, engaged through their own representative bodies on their own terms. Where the corpus cites a living Indigenous scholar (Kimmerer, Coulthard, Simpson, Yunkaporta, Wildcat, Whyte, TallBear, and others), that scholar is owed direct consultation as to whether and how their work is represented, distinct from and additional to the consultation of the nations.

For the **Eastern textual and living traditions** (Daoist, Buddhist across its schools, Confucian, the classical Indian traditions) and the **Islamic** tradition, there is no single authority, and the protocol must say so plainly rather than appoint one. Consultation here means engaging living scholarly, monastic, lineage, and religious communities and their recognized scholars — plural, contesting, and diasporic — with explicit humility about who can and cannot speak for a tradition that has no central voice, and with attention to the difference between a tradition's institutional custodians and its diaspora practitioners. The aim is not a single endorsement (which would be a fiction) but genuine, plural review by people who carry the tradition, and correction of the corpus's representations by them.

For the **African** traditions (Ubuntu philosophy and its scholarly custodians; the Kurukan Fuga and the Mande tradition; the *ujamaa* tradition), the **dalit** tradition (the Ambedkarite movement and its organizations and scholars), the **Black-radical and Caribbean** canons, the **Latin American** liberation traditions, the **Jewish** critical traditions, and the **disability-justice and neurodivergent** movements, rights-holders are the living intellectual and political communities, movements, and where applicable the estates and designated representatives of the named thinkers. Several of these are living political movements with organizations that can be approached directly.

A concrete precedent the project takes as a model is the **San Code of Research Ethics** (2017), in which the San peoples of southern Africa set the terms on which researchers may engage them — a community-authored code that inverts the usual direction of ethics review. The first phase invites each community to produce, or to point the project to, its own such terms, and the project binds itself to whatever terms result.

## 4. The authority rights-holders hold (the ladder)

The project commits in advance to a graded ladder of authority, and to the rights-holders' choice of where on it to stand for their tradition. The ladder runs from least to most, and rights-holders may select the highest rung they wish; the project may not unilaterally select a lower one.

1. **Attribution and notification** — to be correctly named, with affiliation, and informed that and how their tradition is engaged.
2. **Review** — to read the passages engaging their tradition before any further circulation and to have errors of representation corrected.
3. **Edit** — to revise the corpus's engagement of their tradition directly, with their edits authoritative over the author's prior text.
4. **Co-authorship** — to be named authors of the sections engaging their tradition, with the standing that confers.
5. **Consent and conditions** — to authorize use only under stated conditions (permitted contexts, prohibited uses, required framings, TK/BC Labels attached to specific passages).
6. **Withdrawal and refusal** — to require that their tradition's engagement be removed entirely, at any time, including after publication, as a standing and irrevocable right; a refusal is honored in full and the sections are withdrawn rather than rewritten around.
7. **Ownership and ongoing governance** — to hold continuing authority over the portions concerning their tradition, including the right to revise them in future, consistent with OCAP® and the CARE principle of authority-to-control.

Rung 6 is load-bearing: the project commits that the corpus is more incomplete-and-honest with a tradition withdrawn at its holders' request than it is complete-and-extractive with that tradition retained over their objection. The architecture is already built to absorb this — the sunset-and-revision logic the corpus applies to its own decision rights applies to itself: any tradition's engagement is revisable and withdrawable.

## 5. The process, in phases

**Phase 0 — Set the house in order first (preconditions).** Before approaching any community, the project (a) states publicly and on the corpus itself that it is a settler-authored draft engaging these traditions without prior consent and seeking their correction (Section 8); (b) secures the resources that make genuine consultation possible, because consultation without the means to compensate participants and act on their direction is itself extractive; and (c) accepts that the timeline is the communities' to set, not the project's, and that "slow" is not a failure mode.

**Phase 1 — Relationship and protocol co-design.** For each tradition, the project approaches the appropriate rights-holders (Section 3) not with the finished corpus for endorsement but with the question of whether they wish to engage at all, and on what terms. Each community is invited to set or name its own research-ethics terms (the San Code model). This phase is relationship-building in Wilson's and TallBear's sense, conducted on the communities' terms and timeframes, and it may end, for any tradition, in refusal — which concludes the matter for that tradition (withdrawal under rung 6).

**Phase 2 — Review and correction.** Communities that choose to continue review the passages engaging their tradition and correct errors of representation (rungs 2–3). The project treats these corrections as authoritative.

**Phase 3 — Consent, co-authorship, conditions, or withdrawal.** Each tradition's engagement reaches one of the ladder's outcomes: co-authored and consented under stated conditions (rungs 4–5), retained under conditions, or withdrawn (rung 6). TK/BC Labels and Mukurtu-style provenance are attached to specific passages as the rights-holders direct. Only after this phase may the corpus represent a given tradition's engagement as consented.

**Phase 4 — Ongoing governance and the living relationship.** Consent is not a one-time clearance. A standing governance arrangement — a rights-holders' council or its equivalent, structured as the communities prefer — holds continuing authority over their portions (rung 7), with the right to revise or withdraw in future. The relationship is permanent, not transactional; this mirrors the corpus's own "grown, not built" and ongoing-cultivation commitments, now applied to the corpus's relationship with the traditions it engages.

## 6. What changes structurally in the corpus

The protocol is not cosmetic; executing it changes the artifact. Each document gains a **per-tradition consent-status field** recording, for every tradition it engages, where on the ladder its rights-holders have chosen to stand and what conditions attach. **TK and BC Labels** are attached to specific passages. The corpus's authorship line changes from single-author to reflect co-authorship wherever rung 4 is reached. A **withdrawal register** records any tradition removed at its holders' request, with the removal honored rather than worked around. And the corpus's self-description changes: from "a constitutional synthesis carrying the multi-traditional canon at primary-articulator standing" to, accurately, "a settler-authored constitutional draft, portions of which are consented, co-authored, conditioned, or withdrawn as the rights-holders have determined." The capstone's "capacity for revision from sources the project does not author" ceases to be a stated disposition and becomes an operative governance fact.

## 7. The material commitments that keep this from being a move to innocence

Because consultation alone can launder extraction (Tuck and Yang), the protocol binds the process to material commitments. **Benefit-sharing**: any resources, recognition, or proceeds the corpus generates are shared with the communities whose knowledge it engages, on terms they set, consistent with the Nagoya Protocol's access-and-benefit-sharing logic extended from genetic to traditional knowledge. **Support for the material demand**: for the Indigenous traditions, the project explicitly affirms that process does not substitute for land and sovereignty, and directs benefit and platform toward the Land Back and sovereignty commitments the corpus already carries, rather than treating its own consultation as the end of its obligation. **Ceding rather than retaining authority**: the governance arrangement (Phase 4) genuinely transfers authority over each tradition's portions to its rights-holders, including authority the author would prefer to keep. The test the project holds itself to is Tuck and Yang's: does the process change the relations of authority and material benefit, or only the author's conscience? If only the latter, it has failed.

## 8. The honest constraints and failure modes

This plan is demanding, slow, and may not succeed, and saying so is part of doing it honestly. It is resource-intensive and cannot be done well without funding for compensation, translation, travel, and time; an under-resourced version would be extractive consultation theater and should not be attempted. It may result in substantial portions of the corpus being conditioned or withdrawn — which the project accepts as a better outcome than retention-over-objection. Some communities will decline to participate, and their refusal is final, not a gap to be filled from secondary sources. There is a structural asymmetry between a single author and whole communities that no protocol erases; the author cannot consent on a community's behalf or speak for the absent. There is the constant risk that the consultation becomes legitimacy-laundering — a credential the project collects rather than a relationship it enters — and the only guard against it is the material commitments of Section 7 and the rights-holders' real (rung 6) power to make the project worse-off by withdrawing. And there is the reflexive limit noted in Section 1: this very document should be among the first things the rights-holders are invited to rewrite.

## 9. The standing commitment

Until this process is genuinely undertaken, the corpus is held as a settler-authored draft offered for the correction of the traditions it engages, not as a multi-traditional work, and it is to be described that way wherever it circulates. The appropriation question is not answered by this document; it is *opened honestly and given a path*. Whether it is ever answered is not the author's to decide. It rests with the rights-holders, including their right to decide that the answer is no.
