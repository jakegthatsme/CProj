## 2. Load-Bearing Triad

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad: **biocultural governance** as constitutional ontology (the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a biocultural formation, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled, grounded in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights literatures); **eco-mimetic policy-making** as operational method (ecology, complex-adaptive-systems theory, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology concepts inspire how laws are passed, written, and tested); **disturbance-adapted implementation** as temporal orientation (intergenerational architecture cultivated to absorb a plural disturbance landscape comprising five named regimes — cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic — and their interactions). The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the principle's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery. "Grown, not built" is usable as colloquial summary phrase.

For `II.09`, the triad bears on the polity-formation diagnostic the document carries. Disturbance-adapted implementation is foregrounded: climate displacement is the compound disturbance the document treats, and the analytical task is to specify how a biocultural formation absorbs, metabolizes, and recovers from large-scale population movement across intergenerational horizons. Biocultural governance bears because belonging — who is constituted as a member of the polity, on what terms, at which nested scales — is itself a biocultural-formation question, not a juridical-status question the polity can settle by design-imposition. Eco-mimetic policy-making bears on the closing specification: what the constitutional architecture must enable for belonging to be cultivated rather than engineered. The document foregrounds the physical-environmental and political-economic disturbance regimes at their intersection, with the cultural and biological regimes coupled in and the cognitive-and-informational regime bearing through climate-displacement disinformation. Its primary-articulator traditions — Indigenous sovereignty and refusal, the Chicana borderlands tradition, the Pacific Islander climate-displacement tradition — stand at primary-articulator standing, not as comparative supplement to the Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon.

## 4. Substantive Task

`II.09` specifies climate displacement as a cross-regime disturbance *category* — not a single phenomenon — and treats the polity-formation question of belonging, sovereignty, and inclusion under conditions of large-scale population movement. The analytical work has four phases.

First, the document specifies climate displacement as a category of phenomena operating at the intersection of the physical-environmental regime (`II.05`) and the political-economic regime (`II.07`), bearing centrally on the cultural regime (`II.02`) and the biological regime (`II.04`), and bearing on the cognitive-and-informational regime (`II.06`) through climate-displacement disinformation cascades. The category comprises at least six distinct phenomena, each with its own disturbance dynamics, temporal signature, and population geography: drought-induced agricultural-system collapse and the displacement it drives; sea-level rise and coastal displacement; fire-regime shift and wildland-urban-interface displacement; water-scarcity displacement; livability-collapse heat-zone displacement; and political-violence climate-amplification displacement, in which climate disturbance amplifies existing conflict drivers rather than acting as sole cause. The document treats these as a category precisely because the constitutional architecture must absorb the category, not any one phenomenon.

Second, the document conducts the borders-and-sovereignty diagnostic: it specifies the contemporary border-and-sovereignty architecture as a design-imposition fixture — borders treated as engineered, fixed, and prior to the polity rather than as cultivated and revisable conditions of a biocultural formation — and specifies what that architecture does under conditions of climate displacement. The diagnostic engages the waning-sovereignty literature, the statelessness and right-to-have-rights tradition, and the necropolitics-and-expulsions literature on the political economy of border infrastructure, including the cognitive mechanism by which "invasion" and "replacement" disinformation manufactures political consent for border violence.

Third, the document engages three primary-articulator traditions in their own analytical registers: the Indigenous-sovereignty tradition on the border as colonial instrument and climate displacement as colonial continuation; the Chicana borderlands tradition on belonging as lived and plural rather than state-conferred; and the Pacific Islander climate-displacement tradition on belonging-to-place against the framing of the displaced as passive victims, including the legal precedent the tradition has produced.

Fourth, the document specifies the positive polity-formation question — how belonging is cultivated rather than designed — and provides the closing cultivation specification: what the constitutional architecture must enable for a biocultural formation to absorb large-scale population movement without the design-imposition border fixture, and the bridge from this diagnostic to the foundational-rights and public-check decision-rights architecture of Part XII and the cultivation-protocol synthesis of Part XIII.

`II.09` assumes the regime-by-regime diagnostic substance of `II.02`, `II.04`, `II.05`, `II.06`, and `II.07`, and the cross-regime interactions analysis of `II.08`. It assumes the constitutional-ontology and nested-scales reference frame of `III.01` (Part III, drafted in Phase v6.γ before `II.09`'s Phase v6.ε). It does not duplicate the regime-by-regime diagnostic work, and it does not duplicate `II.08`'s seven-pathway interactions analysis; it specifies one cross-regime disturbance category at the polity-formation scale and treats the belonging question that category forces. It does not specify the bounded decision rights, the eco-mimetic policy-making method, or the cultivation-protocol architecture — those are Part XII and Part XIII work the document points forward to.

### Thread A — Climate displacement as a cross-regime disturbance category

The thread specifies climate displacement as a category of six phenomena rather than a single phenomenon, and maps the category across the five disturbance regimes. Each phenomenon is engaged for its distinct disturbance dynamics: drought-induced agricultural-system collapse (slow-onset, compounding, with the Sahel and the Central American Dry Corridor as documented cases); sea-level rise and coastal displacement (slow-onset with acute-event punctuation, with low-lying deltas and atoll states as documented cases); fire-regime shift and wildland-urban-interface displacement (acute-event, recurring, with the western North American and Mediterranean fire records as documented cases); water-scarcity displacement (slow-onset, basin-scale); livability-collapse heat-zone displacement (slow-onset, threshold-crossing, the wet-bulb-temperature literature); and political-violence climate-amplification displacement (climate as threat multiplier rather than sole cause). The thread draws the documentary archive from the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre's *Global Report on Internal Displacement* and the UNHCR climate-displacement documentation, engaging both critically rather than as neutral data — the categories these institutions use are themselves the product of the borders-and-sovereignty architecture the document diagnoses. The thread specifies the cross-regime character of the category: physical-environmental disturbance (`II.05`) as proximate driver, political-economic disturbance (`II.07`) as the structure determining who is displaced and who absorbs the cost, cultural disturbance (`II.02`) as the regime through which displacement is racialized and caste-sorted, biological disturbance (`II.04`) coupled through food-system and disease dynamics, and the cognitive-and-informational regime (`II.06`) bearing through displacement-related disinformation. The thread cites `II.08` for the cross-regime interaction mechanics and does not re-derive them.

### Thread B — The borders-and-sovereignty diagnostic: the case against borders-as-engineered-fixture

The thread specifies the contemporary border-and-sovereignty architecture as a design-imposition fixture and builds the analytical case against the borders-as-engineered-fixture framing under the cultivation framing. It engages Wendy Brown's *Walled States, Waning Sovereignty* (2010) on the proliferation of border walls as a symptom of waning rather than waxing sovereignty — the wall as theatrical compensation for the nation-state's declining capacity to control the flows that constitute it. It engages Reece Jones's *Violent Borders* (2016) on the border as a site of state violence and on the right to move, and *White Borders* (2021) on the racial history of the United States border regime from Chinese Exclusion forward. It engages Catherine Dauvergne's *Making People Illegal* (2008) on how globalization produced illegality as a legal category and *The New Politics of Immigration and the End of Settler Societies* (2016) on the settler-society migration politics specific to the United States, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand. It engages Étienne Balibar's *Politics and the Other Scene* (2002) on the border as an institution that is not located only at the territorial edge but is dispersed through the polity. The thread specifies what the cultivation framing recovers: the border as a cultivated, revisable, scale-matched condition of a biocultural formation rather than an engineered fixture prior to and constitutive of the polity. The thread must not romanticize borderlessness as design-imposition in the opposite direction; the cultivation framing specifies conditions, not the abolition of all boundary.

### Thread C — Statelessness and the right-to-have-rights tradition

The thread engages Hannah Arendt's *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951), specifically the analysis in Chapter Nine, "The Decline of the Nation-State and the End of the Rights of Man," on statelessness as the condition that exposes the dependence of human rights on national membership — the stateless person, stripped of a political community, discovers that the "rights of man" were never independent of the nation-state that was supposed to guarantee them. The thread specifies climate displacement as the phenomenon that threatens to generalize the stateless condition Arendt diagnosed: the climate-displaced person frequently does not cross an international border, does not meet the 1951 Refugee Convention definition, and falls into a juridical gap that the displacement is now widening. The thread treats "the right to have rights" as the foundational articulation the document carries forward — belonging not as a status the polity confers but as the prior condition of having any rights at all — and connects it to the foundational-rights architecture of `XII.09`.

### Thread D — Necropolitics, expulsions, and the political economy of border infrastructure

The thread engages Achille Mbembe's *Necropolitics* (2019) and *On the Postcolony* (2001) on the border regime as a necropolitical architecture — an architecture that distributes exposure to death, that decides which lives are grievable and which are abandonable, and that operates through the production of zones of abandonment rather than only through direct killing. It engages Saskia Sassen's *Expulsions* (2014) on the systemic logic that expels populations from the spaces of the global economy, and *Territory, Authority, Rights* (2006) on the historical assemblage of the territory-authority-rights nexus the border regime depends on. It engages Harsha Walia's *Border and Rule* (2021) on the border regime as constitutive of global capitalism rather than incidental to it. The thread specifies the political economy of border infrastructure — the contractors, the detention industry, the surveillance-technology vendors — and cross-references `II.03` (oligarchy and dark money) for the capture architecture through which border infrastructure is financed and politically entrenched. Within this thread the document engages the cognitive mechanism the planning document names: climate-displacement-related disinformation cascades, the "invasion" and "replacement" narratives through which political consent for necropolitical border violence is manufactured. This engagement cross-references `II.06` (the cognitive-and-informational disturbance regime) for the disinformation mechanics and does not re-derive them; `II.09` specifies how the mechanism operates specifically on the climate-displaced.

### Thread E — Indigenous sovereignty, the border, and climate colonialism

The thread engages the Indigenous-sovereignty tradition at primary-articulator standing. It engages Audra Simpson (Mohawk), *Mohawk Interruptus: Political Life Across the Borders of Settler States* (2014), on sovereignty and refusal — the Haudenosaunee experience of a settler border drawn directly through the territory and political life of a continuing Indigenous nation, and the refusal of the settler state's terms of recognition as itself a sovereign act. It engages Kyle Powys Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi) on climate colonialism and Indigenous displacement — the specification of climate displacement as a continuation of colonial dispossession of Indigenous lands, knowledges, and lifeways rather than a novel and separate phenomenon, through Whyte's "Indigenous Climate Change Studies: Indigenizing Futures, Decolonizing the Anthropocene" (*English Language Notes*, 2017) and the broader corpus. The thread specifies what the Indigenous-sovereignty tradition articulates that the Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon does not: that the border is a colonial instrument, that displacement has a five-century history on the continent, and that the climate-displaced and the colonially-dispossessed are not separate analytical categories. The thread is conducted under the multi-eyed-seeing methodological discipline `VII.01` specifies; it draws on but does not duplicate `III.01`'s primary-articulator engagement.

### Thread F — Belonging from the borderlands

The thread engages the Chicana borderlands tradition at primary-articulator standing. It engages Gloria Anzaldúa's *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza* (1987) on the borderland as a lived condition rather than a line — the *mestiza* consciousness formed in the zone the border produces, belonging as plural, layered, and held by the person rather than conferred by the state, and the border itself as an open wound (*una herida abierta*). It engages Aviva Chomsky's *Undocumented: How Immigration Became Illegal* (2014) on the historical construction of the "illegal" category in the United States and on the lived experience of belonging without juridical status. The thread engages the sanctuary tradition lightly, where the religious-and-conscience thread bears — sanctuary as a cultivated practice of belonging that operates beneath and against the juridical-status architecture. The thread specifies belonging as a cultivation condition: something a polity grows the conditions for, that persons hold and practice, rather than a status an architecture assigns.

### Thread G — The Pacific Islander climate-displacement tradition and legal precedent

The thread engages the Pacific Islander climate-displacement tradition at primary-articulator standing. It engages the Pacific Climate Warriors documentation on the articulation "we are not drowning, we are fighting" — the refusal of the framing of Pacific Islanders as passive climate victims awaiting relocation, and the assertion of belonging-to-place, ancestral relation to specific islands, and the right not to be displaced as prior to any right of resettlement. It engages *Teitiota v. New Zealand* (UN Human Rights Committee, Communication No. 2728/2016, Views adopted 2020) as the legal precedent the tradition has forced into being — the first international determination addressing whether climate conditions can ground a non-refoulement claim, and its specification that climate displacement can in principle engage the right to life even though the particular claim did not succeed. The thread specifies what the Pacific tradition articulates: that the constitutional question is not only how a receiving polity absorbs the displaced but how the architecture honors belonging-to-place and the right not to be displaced in the first instance.

### Thread H — Belonging cultivated, not designed: the positive polity-formation question

The thread specifies the positive polity-formation question the diagnostic forces: how a polity forms and re-forms its membership under conditions of large-scale population movement. It engages Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri's *Multitude* (2004) and *Commonwealth* (2009) on the multitude as a form of political composition that is plural without being reducible to the people of the nation-state, and on the common as that which is produced and shared rather than enclosed. The thread reads the Hardt-Negri articulation critically — as a Western recovery of polity-formation framings the Indigenous, borderlands, and Pacific traditions of Threads E–G articulate more concretely — and specifies the cultivation framing's answer: belonging is cultivated, grown under conditions the architecture specifies, and re-formed across generations, rather than designed and assigned. The thread states the case against both design-imposition framings the document must avoid: the borders-as-engineered-fixture framing of Thread B, and its mirror image, a borderlessness imposed by design rather than cultivated.

### Thread I — The cultivation specification and the bridge to the decision-rights architecture

The closing thread provides the cultivation specification and the analytical bridge to Parts XII and XIII. It specifies what the constitutional architecture must enable for belonging to be cultivated rather than designed: belonging as a foundational right at biospheric scale, cross-referencing `XII.09` (foundational decision rights), where the right to have rights and the right to belong are specified at constitutional weight; public-check rights at ecosystem-and-habitat scale, cross-referencing `XII.10` (public-check decision rights), where the scale-matched checks on displacement-driving and border-infrastructure decisions operate; and the intergenerational-feedback conditions that connect climate displacement to `XII.04` (intergenerational rectification). The thread specifies what `XIII.01` (the decision-rights synthesis) is being asked to integrate from this diagnostic, and what `XIV.01` (the capstone) reads `II.09` as contributing — without performing the integration or the synthesis itself. The thread frames `II.09`'s diagnostic as the polity-formation question the cultivation protocol must answer: the compound disturbance of climate displacement is what the architecture cultivates the polity to absorb while keeping belonging intact.

## 10. What to Avoid

The following document-specific anti-patterns are the principal risks for `II.09` and must be guarded against at every stage.

**Primary-articulator absorption into the Euro-American borders-and-sovereignty canon.** The strongest risk for `II.09`: drafting the Indigenous-sovereignty tradition (Simpson, Whyte), the Chicana borderlands tradition (Anzaldúa), and the Pacific Islander climate-displacement tradition (the Pacific Climate Warriors) as case studies or illustrative supplements to the analytical apparatus of Brown, Mbembe, Sassen, and Hardt and Negri. Threads E, F, and G must stand at primary-articulator standing; the Euro-American canon is recent and partial recovery of the polity-formation question these traditions articulate from longer and more concrete experience. The document must not turn the Pacific articulation into "Indigenous resilience," the borderlands tradition into "lived-experience color" on a theoretical frame, or Simpson's refusal into an instance of a Western sovereignty concept.

**Treating climate displacement as a single phenomenon.** The planning document specifies a category of at least six phenomena with distinct disturbance dynamics. The risk is collapsing the category into "climate refugees" as an undifferentiated mass, which both reproduces the disinformation framing the document diagnoses and builds the downstream architecture against an artificially simple disturbance model. Thread A must hold the category open.

**Duplication of the Part II regime-by-regime diagnostics and of `II.08`'s interactions analysis.** `II.09` is integrative at the polity-formation level. It must not re-do the cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-informational, or political-economic regime diagnostics, and it must not re-derive `II.08`'s seven interaction pathways. It cites the upstream documents and specifies the one cross-regime disturbance category and the belonging question that category forces.

**Design-imposition slip-back in the mirror-image form.** The document argues against borders-as-engineered-fixture. The symmetrical risk is arguing *for* borderlessness as a design-imposition in the opposite direction — specifying the abolition of all boundary in advance of cultivation conditions. The cultivation framing specifies revisable, scale-matched, cultivated conditions of membership; it does not engineer either the wall or its absence. Thread B and Thread H must both hold this line.

**Duplication of the downstream decision-rights and synthesis work.** Thread I is the bridge to Parts XII and XIII. The risk is that Thread I drafts the foundational-rights specification, the public-check specification, or the cultivation-protocol synthesis itself, pre-empting `XII.09`, `XII.10`, and `XIII.01`. Thread I names what those documents are being asked to integrate; it does not perform the specification.

**Gestural treatment of the cognitive-informational bearing.** The planning document names climate-displacement disinformation cascades as a real bearing of the category on the cognitive-and-informational regime. The risk is naming "invasion narratives" once and moving on. Within Thread D the document must engage the mechanism — how displacement disinformation manufactures consent for necropolitical border infrastructure — while cross-referencing `II.06` for the disinformation mechanics rather than re-deriving them.

**Filler-density inflation.** The fifth-pass failure mode the sixth-pass discipline targets. `II.09`'s analytical terrain makes certain terms load-bearing and therefore high-frequency — see Section 11.
