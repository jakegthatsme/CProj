## 2. Load-Bearing Triad

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad: **biocultural governance** as constitutional ontology (the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a biocultural formation, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled, grounded in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights literatures); **eco-mimetic policy-making** as operational method (ecology, complex-adaptive-systems theory, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology concepts inspire how laws are passed, written, and tested); **disturbance-adapted implementation** as temporal orientation (intergenerational architecture cultivated to absorb a plural disturbance landscape comprising five named regimes — cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic — and their interactions). The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the principle's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery. "Grown, not built" is colloquial summary.

For `III.01`, the triad is the document's substantive subject matter rather than a framing applied to other content. The document articulates biocultural governance as constitutional ontology at substantive scholarly analytical depth; it operationalizes the first element of the triad and provides the ontological grounding the other two presuppose. The primary-articulator traditions whose work the document carries at primary-articulator standing are the Haudenosaunee Great Law tradition; the broader Indigenous-ecological tradition (Kimmerer, Yunkaporta, Wildcat, Whyte); the Daoist relational ontology; the Buddhist dependent-origination tradition; the African Ubuntu tradition; the Andean ayllu and ayni traditions; the Pacific Islander Talanoa and Vā traditions; the Maori Whakapapa tradition; and Black-radical ecological thought. The sixth-pass revision adds the arts-and-language-and-cultural-production thread, specifying cultural production at constitutional weight as a load-bearing element of the biocultural formation.

## 5. Required Threads

### Thread A — Biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights literatures as scholarly grounding

The thread grounds the ontology in the biocultural-diversity literature (Luisa Maffi, ed., *On Biocultural Diversity*, 2001; Fikret Berkes, *Sacred Ecology*, 1999; Darrell Posey, ed., *Cultural and Spiritual Values of Biodiversity*, 1999) and the biocultural-rights literature (Kabir Bavikatte, *Stewarding the Earth*, 2014; Daniel Robinson and Bavikatte on biocultural rights and the Nagoya Protocol; the ICCA Consortium and IIFB documentation), and specifies the analytical move from biocultural diversity (descriptive) and biocultural heritage (legal) to biocultural governance (constitutional).

### Thread B — The Haudenosaunee Great Law tradition at primary-articulator standing

The thread engages the Haudenosaunee Great Law (Kaianerekowa) tradition through Oren Lyons (Onondaga), John Mohawk (Seneca; *Utopian Legacies*, 2000), Doug George-Kanentiio (Mohawk; *Iroquois On Fire*, 2008), and the broader Haudenosaunee corpus, specifying the seven-generations principle, the clan-mother architecture, the wampum-belt record, and the Great Tree and Council Fire as cultivation-protocol metaphors with substantive analytical content. The Western recovery canon is positioned downstream of the Haudenosaunee articulation.

### Thread C — The broader Indigenous-ecological tradition at primary-articulator standing

The thread engages Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi; *Braiding Sweetgrass*, 2013; *Gathering Moss*, 2003) on the grammar of animacy and reciprocity; Tyson Yunkaporta (Apalech; *Sand Talk*, 2019) on Indigenous pattern-thinking; Daniel Wildcat (Yuchi/Muscogee; *Red Alert!*, 2009) on Indigenous knowledge of place; and Kyle Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi) on kinship time and climate colonialism, each in its own register.

### Thread D — Daoist relational ontology at primary-articulator standing

The thread engages the Daoist tradition (*Daodejing* and *Zhuangzi*; *wu wei* and *ziran*) through Roger Ames and David Hall's translations and *Thinking from the Han* (1998), Brook Ziporyn's *Zhuangzi* (2020), and James Miller's *China's Green Religion* (2017), as primary articulator of cultivation-not-design ontology, distinguished from the laissez-faire reading.

### Thread E — Buddhist dependent-origination tradition at primary-articulator standing

The thread engages the dependent-origination tradition (*pratītyasamutpāda*) through Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (Garfield's *Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way*, 1995; Siderits and Katsura, 2013), Thich Nhat Hanh on interbeing, Joanna Macy's *Mutual Causality in Buddhism and General Systems Theory* (1991), and David Loy's *The Great Awakening* (2003), specifying the polity as relational rather than substantive and the case against the Lockean possessive-individualist framing.

### Thread F — African Ubuntu tradition at primary-articulator standing

The thread engages the Ubuntu tradition through Mogobe Ramose's *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (1999), Drucilla Cornell and Karin van Marle's *Ubuntu and the Law* (2011), the South African Constitutional Court's *S v Makwanyane* (1995), Augustine Shutte's *Ubuntu* (2001), and Thaddeus Metz's *A Relational Moral Theory* (2022), engaging the constitutional-jurisprudential reception of Ubuntu as a case study and distinguishing its relational ontology from the dependent-origination and Daoist relational ontologies.

### Thread G — Andean ayllu and ayni traditions at primary-articulator standing

The thread engages the Andean ayllu and ayni traditions through Josef Estermann's *Andean Philosophy* (2006), Eduardo Gudynas on *buen vivir* and Rights of Nature, Marisol de la Cadena's *Earth Beings* (2015), Alberto Acosta's *Buen Vivir* (2013), and the Ecuadorian (2008) and Bolivian (2009) constitutional incorporation of Pacha Mama and *sumak kawsay*/*suma qamaña*, engaging the constitutional-incorporation case studies honestly, including the uneven implementation record.

### Thread H — Pacific Islander Talanoa and Vā, and Maori Whakapapa, traditions at primary-articulator standing

The thread engages the Pacific Talanoa and Vā traditions through Sitiveni Halapua, Albert Wendt, and Konai Helu Thaman; and the Maori Whakapapa tradition through Mason Durie's *Te Mana Te Kāwanatanga* (1998), Margaret Mutu (Ngāti Kahu) and Moana Jackson (Ngāti Kahungunu/Ngāti Porou) on *Matike Mai Aotearoa* (2016), the *Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017*, and Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies* (1999), reading *Matike Mai* and the Whanganui case as the closest existing approximations to biocultural-governance constitutional implementation in the Pacific.

### Thread I — Black-radical ecological thought at primary-articulator standing

The thread engages Black-radical ecological thought through Octavia Butler's *Parable of the Sower* (1993) and *Parable of the Talents* (1998), adrienne maree brown's *Emergent Strategy* (2017) reading Butler as cultivation-protocol specification, Robin D. G. Kelley's *Freedom Dreams* (2002), and the Black-agrarian tradition through Leah Penniman's *Farming While Black* (2018) and Monica White's *Freedom Farmers* (2018), as primary articulator of cultivation-under-disturbance.

### Thread J — Western recovery canon as recent partial recovery

The thread engages the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canon — Holling on panarchy, Gunderson and Holling's *Panarchy* (2002), Ostrom on polycentricity (*Governing the Commons*, 1990; *Understanding Institutional Diversity*, 2005), Benyus's *Biomimicry* (1997), Alexander's *The Nature of Order* (2002–2004), Mang and Reed on regenerative development, Pickett and White on disturbance ecology, Folke and Walker on resilience, John Todd on living machines, and Wendell Berry on agrarian cultivation — as recovery of framings the primary-articulator traditions had already specified, naming what each recovers, extends, and falls short of.

### Thread N — Arts, language, and cultural production as a load-bearing element of the biocultural formation (sixth-pass addition; drafted in document position after Thread J, before the apex Threads K–M)

The thread carries the sixth-pass arts-and-language-and-cultural-production thread, specifying cultural production at constitutional weight. The biocultural-governance ontology of `III.01` couples biological substrate and cultural plurality; the thread specifies that arts, language, and cultural production are not an ornament on the cultural side of that coupling but a load-bearing element of the biocultural formation — the means by which a culture articulates, transmits, revises, and continues itself across generations, and therefore a constitutional rather than a discretionary matter. It engages Raymond Williams's *Culture and Society* (1958) and *The Country and the City* (1973) on culture as a whole way of life and as ordinary rather than elite production; Stuart Hall's *Cultural Studies 1983* (2016) and the Birmingham-school corpus on cultural production as a site of meaning-making and contestation; the language-and-cultural-sovereignty literature on language as the carrier of a biocultural inheritance — the Kōhanga Reo and Kura Kaupapa Māori movements, the Hawaiian-language-revitalization documentation, and the broader Indigenous-language-revitalization corpus through Leanne Hinton's *How to Keep Your Language Alive* (2002), at primary-articulator standing and coordinated with the Pacific and Maori engagement of Thread H; the Black Arts Movement through Amiri Baraka; the Latinx-arts tradition through Gloria Anzaldúa's *Borderlands/La Frontera* (1987); and bell hooks's *Art on My Mind* (1995). The thread specifies how cultural production functions in the biocultural-governance ontology — as the activity through which biocultural diversity is actually lived and continued — and connects this specification to the analytical apex (Threads K–M): cultural production is one of the conditions a constitutional architecture under the cultivation framing must cultivate, and the project's refusal of synthesis-into-architecture is itself a commitment to maintaining the conditions under which plural cultural production continues. The thread coordinates with `II.02` and `VI.05`, the other arts/culture carriers, and houses comparison gestures in `XIV.01`.

### Thread K — Analytical case for biocultural governance as constitutional ontology

The thread specifies the analytical case at the level of constitutional ontology through three moves: the failure modes of design-imposition framings; the analytical limits of social-contract reasoning at scale (engaging Charles Mills's *The Racial Contract*, 1997, and Carole Pateman's *The Sexual Contract*, 1988); and the necessity of feedback and cultivation conditions in any system whose components are themselves living and adaptive. This is one of the document's three apex threads and is drafted at the document's deepest analytical depth.

### Thread L — The nested-scales reference frame as constitutional category

The thread specifies the nested-scales reference frame — the spatial scales (biosphere, ecozone, ecosystem, habitat, population, organism, organ system) and the temporal analogs — as constitutional category, with examples of decision rights matched to each scale, engaging the cross-scale-governance literature (Cash et al., 2006; Lebel et al., 2005; Berkes and Folke, 1998; the Ostroms on polycentricity), and distinguishing scale-matching from scale-reduction and scale-blindness. This is the second apex thread, drafted at the document's deepest analytical depth.

### Thread M — Renovation-versus-decolonial under the cultivation-rather-than-design-renovation framing

The thread engages the renovation-versus-decolonial question as a substantive analytical move, engaging the decolonial canon (Walter Mignolo's *The Darker Side of Western Modernity*, 2011; Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies*; Aníbal Quijano on coloniality of power; Boaventura de Sousa Santos's *Epistemologies of the South*, 2014) substantively, and arguing that cultivation honors the multi-traditional canon by maintaining conditions under which traditions continue to grow alongside one another. It engages the strongest version of the decolonial critique — that even cultivation-as-conditions can serve as cover for synthesis-as-appropriation — honestly. This is the third apex thread, drafted at the document's deepest analytical depth, in conversation with `VII.01` but as its own substantive work.
