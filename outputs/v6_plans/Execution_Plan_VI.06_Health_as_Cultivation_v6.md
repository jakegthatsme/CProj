## 2. Load-Bearing Triad

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad: **biocultural governance** as constitutional ontology (the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a biocultural formation, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled, grounded in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights literatures); **eco-mimetic policy-making** as operational method (ecology, complex-adaptive-systems theory, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology concepts inspire how laws are passed, written, and tested); **disturbance-adapted implementation** as temporal orientation (intergenerational architecture cultivated to absorb a plural disturbance landscape comprising five named regimes — cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic — and their interactions). The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the principle's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery. "Grown, not built" is usable as colloquial summary phrase.

For `VI.06`, the triad bears as follows. Biocultural governance is foregrounded: health is the paradigm case of a biocultural formation — bodies are cultivated within ecological, social, and economic conditions, not engineered as repairable mechanisms — and the document specifies health as cultivation terrain operating at the bodily and ecological scales of the nested-scales reference frame. Disturbance-adapted implementation bears because the biological-disturbance regime (`II.04`) acts on the body, and the document treats pandemic governance and environmental-health disturbance under the cultivation framing. Eco-mimetic policy-making bears on the closing specification of what health architecture the constitutional order must enable. The document foregrounds the biological-disturbance regime and treats its coupling with the physical-environmental and political-economic regimes. Its primary-articulator traditions — the Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition above all, with the structural-violence, social-determinants, health-justice, and disability-justice traditions alongside — stand at primary-articulator standing; the engineered-medical-system framing is the design-imposition tradition under diagnosis.

## 4. Substantive Task

`VI.06` specifies health as cultivation terrain operating at the bodily and ecological scales of the nested-scales reference frame, and articulates the cultivation case against the engineered-medical-system framing that treats bodies as repairable mechanisms. The analytical work has four phases.

First, the document specifies health as cultivation terrain — what it means to treat the body not as a machine to be repaired by medical intervention but as a biocultural formation cultivated within ecological, social, and economic conditions. This phase establishes the bodily-and-ecological cross-scale frame and distinguishes the cultivation framing from both the biomedical-repair model and a naive wellness individualism.

Second, the document conducts the engineered-medical-system diagnostic: it specifies the dominant framing — health as the output of a medical system that intervenes on bodies as repairable mechanisms — as a design-imposition framing, and specifies the iatrogenic and structural costs of that framing.

Third, the document engages the primary-articulator traditions in their own analytical registers: the structural-violence-and-health tradition; the social-determinants-of-health tradition; the health-as-membership and One Health framings; the Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition; the medical-anthropology tradition on the meaning of illness; the disability-justice critique of the medical model; and the global-health, health-justice, pandemic-governance, and environmental-health literatures.

Fourth, the document specifies what the constitutional architecture must enable for health to be cultivated rather than engineered, and bridges to the provisioning and foundational decision-rights architecture of Part XII.

`VI.06` assumes the constitutional-ontology and nested-scales reference frame of `III.01`, the biological-disturbance diagnostic of `II.04`, and the food-and-soil and built-environment analyses of `VI.02` and `VI.05` as health substrate. It does not duplicate those documents; it specifies the bodily substrate they bear on. It does not specify the bounded decision rights or the cultivation protocol — those are Part XII and Part XIII work the document points forward to.

## 5. Required Threads

`VI.06` is **not** a designated carrier for any of the five sixth-pass new threads. It operates under the fourteen cross-cutting threads, foregrounding biocultural governance, disturbance-adapted implementation, the nested-scale reference frame, and the multi-traditional canon at co-equal weight. The Required Threads below are the document's analytical content threads; each must be engaged as prose, at the depth its standing warrants.

### Thread A — Health as cultivation terrain at bodily and ecological scales

The thread establishes the document's frame: health as cultivation terrain operating at the bodily scale (the organism cultivated within its conditions) and the ecological scale (the body coupled to the ecosystems it inhabits and depends on). It specifies what is at stake in treating the body as a biocultural formation rather than as a repairable mechanism, and distinguishes the cultivation framing from a naive wellness individualism that would relocate responsibility for health onto the individual while leaving the conditions untouched.

### Thread B — The engineered-medical-system diagnostic

The thread specifies the dominant framing — health as the output of a medical system that intervenes on bodies as repairable mechanisms — as a design-imposition framing, and builds the cultivation case against it. It engages Ivan Illich's *Medical Nemesis* (1976) on iatrogenesis — clinical, social, and cultural — and on the way the medicalization of health expropriates the capacity of persons and communities to cultivate their own health. The thread specifies what survives of the medical system under the cultivation framing (the genuine goods of acute care, surgery, and crisis intervention) and what does not (the framing of the body as a mechanism, the expropriation of health into a service, the displacement of the conditions of health by intervention on its failures).

### Thread C — Structural violence and the social determinants of health

The thread engages the structural-violence-and-health tradition and the social-determinants-of-health tradition. It engages Paul Farmer's *Pathologies of Power* (2003) on structural violence — the way social arrangements put individuals and populations in harm's way, and the way the distribution of disease tracks the distribution of power — and on the demand that health be understood as a question of justice. It engages Margaret Whitehead's work on health inequities and the WHO social-determinants-of-health framework on the conditions — income, housing, work, education, environment — that determine health upstream of any medical encounter. The thread specifies what these traditions establish for the cultivation framing: that health is conditioned, that the conditions are unjustly distributed, and that a constitutional architecture that addresses only the medical system addresses only the downstream failure.

### Thread D — Health as membership and the One Health framework

The thread engages the health-as-membership framing and the One Health framework. It engages Wendell Berry's essay "Health Is Membership," in *Another Turn of the Crank* (1995), on health as wholeness and as membership in a community and a place — the argument that health cannot be abstracted from the health of the land, the community, and the relations a person lives within. It engages the One Health framework documentation on the coupling of human, animal, and ecosystem health — the recognition that these are not separable domains. The thread specifies how the membership framing and the One Health framework together establish health as an ecological-scale phenomenon, not only a bodily-scale one, and connects this to the biocultural-governance ontology.

### Thread E — Indigenous health sovereignty

The thread engages the Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition at primary-articulator standing — a load-bearing thread. It engages Lewis Mehl-Madrona (Lakota) on integrative Indigenous medicine and on narrative and relational approaches to healing; the Indigenous Health Adaptation to Climate Change framework on the coupling of Indigenous health with land, climate, and sovereignty; the *International Journal of Indigenous Health* corpus; and the Native American Health Center and Urban Indian Health Institute publications on Indigenous-controlled health provision and on the data sovereignty that health sovereignty requires. The thread specifies what the Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition articulates that the Euro-American canons do not: that health is inseparable from land, sovereignty, and the continuity of a people, that healing is relational and narrative rather than only biomedical, and that health sovereignty — Indigenous control over Indigenous health — is a constitutional question. The thread is conducted under the multi-eyed-seeing discipline `VII.01` specifies.

### Thread F — Medical anthropology and the meaning of illness

The thread engages the medical-anthropology tradition on illness as a meaningful and culturally constituted experience, not only a biological event. It engages Arthur Kleinman on illness narratives and on the distinction between disease (the biomedical entity) and illness (the lived experience); Byron Good on the cultural constitution of medical knowledge; and Margaret Lock on the way bodies and biologies are themselves shaped by local cultural and material worlds. The thread specifies what medical anthropology contributes to the cultivation framing: that the body and its illnesses are biocultural, that the meaning of illness is part of its reality, and that a health architecture that addresses only disease misses illness.

### Thread G — The disability-justice critique of the medical model

The thread engages the disability-justice critique of medical-model framings at primary-articulator standing. It engages Alison Kafer's *Feminist, Queer, Crip* (2013) on the political and relational model of disability, on crip futurity, and on the refusal of the framing of disability as a problem to be cured; and Eli Clare's *Brilliant Imperfection* (2017) on the ideology of cure and on the tension between wanting bodily change and refusing the demand that disabled bodies be eliminated. The thread specifies what the disability-justice tradition articulates that the cultivation framing must take up: that "health" must not become a norm against which disabled lives are measured and found wanting, that the cultivation of conditions is owed to disabled people on their own terms, and that the medical model's cure imperative is itself a design-imposition on bodies.

### Thread H — Global health, health justice, pandemic governance, and environmental health

The thread engages the global-health and health-justice literature, the pandemic-governance literature, and the environmental-health literature. It engages Solomon Benatar on global health ethics, Jonathan Mann on health and human rights, and the *Health and Human Rights* journal corpus on the rights framing of health; the pandemic-governance literature through Jonathan Quick and the WHO Pandemic Preparedness framework, engaged critically alongside the global-South critiques of pandemic governance and vaccine inequity; and the environmental-health literature through Rob Wallace's *Big Farms Make Big Flu* (2016) on the industrial food system as pandemic-generative architecture and Sandra Steingraber's *Living Downstream* (1997) on the environmental causes of cancer and on the body's exposure to its chemical environment. The thread specifies how these literatures establish health as a cross-scale and cross-regime phenomenon — bearing on `II.04` and on the food-system analysis of `VI.02` — without re-deriving the disturbance-regime substance.

### Thread I — The cultivation specification and the bridge to the decision-rights architecture

The closing thread specifies what the constitutional architecture must enable for health to be cultivated rather than engineered, and bridges to Part XII and Part XIII. It specifies the health-cultivation conditions the architecture must enable — the upstream conditions of health as a constitutional commitment, health sovereignty for Indigenous nations, the refusal of the cure imperative as a measure of citizenship — cross-referencing `XII.11` (provisioning decision rights) where the right to health provisioning is instantiated and `XII.09` (foundational decision rights) where the right not to be harmed at biospheric scale is specified. The thread specifies what `XIII.01` is being asked to integrate and what `XIV.01` reads `VI.06` as contributing, without performing the integration. It coordinates the bridge with `XII.05` on neurodivergent and disability cultivation.

## 10. What to Avoid

**Primary-articulator absorption into the Euro-American medical and public-health canons.** The strongest risk for `VI.06`: drafting the Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition as a complementary or alternative medicine supplementing a frame whose real apparatus is the social-determinants and structural-violence literatures. The Indigenous health-sovereignty tradition is a primary articulator of what health is and of the sovereignty health requires; it must not be folded into "cultural competence" or "traditional healing" as a category Western public health manages.

**Reintroducing the medical-norm against disabled lives.** Thread G is load-bearing precisely because "health as cultivation" can slide into a wellness norm that measures disabled lives against an ideal of the cured body. The document must hold the disability-justice line: the cultivation of conditions is owed to disabled people on their own terms, and "health" is not a citizenship test.

**Naive wellness individualism.** The cultivation framing must not be drafted as a relocation of responsibility for health onto the individual. The structural-violence and social-determinants threads (C) establish that health is conditioned and the conditions are unjustly distributed; the cultivation framing specifies the cultivation of conditions, not the moralization of personal choices.

**Design-imposition slip-back.** The document argues against the engineered-medical-system framing. The risk is specifying a replacement health architecture as an engineered system designed in advance. The cultivation framing specifies conditions; it does not design the system.

**Duplication of the disturbance-regime and Part VI peer documents.** `VI.06` must not re-derive `II.04`'s biological-disturbance diagnostic, `VI.02`'s food-and-soil analysis, or `VI.05`'s built-environment analysis. It cites them as health substrate.

**Duplication of the downstream decision-rights and synthesis work.** Thread I names what `XII.09`, `XII.11`, and `XIII.01` are being asked to integrate; it does not perform the rights specification or the synthesis.

**Filler-density inflation.** Match the depth of the Phase α exemplars and the substantively-drafted v5 exemplars; do not pad with gestural project-vocabulary. See Section 11.
