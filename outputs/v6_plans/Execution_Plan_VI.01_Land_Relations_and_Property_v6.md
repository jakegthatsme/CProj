## 2. Load-Bearing Triad

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad: **biocultural governance** as constitutional ontology (the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a biocultural formation, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled, grounded in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights literatures); **eco-mimetic policy-making** as operational method (ecology, complex-adaptive-systems theory, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology concepts inspire how laws are passed, written, and tested); **disturbance-adapted implementation** as temporal orientation (intergenerational architecture cultivated to absorb a plural disturbance landscape comprising five named regimes — cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic — and their interactions). The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the principle's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery. "Grown, not built" is usable as colloquial summary phrase.

For `VI.01`, the triad bears as follows. Biocultural governance is foregrounded: land relations are the paradigm case of a biocultural formation — biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled at the ecosystem, ecozonic, and biospheric scales — and the document specifies land not as alienable resource but as the terrain on which the polity's biocultural life is cultivated. Eco-mimetic policy-making bears on the closing specification of what land-relation architecture the constitutional order must enable. Disturbance-adapted implementation bears because land relations are cultivated across intergenerational horizons and must absorb the disturbance landscape without reverting to the property-as-commodity framing. The document foregrounds no single disturbance regime but treats land as the substrate all five regimes act upon. Its primary-articulator traditions — the Indigenous land-relations tradition above all, with the commons tradition and the agrarian-and-bioregional traditions alongside — stand at primary-articulator standing; the Euro-American property-theory canon is engaged as the tradition whose design-imposition framing the document diagnoses and as recent partial recovery where it gestures past that framing.

## 4. Substantive Task

`VI.01` specifies land relations as cultivation terrain operating across the biospheric, ecozonic, and ecosystem scales of the nested-scales reference frame, and articulates the cultivation case against the property-as-engineered-bundle-of-rights framing that treats land as alienable, commodified resource. The analytical work has four phases.

First, the document specifies land relations as cultivation terrain — what it means to treat land as the substrate of a biocultural formation cultivated across nested scales rather than as a stock of resource to be allocated. This phase establishes the cross-scale frame: land relations at the ecosystem scale (the specific watershed, forest, prairie, or coastline), at the ecozonic scale (the biome and bioregion), and at the biospheric scale (land as the planetary substrate).

Second, the document conducts the property-as-engineered-bundle-of-rights diagnostic: it specifies the dominant Euro-American property concept — property as a bundle of separable, alienable rights, abstracted from the particular land and from relation to it — as a design-imposition framing, and specifies what that framing does to land relations.

Third, the document engages the primary-articulator traditions in their own analytical registers: the Indigenous land-relations tradition on land as relation, gift, kin, and grounded normativity; the white-possessive critique on the colonial logic of possession; the Land Back articulation on the contemporary constitutional question; the commons tradition on land held and governed in common; the agrarian-and-bioregional traditions on the land ethic and reinhabitation; and the Black agrarian tradition on land, freedom, and repair.

Fourth, the document specifies what the constitutional architecture must enable for land relations to be cultivated rather than engineered, and bridges to the foundational-rights and provisioning decision-rights architecture of Part XII.

`VI.01` assumes the constitutional-ontology and nested-scales reference frame of `III.01` and the foundational-normative inheritance of `I.01`, in which land-and-resource sovereignty was specified at Indigenous primary-articulator standing. It does not duplicate `VI.02`'s food-and-soil analysis, `VI.04`'s materials analysis, or `VI.05`'s built-environment analysis; it specifies the land-relation substrate those documents draw on. It does not specify the bounded decision rights or the cultivation protocol — those are Part XII and Part XIII work the document points forward to.

## 5. Required Threads

`VI.01` is **not** a designated carrier for any of the five sixth-pass new threads. It operates under the fourteen cross-cutting threads, foregrounding biocultural governance, the nested-scale reference frame, intergenerational temporal orientation, and the multi-traditional canon at co-equal weight. The Required Threads below are the document's analytical content threads; each must be engaged as prose, at the depth its standing warrants.

### Thread A — Land relations as cultivation terrain across nested scales

The thread establishes the document's frame: land relations as cultivation terrain operating at the ecosystem, ecozonic, and biospheric scales of the nested-scales reference frame. It specifies what is at stake in treating land as the substrate of a biocultural formation — biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled in a particular place — rather than as a resource stock. The thread draws the nested-scales frame from `III.01` and does not re-derive it; it specifies how land relations differ in kind at each scale and why a constitutional architecture must address all three.

### Thread B — The property-as-engineered-bundle-of-rights diagnostic

The thread specifies the dominant Euro-American property concept as a design-imposition framing and builds the cultivation case against it. It engages C. B. Macpherson's *The Political Theory of Possessive Individualism* (1962) on the genealogy of property as possession by an individual abstracted from social and ecological relation; Margaret Jane Radin's *Reinterpreting Property* (1993) on the distinction between fungible and personal property and on property as bound up with personhood; and Carol Rose's *Property and Persuasion* (1994) on property as a system of persuasion, narrative, and convention rather than a natural fact. The thread specifies the bundle-of-rights metaphor — property as a bundle of separable, alienable sticks — as the design-imposition framing that abstracts land from relation and renders it commodifiable, and specifies what survives of the property concept under the cultivation framing (stewardship obligations, scale-matched use rights, security of relation to place) and what does not (the abstraction from particular land, the presumption of alienability as definitional, the bundle as engineered fixture).

### Thread C — Indigenous land-relations as primary articulation

The thread engages the Indigenous land-relations tradition at primary-articulator standing — the document's central and load-bearing thread. It engages Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi), *Braiding Sweetgrass* (2013), on land as relation, as gift, and as a giver to whom reciprocity is owed; Vine Deloria Jr. (Standing Rock Sioux), *God Is Red* (1973), on land as the ground of religion and on the spatial rather than temporal organization of Indigenous relation to place; Glen Coulthard (Yellowknives Dene), *Red Skin, White Masks* (2014), on grounded normativity — the ethical framework that emerges from sustained relationship to land — and on the critique of the politics of recognition; Leanne Betasamosake Simpson (Michi Saagiig Nishnaabeg), *As We Have Always Done* (2017), on land as pedagogy and on Indigenous resurgence; and Winona LaDuke (Anishinaabe), *All Our Relations* (1999) and *Recovering the Sacred* (2005), on Indigenous land-based economies and the recovery of sacred sites and relations. The thread specifies what this tradition articulates that the Euro-American property canon does not: that land is kin and relative rather than object, that relation to land is constitutive of personhood and law, and that these are not metaphors but the literal terms of a different and prior land-relation tradition. The thread is conducted under the multi-eyed-seeing discipline `VII.01` specifies and draws on but does not duplicate `III.01`'s primary-articulator engagement.

### Thread D — The white-possessive logic and the colonial transformation of land relation

The thread engages Aileen Moreton-Robinson (Goenpul), *The White Possessive* (2015), on the possessive logic of white sovereignty — the way settler-colonial law constitutes land as ownable and the settler subject as the proper owner — and on the racialization of the property relation itself. It engages William Cronon's *Changes in the Land* (1983) on the historical transformation of land relations in colonial New England, from Indigenous use-and-relation regimes to the commodity-and-boundary regime of the colonists. The thread specifies the colonial transformation of land relation as the historical event that installed the property-as-engineered-bundle framing, and specifies that the framing is therefore not natural or universal but a particular and recent imposition with a datable history.

### Thread E — Land Back as the contemporary constitutional question

The thread engages the Land Back articulation as a present constitutional question, not a historical grievance. It engages the *Yellowhead Institute Land Back Red Paper* (2019) on Land Back as the return of decision-making jurisdiction over land to Indigenous nations, and on the distinction between consultation and consent; and Nick Estes (Lower Brule Sioux), *Our History Is the Future* (2019), on the long history of Indigenous resistance and on the Standing Rock struggle as a continuation of that history. The thread specifies what Land Back asks of a constitutional architecture and why the cultivation framing — land relations grown under conditions the architecture specifies — must take the Land Back question as a live constitutional commitment rather than a settled historical matter.

### Thread F — The commons tradition

The thread engages the commons tradition on land held and governed in common. It engages Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) on the empirical refutation of the tragedy-of-the-commons thesis and on the design principles of durable common-pool-resource institutions; Peter Linebaugh's *The Magna Carta Manifesto* (2008) on the commons-and-commoning tradition and on the Charter of the Forest as a suppressed history of common right; and James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (1998) on land-as-legibility — the way the modern state renders land legible for taxation and control by flattening the complex customary relations that the commons depended on. The thread specifies what the commons tradition recovers for the cultivation framing: land governed at scale-matched institutions, common right as a real and durable alternative to both private property and state ownership, and the warning Scott supplies against the legibility imposition.

### Thread G — The agrarian and bioregional traditions

The thread engages the agrarian and bioregional traditions on the ethic of relation to land. It engages Aldo Leopold's *A Sand County Almanac* (1949) on the land ethic — the extension of the community to include soils, waters, plants, and animals; Wendell Berry's *The Unsettling of America* (1977) and *The Gift of Good Land* (1981) on agrarianism, on the culture of husbandry, and on the membership of the human in the land community; Peter Berg's *Reinhabiting a Separate Country* (1978) and Kirkpatrick Sale's *Dwellers in the Land* (1985) on bioregionalism and reinhabitation — the practice of living in place at the scale of the bioregion; and Henry George's *Progress and Poverty* (1879) on land-value taxation as a response to the capture of land rent. The thread specifies what these traditions contribute to the cultivation framing and engages them critically where the agrarian tradition has carried a romanticism or an exclusionary politics that the document must not reproduce.

### Thread H — The Black agrarian tradition

The thread engages the Black agrarian tradition at primary-articulator standing. It engages Monica White's *Freedom Farmers* (2018) on Black agricultural cooperatives and on farming as a practice of resistance, self-determination, and freedom from the Reconstruction era forward; and Leah Penniman's *Farming While Black* (2018) on the recovery of African and African-diasporic agrarian knowledge and on land as a site of healing from the trauma of slavery and sharecropping. The thread specifies what the Black agrarian tradition articulates that neither the white agrarian tradition nor the Indigenous land-relations tradition does: land relation under the specific history of enslavement, dispossession, and the long Black struggle for land as the material basis of freedom.

### Thread I — The cultivation specification and the bridge to the decision-rights architecture

The closing thread specifies what the constitutional architecture must enable for land relations to be cultivated rather than engineered, and bridges to Part XII and Part XIII. It specifies the land-relation conditions the architecture must cultivate — scale-matched land jurisdiction, the Land Back question as a live commitment, common right as a recognized form of land holding, stewardship obligation as constitutive of any land relation — cross-referencing `XII.09` (foundational decision rights) where land-and-resource sovereignty is specified at constitutional weight and `XII.11` (provisioning decision rights) where land as provisioning substrate is instantiated. The thread specifies what `XIII.01` is being asked to integrate and what `XIV.01` reads `VI.01` as contributing, without performing the integration. It frames `VI.01` as the foundational-substrate document of Part VI.

## 10. What to Avoid

**Primary-articulator absorption into the Euro-American property canon.** The strongest risk for `VI.01`: drafting Kimmerer, Deloria, Coulthard, Leanne Simpson, and LaDuke as a values-supplement or a spiritual gloss on a property-theory frame supplied by Macpherson, Radin, and Rose. The Indigenous land-relations tradition is the document's primary articulator of what land is; the property-theory canon is the design-imposition tradition under diagnosis. The document must not render "land as relation" as a metaphor decorating an analysis whose real apparatus is Western property theory.

**Synthesis-into-architecture across the land traditions.** The Indigenous land-relations tradition, the commons tradition, the agrarian-and-bioregional traditions, and the Black agrarian tradition are distinct, and they are in real tension at points — the commons tradition's English genealogy and the Indigenous tradition's refusal of the commons-versus-private frame are not the same analysis. The document must not synthesize them into a single architectural register; it engages each in its own register and lets the tensions stand.

**Romanticism in the agrarian thread.** The white agrarian tradition (Leopold, Berry, the bioregionalists) has at points carried a romanticism, a settler innocence, or an exclusionary politics of place. Thread G must engage the tradition critically and must not let the agrarian register soften the Land Back question of Thread E or the dispossession history of Threads D and H.

**Treating Land Back as historical grievance rather than live constitutional question.** Thread E specifies Land Back as a present commitment a constitutional architecture must take up. The risk is drafting it as a past wrong to be acknowledged rather than a jurisdictional question to be answered.

**Design-imposition slip-back in the mirror-image form.** The document argues against property-as-engineered-bundle. The symmetrical risk is engineering an abolition of property in advance of cultivation conditions. The cultivation framing specifies scale-matched, revisable, stewardship-bound land-relation conditions; it does not design either absolute property or its abolition.

**Duplication of the Part VI peer documents.** `VI.01` is the foundational substrate. It must not perform `VI.02`'s food-and-soil analysis, `VI.04`'s materials analysis, or `VI.05`'s built-environment analysis. It specifies the land-relation substrate and cites the peers forward.

**Duplication of the downstream decision-rights and synthesis work.** Thread I names what `XII.09`, `XII.11`, and `XIII.01` are being asked to integrate; it does not perform the rights specification or the synthesis.

**Filler-density inflation.** Match the depth of the Phase α exemplars and the substantively-drafted v5 exemplars; do not pad with gestural project-vocabulary. See Section 11.
