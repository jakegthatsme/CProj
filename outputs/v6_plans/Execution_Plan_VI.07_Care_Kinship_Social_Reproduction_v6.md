## 2. Load-Bearing Triad

The sixth-pass project rests on a load-bearing triad: **biocultural governance** as constitutional ontology (the polity is a complex adaptive system cultivated across nested spatial and temporal scales as a biocultural formation, with biological substrate and cultural plurality coupled, grounded in the biocultural-diversity and biocultural-rights literatures); **eco-mimetic policy-making** as operational method (ecology, complex-adaptive-systems theory, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology concepts inspire how laws are passed, written, and tested); **disturbance-adapted implementation** as temporal orientation (intergenerational architecture cultivated to absorb a plural disturbance landscape comprising five named regimes — cultural, biological, physical-environmental, cognitive-and-informational, political-economic — and their interactions). The Indigenous and Eastern traditions are the principle's primary articulators; the Western complex-adaptive-systems, ecology, biomimicry, and disturbance-ecology canons are recent and partial recovery. "Grown, not built" is usable as colloquial summary phrase.

For `VI.07`, the triad bears as follows. Biocultural governance is foregrounded: care work, kinship, and social reproduction are the relational substrate of the biocultural formation — the work and the relations through which the polity is reproduced across generations — and the document specifies them as cultivation terrain operating at the household, kin-network, and community scales of the nested-scales reference frame. Disturbance-adapted implementation bears because social reproduction is the intergenerational work the architecture depends on and because the contemporary crisis of care is itself a disturbance the architecture must absorb. Eco-mimetic policy-making bears on the closing specification of what care architecture the constitutional order must enable. The document foregrounds no single disturbance regime but treats the political-economic regime where care is marketized and the cultural regime where care is gendered and racialized. Its primary-articulator traditions — the Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition, the feminist political theory of care, the Black-feminist and women-of-color care tradition, the queer-kinship tradition, and the disability-justice critique of care-as-burden — stand at primary-articulator standing.

## 4. Substantive Task

`VI.07` specifies care work, kinship architecture, and social reproduction as cultivation terrain operating at the household, kin-network, and community scales of the nested-scales reference frame, and articulates the cultivation case against the marketization-of-care framing that treats care as a commodifiable service. The analytical work has four phases.

First, the document specifies care, kinship, and social reproduction as cultivation terrain — the work and the relations through which the polity reproduces itself across generations, located at the household, kin-network, and community scales. This phase establishes that care is constitutional terrain, not a private matter the constitutional architecture may pass over.

Second, the document conducts the marketization-of-care diagnostic: it specifies the dominant framing — care as a service to be commodified, outsourced, and priced — as a design-imposition framing, and specifies the contemporary crisis of care that framing has produced.

Third, the document engages the primary-articulator traditions in their own analytical registers: the feminist political theory of care; social-reproduction theory; the Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition; the Black-feminist and women-of-color care tradition; the queer-kinship tradition; and the disability-justice critique of care-as-burden.

Fourth, the document specifies what the constitutional architecture must enable for care and kinship to be cultivated rather than marketized, and bridges to the provisioning and cognitive-intergenerational decision-rights architecture of Part XII.

`VI.07` assumes the constitutional-ontology and nested-scales reference frame of `III.01`, in which kinship is engaged as a biocultural formation. It does not duplicate `XII.05`'s neurodivergent-polis analysis; it engages care-receiving and care-giving disability politics and cross-references `XII.05`. It does not specify the bounded decision rights or the cultivation protocol — those are Part XII and Part XIII work the document points forward to.

## 5. Required Threads

`VI.07` is **not** a designated carrier for any of the five sixth-pass new threads; the sexuality-and-gender thread is carried through `II.02`, `XII.11`, `XII.12`, and `XII.05`, not here, though `VI.07`'s queer-kinship thread coordinates with it. `VI.07` operates under the fourteen cross-cutting threads, foregrounding biocultural governance, intergenerational temporal orientation, the nested-scale reference frame, and the multi-traditional canon at co-equal weight. The Required Threads below are the document's analytical content threads; each must be engaged as prose, at the depth its standing warrants.

### Thread A — Care, kinship, and social reproduction as cultivation terrain

The thread establishes the document's frame: care work, kinship, and social reproduction as cultivation terrain operating at the household, kin-network, and community scales. It specifies that the reproduction of the polity across generations — the feeding, raising, tending, and sustaining of persons — is constitutional terrain, and that liberal political theory's treatment of this work as a private and pre-political matter is itself the omission the sixth pass corrects.

### Thread B — The marketization-of-care diagnostic

The thread specifies the marketization-of-care framing — care as a commodifiable, outsourceable, priceable service — as a design-imposition framing, and builds the cultivation case against it. The thread specifies the contemporary crisis of care: the simultaneous demand that care work be done and the refusal to value or resource it, the offloading of care onto racialized and migrant labor, and the strain the marketization framing places on the relations care depends on. The thread specifies what survives of paid and organized care under the cultivation framing (the genuine goods of resourced, skilled, supported care work) and what does not (the framing of care as a service abstracted from relation, the commodity logic, the devaluation that the marketization framing both produces and conceals).

### Thread C — The feminist political theory of care

The thread engages the feminist political theory of care at primary-articulator standing. It engages Joan Tronto's *Moral Boundaries* (1993) on care as a political and not merely private category and *Caring Democracy* (2013) on the redistribution of caring responsibilities as a question of democratic justice; Virginia Held's *The Ethics of Care* (2006) on care as a moral framework rivaling and correcting the justice tradition; Eva Feder Kittay's *Love's Labor* (1999) on dependency, on the dependency worker, and on the claim that the liberal fiction of the independent citizen erases the universal fact of human dependency; and Sara Ruddick's *Maternal Thinking* (1989) on the distinctive rationality of caring work. The thread specifies what the feminist care tradition establishes for the cultivation framing: that dependency is universal, that care is political, and that an architecture built on the fiction of the independent citizen has already failed the relational substrate.

### Thread D — Social-reproduction theory

The thread engages the social-reproduction-theory tradition. It engages Silvia Federici's *Caliban and the Witch* (2004) on the historical production of the gendered division of labor and on the enclosure of women's reproductive work, and *Wages Against Housework* (1975) on the demand that reproductive labor be recognized as labor; Nancy Fraser's *Fortunes of Feminism* (2013) and her analysis of the crisis of care in *The Old Is Dying and the New Cannot Be Born* (2019) on the contradiction between capitalism's dependence on social reproduction and its refusal to sustain it; Evelyn Nakano Glenn's *Forced to Care* (2010) on the racialized history of coerced care labor in the United States; and Arlie Hochschild's *The Second Shift* (1989) and *The Time Bind* (1997) on the unequal distribution of care work within households and the colonization of family time by waged work. The thread specifies how social-reproduction theory establishes care as the substrate the political economy depends on and systematically refuses to sustain.

### Thread E — Indigenous kinship as constitutive

The thread engages the Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition at primary-articulator standing — a load-bearing thread. It engages Kim TallBear (Sisseton Wahpeton Oyate), *Native American DNA* (2013) and "Making Love and Relations Beyond Settler Sex and Family" (2018), on kinship as a made and extended relation, on the critique of settler sexuality and the nuclear family, and on relations beyond the biological and the marital; Audra Simpson (Mohawk), *Mohawk Interruptus* (2014), on kinship and political membership and on the way Haudenosaunee kinship constitutes nationhood; and Vine Deloria Jr. (Standing Rock Sioux), *God Is Red* (1973) and *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969), on kinship as the organizing principle of Indigenous social and political life. The thread specifies what the Indigenous kinship tradition articulates that the feminist care tradition does not: that kinship is constitutive of the polity itself, that it extends beyond the human, and that it is not a private domain to be valued but the structure of political life. The thread is conducted under the multi-eyed-seeing discipline `VII.01` specifies.

### Thread F — Black-feminist and women-of-color care

The thread engages the Black-feminist and women-of-color care tradition at primary-articulator standing. It engages Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) on othermothering, on community-based child-care networks, and on the intergenerational caring work of Black women; Carol Stack's *All Our Kin* (1974) on the survival networks of kin and fictive kin in a poor Black community; Audre Lorde's *A Burst of Light* (1988) on self-care as self-preservation and as political warfare under conditions that grind Black women down; and Gloria Anzaldúa's *Borderlands/La Frontera* (1987) on the caring labor and the kin relations of the borderland. The thread specifies what this tradition articulates: care under the specific conditions of racialized poverty and racialized care-extraction, and the kin networks that survive and resist those conditions.

### Thread G — Queer kinship

The thread engages the queer-kinship tradition. It engages Kath Weston's *Families We Choose* (1991) on chosen family and on the gay and lesbian reconstruction of kinship outside the biological and the legal; and Judith Butler's *Antigone's Claim* (2000) on the limits of the normative kinship the state recognizes and on the kinship that falls outside it. The thread specifies what the queer-kinship tradition establishes for the cultivation framing: that kinship is made and chosen, that the state's recognition of kinship is partial and exclusionary, and that the cultivation of care relations cannot be tied to one normative family form. The thread coordinates with the sixth-pass sexuality-and-gender thread carried in `XII.11` and `XII.12` without duplicating it.

### Thread H — The disability-justice critique of care-as-burden, and elder care

The thread engages the disability-justice critique of care-as-burden at primary-articulator standing. It engages the disability-justice care literature through Mia Mingus on access intimacy and on care webs, Patty Berne and the Sins Invalid corpus on collective access and on disability-justice principles, and the broader disability-justice articulation that refuses the framing of disabled people as burdens and reframes care as mutual, reciprocal, and collective. It engages The Care Collective's *The Care Manifesto* (2020) on care as a politics of interdependence and on universal care. It engages Margaret Cruikshank's *Learning to Be Old* (2003) on aging and elder care and on the cultural devaluation of old age. The thread specifies what the disability-justice tradition articulates that the feminist care tradition has at points missed: that care must be drafted with the care-receiver as agent and not only object, that interdependence rather than dependency is the frame, and that care webs rather than dyadic care relations are the cultivation unit.

### Thread I — The cultivation specification and the bridge to the decision-rights architecture

The closing thread specifies what the constitutional architecture must enable for care and kinship to be cultivated rather than marketized, and bridges to Part XII and Part XIII. It specifies the care-cultivation conditions the architecture must enable — care work resourced and valued as constitutional terrain, kinship recognized in plural forms, care webs supported at household and community scales, the universal fact of dependency built into the architecture rather than erased by the independent-citizen fiction — cross-referencing `XII.11` (provisioning decision rights) where care provisioning is instantiated and `XII.12` (cognitive-and-intergenerational decision rights) where intergenerational social reproduction operates. The thread specifies what `XIII.01` is being asked to integrate and what `XIV.01` reads `VI.07` as contributing, without performing the integration. It coordinates the bridge with `XII.04` (intergenerational rectification) and `XII.05` (the neurodivergent polis).

## 10. What to Avoid

**Synthesis-into-architecture across the care and kinship traditions.** The strongest risk for `VI.07`: collapsing the feminist political theory of care, social-reproduction theory, the Indigenous kinship-as-constitutive tradition, the Black-feminist care tradition, the queer-kinship tradition, and the disability-justice critique into a single "ethics of care" architectural register. These traditions are distinct and at points in tension — the Indigenous tradition's claim that kinship constitutes the polity is not the feminist tradition's claim that care has been wrongly privatized, and the disability-justice critique corrects the feminist tradition's dependency framing. The document engages each in its own register and lets the tensions stand.

**Primary-articulator absorption.** The Indigenous kinship tradition (TallBear, Simpson, Deloria) must not be drafted as an illustration of, or a supplement to, the feminist political theory of care. Kinship-as-constitutive is a primary articulation of what a polity is; it is not "Indigenous family values" appended to a Western care frame.

**Reproducing the dependency framing the disability-justice tradition corrects.** Thread H is load-bearing because the feminist care tradition has at points framed the care-receiver as a dependent object of care. The document must hold the disability-justice line: interdependence, not dependency; care webs, not dyads; the care-receiver as agent.

**Treating care as a private matter the architecture values rather than as constitutional terrain.** The document must not draft care as a private good the constitutional order should respect or subsidize. The thesis is that care, kinship, and social reproduction are constitutional terrain — the relational substrate of the biocultural formation — and the marketization framing is the design-imposition under diagnosis.

**Tying the cultivation of care to one normative family form.** Thread G is load-bearing precisely so that the closing specification does not quietly assume the nuclear family. The architecture cultivates care relations in plural kinship forms.

**Design-imposition slip-back.** The document argues against the marketization-of-care framing. The risk is engineering a replacement care system designed in advance. The cultivation framing specifies conditions; it does not design the system.

**Duplication of the downstream decision-rights and synthesis work.** Thread I names what `XII.11`, `XII.12`, and `XIII.01` are being asked to integrate; it does not perform the rights specification or the synthesis.

**Filler-density inflation.** Match the depth of the Phase α exemplars and the substantively-drafted v5 exemplars; do not pad with gestural project-vocabulary. See Section 11.
